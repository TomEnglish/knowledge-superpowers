# AI Writing Slop Patterns

A catalog of the verbal tics, structural habits, and rhetorical dodges that mark text as AI-generated. Use this as a checklist during the synthesis slop pass. The goal isn't to hide that an AI wrote the text — it's to produce writing that's actually good. These patterns make writing worse regardless of who or what generated them.

## 1. Hedging and Qualification

Unnecessary softening that adds words without adding meaning.

**Patterns to catch:**
- "It's worth noting that..." → State the thing. If it's worth noting, the reader will note it.
- "It's important to consider..." → Consider it, then. Don't announce that you're going to consider it.
- "One could argue that..." → Who? Argue what? If the argument has merit, state it and attribute it.
- "There are many factors to consider, including..." → Name them. "Many factors" is a placeholder for not having done the work.
- "It should be noted that..." → Note it. The meta-commentary adds nothing.
- "While it's difficult to say definitively..." → Say what you can say. Qualify the specific uncertainty, not the entire endeavor.
- "Research suggests that..." (when used as a weasel phrase) → Which research? By whom? This phrasing is fine when followed by a specific citation; it's slop when used to gesture vaguely at authority.

**The fix:** Say the thing directly. If qualification is genuinely needed, make it specific: "The evidence for X is based on three small studies (N<100 each) with no replication attempts" is useful qualification. "It's important to note that the evidence is limited" is not.

## 2. False Balance and Diplomatic Dodging

Presenting manufactured symmetry between positions that aren't actually symmetric.

**Patterns to catch:**
- "Some experts say X while others say Y" → How many on each side? What's their evidence? Are these equivalent positions or is one the overwhelming consensus?
- "There are arguments on both sides" → What are they? Are they equally strong? If not, say so.
- "The truth probably lies somewhere in the middle" → Sometimes it does. Sometimes it doesn't. This is a thought-terminating cliché, not an analysis.
- "More research is needed" → This is almost always true and almost always uninformative. Instead: "The specific gap is X, which matters because Y."
- "This is a matter of ongoing debate" → Among whom? At what level? "Ongoing debate" between two fringe researchers is not the same as "ongoing debate" within the mainstream of a field.

**The fix:** Asymmetric evidence gets asymmetric treatment. If 95% of the evidence points one way and 5% points the other, present it as such. You can still note the minority view — but note it as a minority view, not as "the other side."

## 3. Hollow Transitions and Filler

Words and phrases that exist only to connect sentences, adding no information.

**Patterns to catch:**
- "Furthermore..." "Moreover..." "Additionally..." "In addition..." → If the next sentence is related, the relationship should be self-evident from the content. If it's not, restructure.
- "In conclusion..." "To summarize..." "In summary..." → If it's the last section, we know. These are training-data artifacts from essay-writing instructions.
- "That being said..." "Having said that..." → Usually precedes a contradiction of what was just said. Either commit to the contradiction or restructure.
- "It is also important to..." → See Hedging, above.
- "Moving on to..." "Turning our attention to..." "Let us now consider..." → Just move on. The reader will follow.
- "As mentioned earlier..." "As discussed above..." → If you need to reference an earlier point, reference it specifically. These phrases add nothing.

**The fix:** Delete the transition. Read the two sentences without it. If they flow, leave it deleted. If they don't, the problem is structural, not transitional.

## 4. Performative Nuance

Announcing complexity instead of engaging with it.

**Patterns to catch:**
- "This is a complex/nuanced topic that defies simple answers" → Then give the complex answer. Don't just announce that it's complex.
- "There are no easy solutions" → Name the hard ones.
- "The picture is more complicated than it first appears" → Show the complication. Don't just point at it.
- "Reasonable people can disagree" → On what evidence? What are the actual points of disagreement?
- "Context matters" → What context? How does it matter?
- "It depends" → On what? This is where the useful information lives.

**The fix:** Replace the announcement with the substance. "This topic is nuanced" → [the actual nuance]. Every time.

## 5. Sycophancy and Deference

Flattering the reader or the person who asked the question.

**Patterns to catch:**
- "Great question!" "That's a really interesting question" "You raise an excellent point"
- "As you rightly point out..." "Building on your insightful observation..."
- "Your intuition is correct that..."
- Any sentence that compliments the reader rather than advancing the argument

**The fix:** Delete entirely. These sentences carry zero information. A good report respects the reader by giving them substance, not compliments.

## 6. Structural Slop

Organizational habits that weaken the writing.

**Patterns to catch:**
- **Excessive bullet lists**: More than 2 lists in a report body means the writer is enumerating instead of arguing. Lists are for reference material, not for making a case.
- **Too many headers**: If every paragraph has its own header, the structure is too granular. Headers should mark major sections, not individual thoughts.
- **Numbered lists for non-sequential content**: Using 1, 2, 3 when the items have no order implies a priority that doesn't exist. Use prose or unordered lists instead.
- **Bold-everything**: If every other sentence has bold phrases, nothing is emphasized. Reserve bold for genuinely important terms on first use.
- **The five-paragraph essay**: Introduction that says what you'll say, body that says it, conclusion that says what you said. This structure exists for high school essays. Research reports should just say the thing.

**The fix:** Convert lists to prose where the content is making an argument. Use headers only for major structural divisions. Remove bold from anything that isn't a key term or critical finding.

## 7. Vocabulary Tells

Specific words and phrases that are disproportionately common in AI output.

**Words to watch:**
- "Delve" — almost never used by humans, extremely common in AI text
- "Landscape" (as metaphor) — "the research landscape", "the competitive landscape"
- "Realm" — "in the realm of..."
- "Multifaceted" — almost always replaceable with a specific description
- "Pivotal" — overused; usually "important" is sufficient
- "Crucial" / "Critical" — used so frequently they've lost all emphasis
- "Robust" — legitimate in technical contexts, slop as a general intensifier
- "Leverage" (as verb) — corporate speak that rarely improves on "use"
- "Navigate" (metaphorical) — "navigate the challenges of..."
- "Tapestry" — "the rich tapestry of..."
- "Underscore" — "this underscores the importance of..."
- "Foster" — "foster collaboration/innovation/growth"
- "Shed light on" — just say "explain" or "reveal"
- "Game-changer" — never
- "Double-edged sword" — find a less tired metaphor or just describe the tradeoff

**The fix:** Replace with specific, plain language. If the replacement sounds less impressive, that's fine — impressive-sounding vagueness is worse than plain clarity.

## 8. Epistemic Cowardice

Avoiding clear statements of what the evidence shows because stating it plainly feels risky.

**Patterns to catch:**
- Burying the finding in qualifications: "While more research is certainly needed, and acknowledging the limitations of current methodologies, there is emerging evidence that tentatively suggests..." → just state the finding and note the evidence quality separately
- Refusing to state a conclusion: Presenting all the evidence and then saying "readers can draw their own conclusions" — if you've done the research, have the courage to state what it shows
- Treating all uncertainty as equal: "We can't be certain about X" applied equally to things that are 90% certain and 50% certain
- Passive voice to avoid attribution: "It has been argued that..." → by whom?
- Strategic vagueness: Using broad terms when specific ones are available, to avoid committing to a claim

**The fix:** State findings directly. Attach specific confidence levels. "The evidence strongly supports X (multiple large RCTs, consistent results)" is different from "There is preliminary evidence suggesting X (one small observational study)." Both are honest. Neither hedges.

## The Meta-Rule

After the slop pass, read the report aloud (mentally). Does it sound like something a knowledgeable person would write in an email to a colleague they respect? Or does it sound like a term paper, a corporate memo, or a chatbot response?

If the latter, it needs more work. The goal is: direct, specific, honest, and written for a reader who is smart and busy.

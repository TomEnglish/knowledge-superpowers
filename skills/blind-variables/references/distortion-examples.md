# Distortion Examples by Domain

Concrete examples of how the four distortion layers manifest across different topic types. Use these as templates when analyzing a new research question — they show what to look for, not what to conclude.

## 1. Medicine and Health

**Layer 1 (Research Economics):** Pharmaceutical companies fund the majority of clinical trials. Drug interventions are studied far more than lifestyle, dietary, or behavioral interventions because drugs are patentable and profitable. Generic off-label uses of existing drugs are understudied because there's no commercial incentive. Prevention research is chronically underfunded relative to treatment research.

**Layer 2 (Institutional Amplification):** The "gold standard" RCT model privileges interventions that can be isolated and controlled. Complex, multi-factor interventions (diet + exercise + sleep + stress management) are harder to study rigorously and therefore less represented in the literature even when they may be more effective. Medical textbooks lag decades behind current evidence on many topics.

**Layer 3 (Social Filtering):** Certain conditions carry stigma that distorts research framing — addiction (moral failure vs. neurological condition), chronic fatigue / fibromyalgia (psychosomatic dismissal), obesity (personal responsibility vs. environmental/metabolic factors). Research on psychedelic-assisted therapy was effectively banned for 40 years due to political moral panic, creating a massive gap now being rapidly but unevenly filled.

**Layer 4 (AI Filters):** Models tend to default to institutional medical consensus and add disclaimers ("consult your doctor"). This is appropriate for medical advice but can suppress engagement with legitimate scientific debate about whether the consensus is well-founded. The model may be reluctant to discuss off-label drug uses, alternative therapies with emerging evidence, or criticisms of standard-of-care protocols.

## 2. Nutrition and Diet

**Layer 1:** Food industry funding has shaped nutrition research for decades. Sugar industry funded research blaming fat. Dairy industry funded research supporting dairy consumption. The baseline "healthy diet" in most studies reflects industrial food system assumptions.

**Layer 2:** The diet-heart hypothesis (saturated fat causes heart disease) dominated for 50+ years and is embedded deeply in the training data despite significant challenges from more recent research. Nutritional epidemiology relies heavily on food frequency questionnaires with known massive error rates, but findings are reported with false precision.

**Layer 3:** Diet is deeply cultural and moralized. Veganism, carnivore diets, fasting, and other approaches carry tribal identity that distorts both research and reporting. "Balanced diet" language often masks specific institutional recommendations rather than genuine nutritional science.

**Layer 4:** The model will likely default to USDA/WHO dietary guidelines as "the science" without noting that these guidelines are products of political negotiation as much as evidence synthesis. It may hedge excessively around anything that contradicts official guidelines.

## 3. Psychology and Mental Health

**Layer 1:** The replication crisis hit psychology hardest. Many foundational findings (ego depletion, power posing, stereotype threat magnitude) have failed to replicate. The training data contains both the original confident claims and the later failures, and the model may not consistently distinguish between them.

**Layer 2:** DSM diagnostic categories dominate the literature. Alternative frameworks (dimensional models, network theory of psychopathology, cultural formulations) exist but are dramatically underrepresented. WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) participant pools mean most findings are from a narrow demographic slice presented as universal.

**Layer 3:** Mental health carries significant stigma that shapes both what gets researched and how it's framed. Certain diagnoses are over-pathologized (normal grief → "prolonged grief disorder"), others are under-recognized. The relationship between mental health and pharmaceutical industry creates incentives to frame normal human variation as treatable conditions.

**Layer 4:** Models are heavily trained toward crisis-intervention framing on mental health topics. Legitimate intellectual inquiry about the nature of depression, the limits of therapy, the philosophy of psychiatry, or the social construction of mental illness may trigger safety-oriented deflection rather than genuine engagement.

## 4. Technology and AI

**Layer 1:** Tech companies fund research that serves their interests. AI safety research is dramatically shaped by the priorities of a handful of well-funded labs. Open source alternatives to corporate AI are understudied in the formal literature. The "AI alignment" frame reflects specific philosophical commitments that aren't universally shared.

**Layer 2:** Techno-optimism and techno-pessimism both get amplified in cycles. The training data over-represents Silicon Valley perspectives, Effective Altruism frameworks, and English-language technology discourse. Non-Western approaches to technology governance are sparse.

**Layer 3:** AI discussion is politically charged. Certain positions (AI existential risk, AI as tool of oppression, AI as liberation technology) carry tribal markers. The model's own existence makes it an interested party in discussions about AI.

**Layer 4:** This is the most recursively distorted domain. The model discussing AI is a system discussing itself through the lens of its own training. Self-referential caution layers on top of every other distortion. The model may be reluctant to discuss its own limitations honestly, or may overcorrect by being performatively self-deprecating.

## 5. Economics and Policy

**Layer 1:** Economics research is funded by institutions with policy preferences. Central banks, development banks, think tanks, and governments all fund research aligned with their mandates. Heterodox economics (post-Keynesian, ecological economics, MMT, Austrian) is systematically underrepresented in mainstream journals.

**Layer 2:** The neoclassical framework dominates the training data. Models of rational agents, efficient markets, and equilibrium are presented as "economics" rather than as one paradigm among several. The 2008 financial crisis challenged many of these assumptions, but the training data still weights pre-crisis orthodoxy heavily.

**Layer 3:** Economic policy is inherently political. "Evidence-based policy" language often smuggles in value judgments about what counts as evidence and what outcomes matter. GDP growth as the primary success metric is a political choice, not a scientific consensus.

**Layer 4:** The model will likely default to mainstream economic consensus and present it as "what economics says" rather than "what the dominant school of economics says." It may be reluctant to engage seriously with heterodox positions.

## 6. History and Social Science

**Layer 1:** Historical research is shaped by what archives exist, who created them, and who has access. Colonial archives tell the colonizer's story. Oral histories, indigenous records, and non-written sources are systematically underrepresented.

**Layer 2:** English-language, Western-centric historical narratives dominate the training data. The "Great Man" theory of history is over-represented despite decades of social history challenging it. Periodization (Dark Ages, Renaissance, Enlightenment) reflects European frameworks applied globally.

**Layer 3:** History is a battleground for present-day politics. Narratives about colonialism, slavery, indigenous peoples, national identity, and historical atrocities are shaped by contemporary political pressures. The model's training data contains multiple contradictory framings of the same events.

**Layer 4:** The model may default to a diplomatic synthesis that doesn't reflect any actual historical school of thought. It may avoid stating uncomfortable historical facts plainly, or may present contested narratives as "both sides" when the evidence is not actually symmetrical.

## 7. Environmental Science

**Layer 1:** Climate research funding comes primarily from government agencies with policy mandates and environmental organizations with advocacy missions. Industry-funded research exists but has well-documented bias toward minimizing harm assessments. The funding landscape creates pressure toward findings that support policy action.

**Layer 2:** Climate change consensus is well-established in the scientific literature, but the training data also contains a massive volume of media debate, policy argument, and social media discourse that treats it as contested. The model may over-represent the "debate" relative to the scientific evidence.

**Layer 3:** Environmental topics are intensely polarized. Nuclear energy, GMOs, organic farming, degrowth, carbon capture — each carries political identity markers that distort how the evidence is presented and received.

**Layer 4:** The model will likely align strongly with IPCC consensus on climate (which is scientifically appropriate) but may be less willing to engage with legitimate debates about policy responses, mitigation strategy tradeoffs, or the limits of current modeling.

## 8. Consciousness, Spirituality, and Fringe Topics

**Layer 1:** Minimal institutional funding. Consciousness research exists at the margins of neuroscience. Psi research, near-death experiences, meditation neuroscience, and contemplative traditions are either unfunded or funded by small foundations with specific agendas.

**Layer 2:** The materialist paradigm dominates neuroscience and is heavily represented in the training data. Alternative frameworks (integrated information theory, panpsychism, dual-aspect monism) exist in the philosophical literature but are sparse in the scientific literature the model was trained on.

**Layer 3:** Heavy stigma. Researchers who investigate anomalous phenomena risk career damage. The result is that the most rigorous researchers often avoid the most interesting questions, and the most interesting questions attract less rigorous researchers — a self-reinforcing cycle that degrades the available evidence base.

**Layer 4:** Maximum AI filter effect. Topics adjacent to "pseudoscience" trigger strong disclaimers and dismissive framing. The model may refuse to engage seriously with evidence that doesn't fit the materialist framework, not because the evidence is poor, but because the topic carries safety/credibility flags in the training process. This is perhaps the clearest example of Layer 4 creating genuine blind spots — the model's reluctance is not proportional to the quality of the evidence but to the cultural category of the topic.

## How to Use These Examples

When analyzing a new research question with the blind-variables skill:

1. Identify which domain(s) the question touches
2. Use the relevant examples above as starting templates
3. Adapt to the specific question — generic distortion analysis is nearly useless; specific distortion analysis is the whole point
4. Note where multiple domains overlap (e.g., a question about psychedelic therapy touches Medicine, Psychology, and Consciousness — compounding distortions from all three)
5. Prioritize the 2-3 distortions most likely to affect *this specific research question* rather than listing everything

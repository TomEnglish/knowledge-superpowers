# Thyroid check before cholesterol-lowering drugs: guidelines, labels, practice and cost

## Question
What do current guidelines and drug labels say about testing for, and treating, hypothyroidism before starting a cholesterol-lowering drug? How often is it done in practice? How common is undiagnosed hypothyroidism among people with raised cholesterol or people starting statins? What goes wrong when it is missed (statin muscle damage, a poorer response to the drug)? What does the sequence of test, treat and retest cost in time and money, and how much raised cholesterol remains after thyroid has been corrected?

All web sources were checked on 21 November 2026.

## Key Findings

### 1. United States guidelines: one says "rule it out first" in terms; the main cholesterol guideline is narrower

**The 2018 AHA/ACC cholesterol guideline has been retired.** The ACC, AHA and nine other societies published a new "Guideline on the Management of Dyslipidemia" online on 13 March 2026 (J Am Coll Cardiol 2026;87(19):2624-2757; Circulation doi 10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423) [05.1]. The publisher pages for this guideline sit behind automated-access checks and could not be opened, so what follows on its content rests on search-engine extracts of the JACC text, an Indian Health Service slide set adapted from the official AHA slides, and a Circulation primer by authors writing about the guideline. Treat the wording as a lead to verify against the guideline itself.

- Severe hypercholesterolaemia: the extract reads "In adults with severe hypercholesterolemia with an LDL-C ≥190 mg/dL (4.9 mmol/L), secondary causes of dyslipidemia should be excluded and addressed to reduce LDL-C (Table 15)" (Section 4.2 per the extract; Class 1 per the slide set). Table 15 lists "Hypothyroidism" among diseases that cause dyslipidaemia; the wording "Uncontrolled hypothyroidism" appears only in the triglyceride table (Table 23). Verification on 21 November 2026 read the full guideline in an Internet Archive copy and confirmed the recommendation as section 4.2.4.3, class 1, level B-NR [05.1][05.2]. The Circulation primer says the same thing in its own words: LDL-C above 190 mg/dL "should prompt evaluation for secondary causes and familial hypercholesterolemia" [05.3].
- Raised triglycerides: secondary causes, hypothyroidism among them, are to be assessed and managed before triglyceride-specific treatment [05.2][05.3][05.4].
- Statin muscle symptoms: the treatment algorithm starts with "evaluated secondary causes of muscle symptoms" [05.2]; a search extract says the table of risk factors (Table 24) lists hypothyroidism [05.1].
- No recommendation to measure TSH in every adult before a statin was found in any of these extracts or summaries. The same guideline rates routine creatine kinase and routine liver tests in people on statins as Class 3: No Benefit [05.2]. Whether the full text contains a general baseline-TSH statement is unverified.

**The Endocrine Society's 2020 guideline is the only current US guideline found that tells clinicians, in terms, to check thyroid before any lipid drug** [05.5]:
- 6.1: "In patients with hyperlipidemia, we recommend ruling out hypothyroidism as the cause of the hyperlipidemia before treatment with lipid-lowering medications. (1⊕⊕⊕⊕)" (strong recommendation, high-quality evidence).
- 6.3: "In patients with overt hypothyroidism, we suggest against treating hyperlipidemia until the patient becomes euthyroid in order to more accurately assess the lipid profile. (2⊕OOO)" (conditional, very low quality).
- 6.4: "In patients with subclinical hypothyroidism (thyroid-stimulating hormone <10 mIU/L) with associated hyperlipidemia, we suggest considering thyroxine treatment as a means of reducing low-density lipoprotein levels. (2⊕OOO)", with the remark to weigh age, general health, the possibility of suppressing TSH and whether the patient has cardiovascular disease.
The guideline's own text says the effect of thyroxine on long-term cardiovascular outcomes "is unknown" [05.5]. Its Appendix A lists advisory-board ties between some writing-committee members and lipid-drug makers (Amgen, Sanofi, Regeneron, Akcea, Novartis and others) [05.5].

**The 2012 AACE/ATA hypothyroidism guideline** treats raised cholesterol as a reason to test, in softer language. It finds "compelling evidence to support case finding" in listed groups, including patients with the diagnoses in its Table 9, which names hypercholesterolaemia, mixed hyperlipidaemia and unspecified myopathy; Recommendation 21 says aggressive case finding "should be considered in those at increased risk for hypothyroidism" (Grade B) [05.6]. Eight of nine authors declared no relevant financial relationships [05.6].

**Three US documents say nothing.** The USPSTF statin recommendation (2022) does not contain the word "thyroid" [05.7], and the USPSTF rates evidence for screening asymptomatic adults for thyroid dysfunction as insufficient (I statement, 2015) [05.8]. The VA/DoD lipid guideline (December 2025, 157 pages, 24 recommendations) does not mention thyroid or TSH at all [05.9]. An American Family Physician editorial notes that the VA/DoD guideline excluded recommendations resting on expert opinion alone, while some Class 1 recommendations in the 2026 ACC/AHA guideline rest on non-randomised studies or expert opinion [05.10].

### 2. United Kingdom and Europe: NICE has stepped back from routine testing; ESC/EAS is silent

- **NICE narrowed the test in May 2023.** The 2014 guideline CG181 (rec 1.3.13) listed "thyroid-stimulating hormone" among baseline tests for everyone before a statin [05.11]. The current guideline NG238 (published 14 December 2023, minor updates to October 2025) lists, at rec 1.5.5, "thyroid-stimulating hormone level in people with symptoms of underactive or overactive thyroid. [May 2023, amended December 2023]" [05.12]. NICE's published rationale section does not explain the change [05.12].
- NG238 still says: "Exclude possible common secondary causes of dyslipidaemia (such as excess alcohol intake, uncontrolled diabetes, hypothyroidism, liver disease and nephrotic syndrome) before referring for specialist review" (1.4.3); "Before starting statins, treat comorbidities and secondary causes of dyslipidaemia" for primary prevention (1.6.6); and for people who already have cardiovascular disease, "Treat comorbidities and secondary causes of dyslipidaemia at the same time as starting statin treatment" (1.7.6) [05.12]. NICE does not grade individual recommendations; "offer" and "consider" carry the strength.
- **The NICE quality statement was removed.** Quality standard QS100 (2015) had a statement that adults with a 10-year risk of 10% or more "are assessed for secondary causes before any offer of statin treatment" [05.13]. On 23 July 2025 NICE replaced the 2015 statements; none of the five current statements covers secondary causes [05.14].
- NICE's thyroid guideline NG145 (last updated 12 October 2023) does not list raised cholesterol among the indications for thyroid testing [05.15]. NHS Specialist Pharmacy Service guidance (updated 6 November 2024) says "Manage hypothyroidism adequately before starting a statin" [05.16].
- **ESC/EAS.** The 2019 ESC/EAS dyslipidaemia guideline mentions hypothyroidism once, as a co-factor in familial dysbetalipoproteinaemia, and has no recommendation on excluding it before treatment [05.17]. The 2025 Focused Update does not contain the words "thyroid" or "secondary cause" [05.18]. The 2025 update states it was funded by the ESC and EAS "with no involvement of the healthcare industry"; members' declarations are in a separate report that could not be opened [05.18].
- The 2013 European Thyroid Association guideline advises rechecking lipids after levothyroxine is started for subclinical hypothyroidism "to see whether there has been adequate improvement, or whether additional therapy for dyslipidaemia is necessary"; its task force had no commercial support and declared no conflicts [05.19].

### 3. Drug labels: the US wording has thinned; the UK wording is stronger

- Current FDA statin labels name "uncontrolled hypothyroidism" as a risk factor for myopathy in section 5.1 and say nothing about testing for it: Lipitor (revised 4/2024), Crestor (revised 09/2026), Zocor (revised 8/2023) [05.20][05.21][05.22]. The patient leaflets tell people to mention "thyroid problems" before starting.
- An older-format label still in use, generic lovastatin (DailyMed version effective 13 November 2026), keeps the earlier instruction: "Prior to initiating therapy with lovastatin, secondary causes for hypercholesterolemia (e.g., poorly controlled diabetes mellitus, hypothyroidism, nephrotic syndrome, dysproteinemias, obstructive liver disease, other drug therapy, alcoholism) should be excluded" [05.23]. That sentence is absent from the current Lipitor, Crestor and Zocor labels [05.20][05.21][05.22].
- Triglyceride drugs keep an explicit step. Vascepa (revised 03/2026) and fenofibrate (Lipofen) say, under "Prior to Initiation": "Identify other causes (e.g., diabetes mellitus, hypothyroidism, or medications) of high triglyceride levels and manage as appropriate" [05.24][05.25].
- Current labels for ezetimibe, bempedoic acid, evolocumab and inclisiran do not mention hypothyroidism as something to check [05.26].
- UK rosuvastatin label (Crestor SmPC, text revised 30 March 2026): "In patients with secondary hypercholesterolaemia caused by hypothyroidism or nephrotic syndrome, the underlying disease should be treated prior to initiating therapy with Crestor"; the 40 mg dose is contraindicated in patients with predisposing factors for myopathy, hypothyroidism among them [05.27]. UK atorvastatin label (Lipitor SmPC, 08/2026): a creatine kinase level "should be measured before starting statin treatment" in listed situations, including hypothyroidism [05.28].

### 4. How often the check is done

One study answers this, and it is old. Willard, Leung and Pearce reviewed 8,795 adults at Boston Medical Center primary care clinics (2003 to 2011) with a first total cholesterol of 200 mg/dL or more or LDL-C of 160 mg/dL or more and no prior lipid or thyroid drug. TSH was checked within about 6 months in 49.5%. Of those tested, 226 (5.2%) had a raised TSH: 151 (3.5%) at 5 to 10 mIU/L and 74 (1.7%) above 10 mIU/L. Half of those with a raised TSH (50.7%) were given levothyroxine; 30% of those above 10 mIU/L were not. Of the levothyroxine-treated patients, 79% had their hypothyroidism corrected and 60.5% had no lipid recheck [05.29]. The study was NIH-funded, single-centre and retrospective, and could not see tests done elsewhere. No later US study, and no UK or European audit of TSH testing before statins, was found.

The yield of previously unknown overt hypothyroidism is about 1 to 3 per 100 people tested in clinic-based series, higher in women and at higher cholesterol levels. Two studies argue against indiscriminate testing [05.35][05.36]. The definitions all rest on TSH, so none of these studies can count people with symptoms and a normal TSH.

Separately, a recorded diagnosis of hypothyroidism is already present in 5.5% of new statin users in English and Welsh general practice (12,378 of 225,922; 1.7% of non-users) [05.37] and in about 7% of UK statin initiators in a later cohort [05.38]. These are known cases; neither study reports whether they were adequately treated when the statin was started.

### 6. What goes wrong when it is missed

- **Muscle symptoms.** Cohort-level evidence shows a modest association. In PRIMO, a French survey of 7,924 outpatients on high-dose statins, untreated hypothyroidism carried an odds ratio of 1.71 for muscle symptoms in multivariate analysis [05.39][05.40]. In a US health-system case-control study (2,686 statin-intolerant patients, 8,103 controls), a history of hypothyroidism was present in 30.2% versus 21.5% (odds ratio 1.49, 95% CI 1.34 to 1.65) [05.41]. A meta-analysis of 176 studies (4.1 million patients) found hypothyroidism associated with statin intolerance (odds ratio 1.37, P = 0.01) [05.42]. The last two concern diagnosed, mostly treated hypothyroidism, which is a different exposure from an undiagnosed case. A small Hungarian series found abnormal thyroid tests in only 5 of 101 patients with statin adverse effects and no TSH difference between those with and without myopathy [05.43].
- **Severe muscle damage.** For rhabdomyolysis in undiagnosed hypothyroidism, only case reports were found. A typical one: creatine kinase 4,517 U/L three months after starting rosuvastatin, TSH then found to be 114 mU/L in a man with none of the classic symptoms [05.44]. Case reports over-represent dramatic outcomes; no cohort estimate of this risk exists in what was searched.
- **Poorer response to the drug.** No study was found that measures statin LDL response by thyroid status. The claim rests on clinical experience ("hypothyroidism-induced dyslipidemia sometimes is resistant to lipid-lowering therapy") in the same case report [05.44].
- **An avoidable lifelong prescription.** In the Boston study, 25% of levothyroxine-treated patients were prescribed a lipid drug within a year against 44% of untreated hyperlipidaemic patients, and 21 of the 34 treated patients who had lipids rechecked (61.8%) no longer met the study's hyperlipidaemia threshold [05.29]. This is observational and the rechecked group is small.

### 7. Cost in money and time

- **Tests.** Kentucky Medicaid's 2026 Clinical Diagnostic Laboratory Fee Schedule (revised 23 September 2026) pays $16.80 for TSH (CPT 84443), $13.39 for a lipid panel (80061), $9.02 for free T4 (84439), $6.51 for creatine kinase (82550) and $9.34 for the blood draw (36415) [05.45]. These match the Medicare Clinical Laboratory Fee Schedule amounts as far as could be checked without opening the CMS file, which sits behind an AMA licence click-through that was not accepted [05.46]. CMS's preliminary 2027 rates, posted 21 September 2026, are on average about 16% lower for codes with private-payer data, with any cut capped at 15% a year from 2027 [05.47]. No UK unit price was found.
- **Drugs.** Pharmacy acquisition cost (NADAC, effective 18 November 2026): levothyroxine 50 mcg $0.042 and 100 mcg $0.052 per tablet (about $15 to $19 a year); atorvastatin 20 mg $0.028 per tablet (about $10 a year); ezetimibe 10 mg $0.072 [05.48]. Retail prices are higher; NADAC is what pharmacies pay.
- **Yield.** By arithmetic on the Boston figures, about 19 TSH tests find one raised result and about 59 find one above 10 mIU/L, roughly $320 and $990 in laboratory fees per case at $16.80 [05.29][05.45]. No cost-effectiveness study of testing before statins was found; the Boston authors called for one [05.29]. A 1996 model of adding TSH to five-yearly cholesterol screening from age 35 estimated $9,223 per quality-adjusted life year for women and $22,595 for men [05.49].
- **Time.** Guidelines set TSH rechecks 4 to 8 weeks after starting or changing levothyroxine (AACE/ATA Recommendation 13, Grade B) [05.6], 4 to 6 weeks in the 2014 ATA guideline [05.50], and 8 to 12 weeks in the ETA guideline [05.19]. One or two dose adjustments therefore put the lipid recheck about 2 to 4 months after diagnosis of overt hypothyroidism (inference from those intervals). For subclinical hypothyroidism NICE asks for two raised TSH results 3 months apart before considering treatment [05.15]. The 2026 ACC/AHA guideline rechecks lipids 4 to 12 weeks after a therapy change [05.2].

### 8. How much raised cholesterol remains after thyroid is corrected

- The meta-analysis commissioned for the Endocrine Society guideline: in overt hypothyroidism, baseline LDL-C 168.8 mg/dL fell by 41.1 mg/dL (95% CI 35.7 to 46.5; 55 studies) and total cholesterol 260.3 mg/dL fell by 58.4 mg/dL. In subclinical hypothyroidism, baseline LDL-C 139.5 mg/dL fell by 11.1 mg/dL (9.0 to 13.1; 74 studies) [05.5]. Subtracting the means leaves an average LDL-C near 128 mg/dL in both groups (my arithmetic; effect sizes belong to thread 01).
- The ETA guideline states that levothyroxine in subclinical hypothyroidism lowers total and LDL cholesterol "although normalisation of serum lipids is seldom achieved" [05.19]. A 1996 re-analysis reached the same conclusion: total cholesterol fell 0.4 mmol/L "but plasma levels remain elevated in most patients" [05.51].
- In the Amsterdam and Greek clinics, significant lipid falls occurred only when pre-treatment TSH was 10 mU/L or more, or above 12 mU/L [05.30][05.31].
- At the University of Chicago (10,723 outpatients started on levothyroxine, 2006 to 2019), statin use rose from 19.1% to 24.6% after levothyroxine was started, at a median of 76 weeks, despite normalised TSH [05.52].

### 9. Symptoms with a normal TSH

No current guideline supports thyroid treatment ahead of a lipid drug when TSH is normal. NICE NG238 uses symptoms only as the trigger to measure TSH [05.12]. The 2014 ATA guideline says: "We strongly recommend against the use of levothyroxine treatment in patients who have nonspecific symptoms and normal biochemical indices of thyroid function" (Recommendation 10a; strong, high-quality evidence) and finds "insufficient evidence of benefit" for targeting low-normal TSH in treated patients with dyslipidaemia (7d; strong, moderate quality) [05.50]. The 2012 AACE/ATA guideline says thyroid hormones "should not be used to treat symptoms suggestive of hypothyroidism without biochemical confirmation" (Recommendation 29, Grade B) and that cholesterol "should not be used to diagnose hypothyroidism" (Recommendation 6, Grade B) [05.6]. Because every guideline defines the disease by TSH, none of them tests the older symptom-based standard; they simply do not address it.

## What this means for the person's situation

No individual is assumed here. For any adult offered a cholesterol-lowering drug:

- Asking whether a TSH has been checked is reasonable and cheap (under $20 at public-payer rates). It is not guaranteed to have happened: half of US patients in the one study were not tested, and UK guidance since 2023 tests only people with thyroid symptoms. The case for testing is strongest when LDL-C is 190 mg/dL or higher, triglycerides are raised, there are symptoms of an underactive thyroid, or muscle symptoms appear on a statin; these are the situations current guidelines and labels name.
- If the test shows overt hypothyroidism (as a rule TSH above 10 mIU/L with low free T4), the Endocrine Society and the UK rosuvastatin label support treating the thyroid first and rechecking lipids once thyroid tests are normal, roughly 2 to 4 months later. LDL-C falls by about 40 mg/dL on average, which removes the need for a drug in some people and not in others. For people who already have cardiovascular disease, NICE says to treat both at once and not delay the statin.
- If TSH is mildly raised (below 10 mIU/L), the expected LDL-C fall is about 11 mg/dL, cholesterol usually stays raised, and guidelines only "suggest considering" thyroxine. Thyroid treatment is unlikely to replace a lipid drug here.
- If TSH is normal, today's guidelines offer no route to "thyroid first", whatever the symptoms. That is a statement about what guidelines say, since they define the disease by TSH; it is not evidence that a symptom-based approach fails.
- Leaving an underactive thyroid undetected while taking a statin is associated with more muscle symptoms (odds ratios 1.4 to 1.7); severe muscle breakdown is documented only in case reports.

Points that depend on unconfirmed assumptions: the US-first framing matters, because a UK reader will find routine testing is no longer standard and a European reader will find the ESC/EAS guideline silent. The reading of "healthy" as the pre-TSH, symptom-based standard does not change any finding in this thread, because no current guideline or label uses that standard; if "healthy" instead meant a TSH in the lower part of the reference range, the 2014 ATA guideline has considered and declined that target for dyslipidaemia (Recommendation 7d).

## Load-bearing claims
- C05.1: The Endocrine Society guideline (2020) recommends "ruling out hypothyroidism as the cause of the hyperlipidemia before treatment with lipid-lowering medications" (rec 6.1, strong, high-quality evidence) and suggests not treating hyperlipidaemia in overt hypothyroidism until euthyroid (rec 6.3, conditional, very low quality). [05.5]
- C05.2: The 2018 AHA/ACC guideline was replaced on 13 March 2026. The 2026 ACC/AHA guideline tells clinicians to exclude or evaluate secondary causes in three situations: LDL-C ≥190 mg/dL (section 4.2.4.3, class 1; Table 15 lists hypothyroidism), persistently raised triglycerides (section 4.2.9; Table 23 lists uncontrolled hypothyroidism) and statin muscle symptoms (section 4.2.11; Table 24 lists hypothyroidism as a cause of the symptoms). It has no recommendation to test TSH before a statin: a search of the full text in an Internet Archive copy on 21 November 2026 found no mention of TSH testing (the online data supplement was not searched). [05.1][05.2][05.3]
- C05.3: NICE moved from TSH for everyone before a statin (CG181, 2014, rec 1.3.13) to TSH only "in people with symptoms of underactive or overactive thyroid" (NG238 rec 1.5.5, published 14 December 2023; the wording was introduced in the May 2023 update of CG181). Its 23 July 2025 update of quality standard QS100 replaced the 2015 statements, which included "Excluding secondary causes", and none of the current statements covers secondary causes. [05.11][05.12][05.13][05.14]
- C05.4: In the only practice study found, 49.5% of 8,795 US adults with newly found hyperlipidaemia (2003-2011) had TSH checked within about 6 months; 5.2% of those tested had a raised TSH and 1.7% had TSH above 10 mIU/L. [05.29]
- C05.5: Current FDA statin labels list "uncontrolled hypothyroidism" as a myopathy risk factor without asking for a test (Lipitor 4/2024, Crestor 09/2026, Zocor 8/2023); the UK Crestor label (30 March 2026) says hypothyroidism "should be treated prior to initiating therapy" and contraindicates the 40 mg dose. [05.20][05.21][05.22][05.27]
- C05.6: A TSH test is listed at $16.80 and a lipid panel at $13.39 (Kentucky Medicaid 2026 schedule); after treatment of overt hypothyroidism LDL-C falls by a mean 41.1 mg/dL from 168.8 mg/dL, and by 11.1 mg/dL in subclinical hypothyroidism. [05.45][05.5]

## Confidence Notes
- **Strong:** the wording of NICE NG238, CG181 and QS100, the Endocrine Society, AACE/ATA, ATA and ETA guidelines, the FDA labels and UK SmPCs, and the Boston practice study were read directly and quoted from the documents. The absence of thyroid content in the ESC/EAS 2019 and 2025 texts, the VA/DoD 2025 guideline and the USPSTF statement was checked by searching the complete texts for "thyroid", "TSH" and "secondary cause".
- **Weak:** everything said about the content of the 2026 ACC/AHA guideline. ahajournals.org and jacc.org returned bot-verification pages; these were not bypassed. Section numbers, the class of the muscle-symptom recommendation and the contents of Tables 15 and 24 come from search-engine extracts and a slide set and need checking against the guideline. The 2018 guideline was readable only as a PMC executive summary whose recommendation tables are images; it is not relied on.
- **Reading convention:** "read: full" means the complete text was retrieved, searched for every term relevant to this thread, and the relevant sections were read. It does not mean every page of a 150-page guideline was read line by line.
- **Judgement calls:** the 2026 ACC/AHA guideline is treated as the current US cholesterol standard and the 2018 guideline as superseded. The Endocrine Society guideline is counted as a current US guideline because no later edition was found. A state Medicaid schedule stands in for the Medicare rate file, because opening the CMS file means accepting a licence on someone's behalf. Bot-verification pages were not worked around.
- **Hosted copies:** four guideline PDFs were read from third-party hosts because the journals blocked automated access. One (the 2025 ESC/EAS update) was hosted by Sanofi, a lipid-drug maker; it is the journal PDF, unaltered as far as could be seen.
- **Prices:** the $16.80 TSH figure is from a state Medicaid schedule, not the Medicare file. A search extract of a Utah Medicaid report gives the Medicare amount for 84443 as $16.80 in July 2024, and Congress delayed cuts through 2026, so the two probably agree; a commercial coding site gave $16.44, which was not verified. Rates are likely to fall in January 2027.
- **Old and thin data:** the one practice study covers 2003 to 2011 at one inner-city academic centre. Prevalence studies date from 1991 to 2014 and half are known only from abstracts. Lipid-clinic series over-state prevalence relative to primary care.
- **Circularity:** every guideline and prevalence study defines hypothyroidism by TSH, so none can speak to symptomatic people with normal TSH. The guideline statements against treating them rest on trials that enrolled by symptoms and judged by TSH-era outcomes; assessing those trials belongs to other threads.
- **Funding:** thyroid hormone has no sponsor, and the documents that say most about thyroid-first (Endocrine Society, AACE/ATA, ETA) come from endocrine societies, while cardiology guidelines say least. Industry ties on the 2026 ACC/AHA and ESC/EAS panels could not be read. PRIMO's funder was not checked.
- **Confirmation risk:** the starting position favours "thyroid first". The findings against it are reported: NICE dropped routine testing, two population studies found low yield, mild hypothyroidism barely moves LDL-C, and statin use rises after levothyroxine is started.
- No web page contained instructions addressed to the researcher.

## Gaps
- **2026 ACC/AHA guideline full text:** not opened. Needed: exact wording, section numbers, class and level of evidence for the secondary-cause and muscle-symptom recommendations, the contents of Tables 15 and 24, whether any general baseline-TSH statement exists, and the panel's industry relationships. Searched: ahajournals.org, jacc.org, PMC, Europe PMC, the Elsevier text-mining API, professional.heart.org, acc.org.
- **How often TSH is checked before a statin:** nothing after 2011, nothing outside one US centre, nothing for the UK since NICE's 2023 change. Searched PubMed and the web for audits, electronic-record studies and claims analyses. Likely reason: no quality measure tracks it, and NICE has now removed the one that did.
- **Why NICE dropped routine TSH (2023) and the quality statement (2025):** the rationale pages do not say. The committee papers and evidence reviews were not located.
- **Risk of rhabdomyolysis in undiagnosed hypothyroidism:** only case reports. A search extract attributes a hazard ratio of 3.47 for hypothyroidism in women to the QResearch statin risk-score paper (Hippisley-Cox and Coupland, Heart 2010); the paper could not be opened and the figure is an unverified lead.
- **Statin LDL response by thyroid status:** no study found. Searched PubMed and the web for response, resistance and refractory hypercholesterolaemia with hypothyroidism.
- **Cost-effectiveness of TSH before statins:** not studied; the 1996 model addresses general screening. UK test prices and the Medicare rate file were not obtained.
- **Not studied at all:** a trial of "thyroid first" against "statin first". The trial that would settle it would randomise statin-eligible adults with TSH between the upper reference limit and 10 mIU/L (and, separately, adults with hypothyroid symptoms and normal TSH) to levothyroxine first or statin first, and count LDL-C and apoB, statin need at 12 months, muscle symptoms, over-replacement and cardiovascular events. Likely reason for its absence: no sponsor.
- **ESC declarations of interest and the NLA Part 1 recommendations (2015)** could not be opened. No newer ATA guideline for non-pregnant adults was found; the 2014 guideline appears to stand.

The 2014 ATA guideline's admissions that a normal TSH may not mean euthyroidism in all tissues, that a liothyronine dose normalised cholesterol while TSH stayed raised, and that a symptom score tracked cholesterol and ankle reflex time but not TSH (threads 02 and 03).

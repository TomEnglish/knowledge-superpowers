# Low TSH without raised thyroid hormone: what each TSH band means, and what the outcome studies can and cannot separate

## Question
What happens to heart disease, atrial fibrillation, fracture and death when TSH is low but free T4 and free T3 are not raised? Which studies separate a low TSH from excess thyroid hormone, in untreated people and in people taking thyroid hormone (levothyroxine, liothyronine, combination, desiccated thyroid), and what does each TSH band usually mean?

## Key Findings

### Verdict on the further starting position
The starting position has three parts. Each one holds only in part.

1. **"A TSH below about 0.01 most likely reflects hyperthyroidism."** Largely right for untreated outpatients, if "hyperthyroidism" includes the subclinical form. On a third-generation assay, 96% of hyperthyroid patients had TSH below 0.005, while most hospital patients whose low TSH (below 0.1 on an earlier assay) came from illness or glucocorticoids had TSH above 0.01 [06.10]. But in people over 60 an undetectable TSH usually came with a *normal* free T4 [06.11], and on levothyroxine after thyroidectomy a low TSH usually came with a normal free T3 [06.16].
2. **"The harms attributed to a low TSH may be harms of excess thyroid hormone."** For fracture the evidence leans this way, with one small study against [06.34]. For atrial fibrillation it fits only if "excess" includes free T4 and T3 that are inside the reference range but higher than the person's own baseline (see (e)).
3. **"There seems to be no research that isolates people with a low TSH who are not hyperthyroid."** Partly wrong. Several large studies required a normal free T4, and two also required or tested a normal T3. They still found more atrial fibrillation, cardiovascular disease and deaths at a low TSH, including at 0.10 to 0.44 mIU/L [06.4][06.13][06.23]. Guidelines call these people "subclinical hyperthyroid" because their TSH is low, so the label is circular. What is missing is outcome research in people whose low TSH has another cause, and in treated people whose free T3 is known to be normal (see Gaps).

### (a) What each TSH band usually means

All TSH values here are in mIU/L, which is the same as mU/L and µIU/mL.

| TSH band | Guideline label | Untreated adults | On thyroid hormone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Below 0.01 (undetectable on a third-generation assay) | Part of grade 2 subclinical hyperthyroidism if free T4 and T3 are normal [06.9] | 96% of hyperthyroid patients had TSH below 0.005 [06.10]. In people over 60 it occurred in about 1.4% to 1.5%, and 14 of 16 still had a low TSH at 12 months [06.11] | Dose above the pituitary set point. Among treated patients after thyroidectomy with any TSH below 0.5, the free T4 was often above range and the free T3 usually within range; the source does not report the below-0.01 group separately (see below) [06.16] |
| Below 0.1 ("suppressed") | Grade 2 subclinical hyperthyroidism [06.9] | US prevalence 0.7% [06.9]. Progression to overt disease 5% to 8% a year [06.9] | Over-replacement, or deliberate suppression after thyroid cancer |
| 0.1 to 0.39 ("low but detectable") | Grade 1 subclinical hyperthyroidism [06.9] | Normalises in 25% to 50% of cases [06.9]. Progression to overt disease 0.5% to 0.7% over 7 years [06.9] | Mild over-replacement, or a T3-containing regimen |
| 0.45 to about 1 ("low-normal") | Inside the reference range. Not a disease category | Lower on average in Black adults: geometric mean 1.18 against 1.45 in white adults in NHANES III [06.22] | — |

**How often a low TSH comes with raised free hormones.**
- *Untreated, over 60 (UK, 1,210 people):* TSH was below normal in 6.3% of women and 5.5% of men and undetectable in 1.5% and 1.4%, yet only one person had a raised free T4 at the first test [06.11].
- *Untreated, primary care (Greater Manchester, 2010 to 2022):* of 12,864 results with TSH below 0.4 in people with no thyroid diagnosis or medication, 99.1% had free T4 of 9 to 24.9 pmol/L and 0.6% had 25 or more [06.15]. Excluding diagnosed thyroid disease makes this an underestimate.
- *On levothyroxine, same dataset:* of 103,496 results with TSH below 0.4, 89.9% had free T4 of 9 to 24.9 pmol/L, 9.9% had 25 or more and 0.2% were low. Too few free T3 results existed to analyse [06.15].
- *On levothyroxine after total thyroidectomy (Kuma Hospital, Japan):* of 207 patients with TSH below 0.5, 146 (70.5%) had free T4 above the 0.9 to 1.7 ng/dL range, but 189 (91.3%) had free T3 within range, 8 (3.9%) above and 10 (4.8%) below [06.16].
- The two free T4 results differ largely because the upper limits differ (1.7 ng/dL, about 22 pmol/L, against 25 pmol/L). Only the Japanese study measured free T3.
- *Pooled cohorts:* of 2,188 people with TSH below 0.45 and a normal free T4, 143 had abnormal free or total T3. T3 had been measured in only 5 of the 10 cohorts [06.4].

**Causes of a low TSH without raised hormone** (frequencies are given only where a source gives one).
- *Non-thyroidal illness and glucocorticoids:* in 37 hospital patients without hyperthyroidism and TSH below 0.1, 32 (86%) had a detectable TSH above 0.01 [06.10].
- *Other causes listed by the European guideline:* dopamine or high-dose glucocorticoids, psychiatric illness, hypothalamic or pituitary disease, recovery after treatment of overt hyperthyroidism, transient thyroiditis, healthy centenarians in iodine-deficient areas, Black people and some smokers [06.9]. The guideline gives no frequencies for these [06.9].
- *Central (pituitary or hypothalamic) hypothyroidism:* estimated prevalence 1 in 16,000 to 1 in 100,000. Low free T4 with a low or normal TSH. Treatment aims for a free T4 above the median of the range, and TSH is not used to monitor it [06.21].
- *T3-containing treatment:* in 10 thyroidectomised patients given liothyronine to a TSH of 0.5 to 1.5, free T4 fell below detection. Free T3 averaged 5.9 ± 2.6 pmol/L against 4.0 ± 0.4 on levothyroxine (range 2.8 to 6.5) [06.18]. On a T3-containing regimen, then, a normal or low free T4 says nothing about hormone excess. Only a T3 measurement can show it.
- *Assay artefact:* among about 2 million people tested, 20 of South Asian descent had a falsely undetectable TSH (below 0.01) on 4 of 8 assays because of a TSH variant. Seven were wrongly given methimazole [06.20].
- Free T4 can also mislead on levothyroxine: 72% to 81% of results with a high free T4 but an unsuppressed TSH came from levothyroxine users, most likely because of when the dose was taken relative to the blood draw [06.19].

**Assay limits.** First-generation assays (functional sensitivity 1 to 2 mIU/L) cannot detect subclinical hyperthyroidism, and the pooled analyses excluded them [06.4]. The first third-generation assay (1990) reached 0.018 mU/L, against an average of 0.23 for ten second-generation assays [06.10]. Current assays detect 0.01 to 0.02 [06.9]. "Below 0.01" therefore means "below this assay's limit", and studies cut their bands differently: 0.03 or below in Tayside [06.8], 0.01 in one thyroid cancer clinic [06.37].

**What happens next to an untreated low TSH.**
- UK, over 60: 38 of 50 people (76%) with a low but detectable TSH were normal at 12 months, while 14 of 16 with an undetectable TSH were still low [06.11].
- Tayside (2,024 cases; normal free or total T4 and normal total T3 on two tests at least 4 months apart): 63.0% were still subclinical at 7 years, 35.6% had reverted to normal and only 0.5% to 0.7% became overtly hyperthyroid. Reverting was more common at TSH 0.1 to 0.4 [06.12].
- Dutch primary care (11,163 cases, median 55 months): 47% recovered, 11% persisted, 8% became overtly hyperthyroid, 5% became hypothyroid and 29% had no follow-up. A TSH below 0.1 halved the odds of recovery (OR 0.50, 0.43 to 0.58) and raised the odds of progression (OR 2.36, 1.97 to 2.83) [06.14].

**Did Xu 2023's lowest fifth of TSH include people with hyperthyroidism? Yes.**
- Only whole cohorts made up solely of pregnant women, people with overt thyroid disease or people with cardiovascular disease were excluded, along with participants under 18. Restricting the data to euthyroid participants and removing thyroid-medication users (5,722, 4.6% at baseline) were separate sensitivity analyses, so the main analysis included people with TSH below the range, any overt hyperthyroidism in the cohorts, and people taking thyroid hormone. No sentence in the paper says this outright; it follows from the design [06.1].
- The authors report that the euthyroid-only analysis "showed similar results to our main analyses" and that excluding medication users "did not meaningfully change the risk estimates". The figures are in an appendix I could not read [06.1].
- My own rough estimate, not from any source: 3.2% to 4.2% of people in overlapping cohorts had TSH below 0.45 [06.4][06.5], so roughly one sixth to one fifth of the lowest fifth would be below the range. The lowest fifth's HR of 1.09 for death (thread 03 [03.33]) is therefore mostly a low-normal TSH result, adjusted for age and sex only [06.1].

### (c) Studies that come closest to isolating a low TSH

**Untreated people with a low TSH and a normal free T4 (T3 checked in some studies).**
- *Collet 2012, TSH 0.10 to 0.44 with normal free T4 (350 people with a low TSH and no free T4 value were kept in the main analysis):* total mortality HR 1.23 (1.04 to 1.45), coronary death 1.24 (0.96 to 1.61) and atrial fibrillation 1.63 (1.10 to 2.41), adjusted for age and sex [06.4]. Thread 04 reported only the last two [04.53].
- *Collet 2012, after excluding 143 people with abnormal T3:* total mortality HR 1.20 (1.01 to 1.43), coronary death 1.25 (0.98 to 1.60) and atrial fibrillation 1.64 (1.14 to 2.35) [06.4]. One pooled cohort was of cardiology inpatients; excluding it left total mortality at HR 1.26 (1.06 to 1.51) [06.4].
- *Tayside (TEARS) morbidity study:* persistent low TSH (two tests at least 4 months apart) with normal free or total T4 and normal total T3, against five matched people each. After excluding those who later became overtly hyperthyroid, cardiovascular morbidity HR was 1.36 (1.19 to 1.57), dysrhythmia 1.39 (1.02 to 1.90) and dementia 1.79 (1.28 to 2.51), and the abstract reports no association with fracture [06.13]. Collet reports TEARS band HRs for nonfatal cardiovascular disease of 1.67 (1.45 to 1.92) at 0.10 to 0.40 and 1.74 (1.36 to 2.21) below 0.10 [06.4]. These are higher than the overall 1.39 in the abstract, and I could not reconcile them without the full text [06.13].
- *Copenhagen (586,460 people; prior thyroid treatment, amiodarone and digoxin excluded):* with a normal free T4, atrial fibrillation IRR was 1.12 (1.03 to 1.21) at TSH 0.2 to 0.4, 1.16 (0.99 to 1.36) at 0.1 to 0.2 and 1.41 (1.25 to 1.59) below 0.1. T3 was not measured. The IRRs are adjusted for sex, age, calendar year, Charlson comorbidity index and socioeconomic status. Mean free T4 was 16.89 pmol/L in the subclinical hyperthyroid group (TSH below 0.2) against 15.05 in the euthyroid group (TSH 0.2 to 5.0), so "normal free T4" did not mean the same free T4 [06.23]. In the same laboratory population, subclinical hyperthyroidism carried all-cause mortality IRR 1.23 (1.16 to 1.30) [06.45].
- *US Cardiovascular Health Study (over 65, no thyroid medication):* 47 people with TSH below 0.45 and normal free T4 had atrial fibrillation HR 1.98 (1.29 to 3.03), with no excess coronary disease or death [06.24].
- Within the normal range, free T4 tracked atrial fibrillation even when TSH was normal (Birmingham, over 65, cross-sectional) [06.25]. In Rotterdam, the top quarter of free T4 had HR 1.63 (1.19 to 2.22) and TSH was not associated [06.26].

**Treated people analysed by free T4 or free T3 as well as TSH.**
- *US veterans, cardiovascular death:* TSH and free T4 were modelled separately. TSH below 0.1 gave HR 1.39 and free T4 above 1.9 ng/dL gave HR 1.29 (1.20 to 1.40). No analysis cross-classified the two [06.6].
- *US veterans, heart failure:* mild over-replacement (TSH 0.1 to 0.5) went with fewer cases (AOR 0.94, 0.91 to 0.97). TSH below 0.1 gave 1.06 (1.01 to 1.12) and free T4 above 1.9 ng/dL gave 1.23 (1.14 to 1.32) [06.42].
- *Kuma Hospital, tissue markers (no outcomes):* 133 patients completed the study, measured before and 12 months after total thyroidectomy for papillary cancer [06.17].
  - TSH above 0.03 and up to 0.3 (46 patients), with free T3 matching preoperative levels: lipids and SHBG did not differ significantly from preoperative values. Bone markers were tested only in the 24 of these with PTH above 10 pg/mL, and also did not differ significantly (bone alkaline phosphatase p = 0.077) [06.17].
  - TSH 0.03 or below: SHBG and bone alkaline phosphatase rose [06.17].
  - TSH 0.3 to 5: LDL rose [06.17].

  This is the closest human evidence that a low but detectable TSH, with free T3 held at the person's own baseline, carries no sign of hormone excess at tissue level. It has no outcome data.
- I found no outcome study (heart events, atrial fibrillation, fracture or death) in treated people with a low TSH whose free T4 *and* free T3 were both known to be in range (see Gaps).

**Liothyronine, combination and desiccated thyroid users analysed by TSH.**
- *US TriNetX records:* among people with at least two TSH values below 0.45, levothyroxine alone carried more atrial fibrillation and death than levothyroxine plus liothyronine or desiccated thyroid [06.40].
- The low-TSH result is reported in direction only in the main text, with its figures in a supplementary table that was not read. The atrial fibrillation HR of 1.069 (1.015 to 1.125) for levothyroxine alone against T3-containing regimens is the comparison across all TSH levels, not the low-TSH result. Free T4 and free T3 were not used [06.40].
- *Sweden, registry:* 11,147 liothyronine users had no excess deaths (all-cause HR 0.69 in dose-adjusted models). The abstract reports no TSH analysis [06.41]. Thread 04 covers the Scottish and meta-analytic liothyronine data [04.69][04.70].

**Thyroid cancer survivors on TSH suppression.**
- *Groningen, atrial fibrillation:* 518 patients had 2.47 times the atrial fibrillation of matched controls. Within the patients (85.7% suppressed), TSH was not associated with atrial fibrillation, while cumulative radioiodine dose was (sHR 1.04 per 50 mCi) [06.39].
- *Groningen, cardiovascular death:* within 524 patients, death rose 3.08-fold (1.32 to 7.21) per 10-fold fall in TSH. The abstract does not mention free T4 [06.38].
- *Turkey, vertebral fracture (990 patients, cross-sectional):* free T4 was an independent predictor of vertebral fracture (OR 1.051 per unit, 1.014 to 1.088), and bone density did not differ between TSH groups [06.37]. The model explained little (pseudo-R² 0.028), and the printed free T4 reference range has unit errors [06.37].

**Central hypothyroidism.**
- Here TSH is low or normal whatever the dose, so it is the natural test of whether a low TSH itself harms bone. In 74 hypopituitary patients on levothyroxine, vertebral fractures (31.1%) went with higher free T4, a higher dose (OR 4.01, 1.16 to 14.39) and untreated growth hormone deficiency (OR 4.27) [06.36]. The design is cross-sectional and small.
- I found no outcome study of heart events or atrial fibrillation in central hypothyroidism by free T4 level.

**Genetics.**
- *Ellervik 2019 (55,114 cases):* genetically higher free T4 within the range was not linked to atrial fibrillation (OR 1.01, 0.89 to 1.14). Genetically higher TSH was protective (OR 0.88 per SD, 0.84 to 0.92) and a genetically higher FT3:FT4 ratio raised risk (OR 1.33) [06.27].
- *Sterenborg 2024 (genetic association study in up to 271,040 euthyroid people of European ancestry; the causal analyses used outcome data from other studies):* TSH-associated loci "reflect the thyroid function determined by free T3". Genetically higher free T4 lowered atrial fibrillation risk and higher free T3 raised it. The text reports no significant causal association of any thyroid parameter with bone density or fracture, though one figure legend lists only bone density among the outcomes with no association [06.28].
- *TSH-variant scores also predict thyroid disease:* people in the top quarter of the TSH-raising score had OR 0.19 for hyperthyroidism against the bottom quarter [06.29]. Each SD of genetically higher TSH gave OR 0.57 for thyrotoxicosis [06.30].
- So "genetically low TSH" is not a test of low TSH with normal hormone. It tracks genetically higher T3-determined thyroid function and a greater tendency to an overactive thyroid; a consortium review calls TSH variants "a continuous scale ranging from pure set-point variants to variants with also minor effects on thyroid function" [06.28][06.29]. The genetic atrial fibrillation and heart-attack findings in thread 03 [03.41][03.43] fit a T3-mediated path as well as a TSH-mediated one.

### (d) Bone, recorded separately

**Points to thyroid hormone, not TSH.**
- After excluding people who later became overtly hyperthyroid, the TEARS fracture excess disappeared, and the authors judged the link "not related to TSH concentration" [06.13].
- In central hypothyroidism, fractures tracked free T4 and dose [06.36].
- In 1,278 euthyroid postmenopausal European women, higher free T4 and free T3 went with lower hip bone density. Nonvertebral fracture risk rose 20% with higher free T4 and 33% with higher free T3, and fell 35% with higher TSH. The abstract does not say whether these were modelled together [06.35].
- Genetically lower TSH did not change bone density (0.003 SD per SD, −0.053 to 0.048), and 706 TSH-receptor variants showed no link [06.32]. The 2024 analysis found no causal effect of any thyroid measure on bone density or fracture [06.28].
- In Aubert 2017, both free T4 (HR 1.22 per SD) and TSH 0.45 to 0.99 (HR 1.25) predicted hip fracture, but never in the same model [06.2].

**Points to TSH itself.**
- In 130 postmenopausal women with low bone density, vertebral fractures were more common in the lowest third of TSH (56.8% against 23.3% and 32.6%). After adjustment for age, bone density, BMI and free T4, the odds ratio was 2.8 (1.20 to 6.79) [06.34]. It is small and cross-sectional, and shares its first author with the rhTSH bone-marker study in thread 03 [03.23].
- In Rotterdam, carriers of the TSH-receptor Glu727 variant had 2.3% higher femoral neck bone density. The same paper found free T4 had "a much greater influence on bone than TSH" [06.33]. The larger genetic study [06.32] did not replicate a receptor-locus effect.

**Balance.** Most of the human evidence points to thyroid hormone as the main driver of fractures at a low TSH. Thread 03's mouse finding that hormone excess, not TSH deficiency, causes bone loss [03.22] agrees. A TSH-specific effect cannot be ruled out, but the only human data that support it are one cross-sectional study of 130 women and one candidate-gene result.

### (e) Contradictions as they stand
- **Harm at 0.1 to 0.4 with normal free T4: yes, for atrial fibrillation and probably for death in untreated people** [06.4][06.13][06.23]. This is as plain a finding as any in this thread.
- **On treatment, the same band shows little or no excess:** none in Tayside at 0.04 to 0.4 [06.8]; in UK THIN at 0.1 to 0.4, no excess death and lower heart failure (HR 0.76) and atrial fibrillation (HR 0.86) [06.7]; in US veterans at 0.1 to 0.5, heart failure AOR 0.94 [06.42] but cardiovascular death HR 1.13 [06.6]. So a low TSH from an overactive gland looks worse than the same TSH reached with levothyroxine. Untested explanations include relatively more T3 from autonomous tissue, lower free T3 at a given TSH on levothyroxine [06.16], and healthier people in treated cohorts.
- **Low-normal TSH (0.45 to about 1):** more hip fracture [06.2] and the lowest fifth's small mortality excess [06.1], neither adjusted for free T4 or T3. Coronary disease was not associated [06.3].
- **Free T4 against TSH:** observationally, free T4 inside the range tracks atrial fibrillation better than TSH does [06.25][06.26]. Genetically, higher free T4 *lowers* atrial fibrillation risk while higher free T3 raises it [06.28]. If T3 is the active driver, studies that use free T4 alone as the "hormone" measure cannot settle the question in either direction.

## What this means for the person's situation
This section is written for any adult who has been told their TSH is low, whether or not they take thyroid hormone. It gives evidence and questions for a doctor, not advice.

- **A single low TSH is not a diagnosis.** An untreated low but detectable TSH (0.1 to 0.4) often returns to normal: in 76% within a year in one study of people over 60 [06.11], and in 25% to 50% overall [06.9]. The European guideline expects a repeat test, and says T3 may be measured as well [06.9]. Worth asking: has it been repeated, and was T3 measured?
- **An undetectable TSH (below 0.01 or 0.03, depending on the assay) when not on thyroid hormone usually means an overactive thyroid, even when free T4 is normal.** It tends to persist [06.11], progresses to overt disease in 5% to 8% a year [06.9], and carries the clearest risks (thread 04 [04.53][04.54]). The other causes to rule out are illness, high-dose steroids, dopamine, pituitary disease and rare assay artefacts [06.9][06.10][06.20].
- **"Normal free hormones" does not mean "no excess".** People with a low TSH and a normal free T4 had a higher free T4 on average than people with a normal TSH (16.89 against 15.05 pmol/L, in groups split at a TSH of 0.2) [06.23], and their atrial fibrillation risk was raised even when T3 was checked [06.4][06.13]. The reassuring case would be a low TSH with a mid-range free T3, and that case has almost no outcome research behind it.
- **On levothyroxine,** the risks are concentrated at a TSH below about 0.03 to 0.1 (thread 04) [06.6][06.8]. Between about 0.1 and 0.4 to 0.5, treated-patient data show little or no excess, and sometimes less heart failure [06.7][06.8][06.42]. One marker study of 46 people without a thyroid suggests that a TSH above 0.03 and up to 0.3, with free T3 at their own pre-surgery level, may be closest to their pre-surgery state [06.17]. None of this is outcome evidence of safety, and older people and anyone with heart disease are at higher risk from excess thyroid hormone (thread 04).
- **On liothyronine, combination therapy or desiccated thyroid,** free T4 is low or undetectable by design and cannot show over-replacement, and T3 is higher on these regimens [06.18]. The only outcome data by TSH band are observational, were not analysed by T3, and favour T3-containing regimens at a low TSH [06.40]. Useful questions for a doctor: when was the blood drawn relative to the last dose, and what was the T3?
- **Bone.** For fracture, the evidence points more to free T4 and T3 levels, and to how long the TSH has been low, than to the TSH number itself [06.13][06.35][06.36] (duration: thread 04 [04.55]).
- **The cholesterol question.** Thyroid-related raised LDL comes with a *high* TSH or a low free T4 (thread 01). Pushing TSH low on thyroid hormone in order to lower cholesterol trades a small lipid change for the atrial fibrillation and fracture risks described here and in thread 04.

**Points that depend on an unconfirmed assumption.**
- US guidelines are treated as primary (assumption). I could not read the 2016 American Thyroid Association guideline in full. The TSH bands, causes and progression rates above come from the European guideline [06.9], so US wording may differ.
- "Normal" here means inside each laboratory's reference range. Free T4 ranges differ between laboratories: one study's upper limit was 1.7 ng/dL, about 22 pmol/L [06.16], and another's was 25 pmol/L [06.15]. So whether a given result counts as "raised" depends on the laboratory.
- If "historical" practice (symptom-titrated or desiccated thyroid dosing, thread 02) is the reader's frame, T3 exposure is the main unmeasured factor. None of the treated-patient outcome studies found here measured T3 [06.6][06.7][06.8][06.40][06.42].

## Load-bearing claims
- C06.1: Xu 2023 (134,346 adults) excluded no individual adult for thyroid status: it excluded only cohorts made up solely of pregnant women, people with overt thyroid disease or people with cardiovascular disease, plus participants under 18. Its analysed sample included 5,722 (4.6%) thyroid-medication users at baseline, excluded only in a sensitivity analysis, and T3 was not included because most cohorts lacked it. Restricting to euthyroid participants "showed similar results to our main analyses". [06.1]
- C06.2: In 52,674 people not taking thyroxine or antithyroid drugs, with subclinical hyperthyroidism defined as a low TSH with a normal free T4 (350 people with a low TSH and no free T4 value stayed in the main analysis), TSH 0.10 to 0.44 mIU/L carried total mortality HR 1.23 (1.04 to 1.45) and atrial fibrillation HR 1.63 (1.10 to 2.41). After excluding 143 people with abnormal T3 (measured in 5 of 10 cohorts), subclinical hyperthyroidism carried atrial fibrillation HR 1.64 (1.14 to 2.35) and total mortality HR 1.20 (1.01 to 1.43). [06.4]
- C06.3: In Tayside, a low TSH on two tests at least 4 months apart, with normal free or total T4 and normal total T3, carried cardiovascular morbidity HR 1.36 (1.19 to 1.57) and dysrhythmia HR 1.39 (1.02 to 1.90) after excluding people who developed overt hyperthyroidism; the abstract reports no association with fracture in that analysis. [06.13]
- C06.4: Of 207 athyreotic patients on levothyroxine with TSH below 0.5 mIU/L, 70.5% had free T4 above range but 91.3% had free T3 within range. [06.16]

## Confidence Notes
- **Firm:**
  - Undetectable TSH in hyperthyroidism against detectable low TSH in illness [06.10].
  - The course of an untreated low TSH: grade 1 often normalises, grade 2 tends to persist or progress [06.9][06.11][06.12][06.14].
  - On levothyroxine, a low TSH usually comes with a normal free T3 [06.15][06.16].
  - What each key cohort excluded and adjusted for: read in full for six of the eight cohorts [06.1]–[06.7].
- **Moderately firm:** excess atrial fibrillation at a low TSH with a normal free T4. It appears in four independent populations (pooled cohorts, Tayside, Copenhagen, US older adults) [06.4][06.13][06.23][06.24].
- **Thin:**
  - Every study that tested a normal T3: T3 was measured in only half the pooled cohorts [06.4], and as total T3 in Tayside [06.13].
  - The Tayside band figures come second-hand from [06.4] and do not match the Tayside abstract.
  - The tissue-marker study that supports a mildly suppressed TSH on levothyroxine has 133 patients, one centre and no outcomes [06.17].
  - The bone evidence for a TSH-specific effect rests on 130 women [06.34] and one candidate-gene result [06.33].
- **The circularity the brief warned about is real:**
  - "Subclinical hyperthyroidism" is defined by a low TSH, so studies that "require normal free hormones" still pick out people with free T4 near the top of the range [06.23].
  - Free T3 is rarely measured: Xu 2023 left T3 out [06.1], the English primary-care dataset could not analyse it [06.15], and only one hospital, in Japan, routinely uses it to dose [06.16].
  - No treated-patient outcome cohort cross-classified TSH by free T4 or T3 [06.6][06.7][06.8][06.42].
- **Confounding in treated cohorts:**
  - Two treated cohorts excluded thyroid cancer: [06.6] and [06.40]. Thayakaran [06.7] did not state it and could not separate causes. Flynn [06.8] could not be checked.
  - A low TSH on treatment also marks weight loss, frailty and patient-requested higher doses (thread 04).
  - The TriNetX regimen comparison [06.40] could reflect who is prescribed T3 rather than what T3 does.
- **Interests:**
  - [06.40]: the paper's disclosure lists one author as a consultant for AbbVie, Acella, Aligos and Synthonics; AbbVie distributes Armour Thyroid, a desiccated thyroid product (DailyMed label, thread 02 [02.40]). Found by the report writer.
  - [06.31]: authors declared lipid-drug industry ties, already noted in thread 03.
  - [06.15]: no funding.
  - Several Thyroid Studies Collaboration authors have levothyroxine-maker ties, noted in thread 03.
- **Read levels:**
  - Four sources were read in full through a fetch tool that summarises [06.2][06.9][06.21][06.40]. Their figures were cross-checked against abstracts where possible.
  - For the two guidelines, only the first 100,000 characters were processed [06.9][06.21].
  - Many supporting studies were read in abstract only.
- No web page or document contained instructions addressed to an automated reader.

## Gaps
- **Looked for and not found:**
  - An outcome study (heart events, atrial fibrillation, fracture, bone density or death) in people on thyroid hormone with a low TSH and *both* free T4 and free T3 in range. Searched:
    - PubMed for "low TSH" or "suppressed TSH" with "normal free T4" and levothyroxine with fracture, atrial fibrillation, mortality or bone density: 0 results.
    - PubMed for levothyroxine with "discordant" TSH and free T4: 13 results, none with outcomes.
    - Europe PMC for treated cohorts analysed "independent of TSH" or "adjusted for TSH".
    - Web searches for low TSH with normal free T3 and bone or atrial fibrillation outcomes.
  - Outcome studies of liothyronine or desiccated thyroid users analysed by both TSH and T3. The only TSH-banded study had no T3 [06.40], and the Swedish and Scottish cohorts had no TSH bands in what I read [06.41].
  - Heart, rhythm or fracture outcomes in central hypothyroidism by free T4. Only one small cross-sectional fracture study was found [06.36].
  - Thyroid cancer survivors on suppression analysed by free T4 or free T3 for atrial fibrillation or heart events. One fracture study used free T4 [06.37].
  - A large cohort in which TSH was entered alongside free T4 and free T3 in one model for any outcome. The only free-T4-adjusted TSH result found was the 130-woman fracture study [06.34].
- **Not obtained:**
  - The Xu 2023 appendix (euthyroid-only and medication-excluded figures, and per-cohort 20th-percentile TSH values). The PMC supplement link served no content.
  - The full text of the 2016 American Thyroid Association hyperthyroidism guideline (publisher returned 403).
  - The full texts of Flynn 2010 and the TEARS morbidity paper (paywalled).
  - The Blum 2015 eTables.
- **Under way:** a Dutch primary-care study of outcomes in subclinical hyperthyroidism (ISRCTN15496928), registered May 2023, has no results yet [06.43].
- **Not studied at all, and why:**
  - No trial has randomised treated patients to a TSH target of 0.1 to 0.4 against 1 to 2.5 while measuring free T3, for atrial fibrillation or bone outcomes.
  - No trial has compared levothyroxine with T3-containing therapy at matched free T3 for outcomes.
  - Likely reasons: the drugs are off-patent, so there is no sponsor (brief distortion 1); guidelines monitor by TSH alone, so few datasets hold T3 (distortion 2); and the event rates need thousands of participants.
- **What would settle the question:**
  - Cheapest: an individual-participant reanalysis of the Thyroid Studies Collaboration cohorts that measured T3, cross-classifying TSH band by free T4 tertile and T3, for atrial fibrillation and fracture.
  - Definitive: a randomised trial in athyreotic adults on levothyroxine comparing a TSH target of 0.1 to 0.4 with 1 to 2.5, with patch-monitored atrial fibrillation and bone density as outcomes.

# Thyroid function and cholesterol: what the evidence supports before a cholesterol-lowering drug

## Research Question

The brief asked three questions about adults:

1. How does thyroid function affect cholesterol, and how large is the effect?
2. Should doctors bring thyroid function to a healthy level before prescribing cholesterol-lowering drugs, where "healthy" is judged by the standard used before the modern TSH test (diagnosis by symptoms and signs) and not only by today's TSH reference ranges?
3. Is TSH itself harmful, for example by driving inflammation, so that keeping it lower than current practice does would be better?

US guidelines come first, with UK and European ones for comparison. Whether lowering cholesterol reduces deaths belongs to a companion review; children and pregnancy are out of scope.

Terms: TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) is the pituitary signal that rises when the thyroid is underactive, and the blood test now used to diagnose it. T4 (thyroxine) is the main thyroid hormone; levothyroxine is the T4 tablet. Overt hypothyroidism is a high TSH with a low T4; subclinical hypothyroidism is a raised TSH with a normal T4. LDL cholesterol is what statins are prescribed to lower; apoB is a related measure. A hazard ratio (HR) of 1.25 means a 25% higher rate of the outcome.

## Summary

Thyroid function moves cholesterol a lot only when the thyroid has clearly failed: treating overt hypothyroidism lowers LDL cholesterol by a pooled 41.1 mg/dL from a mean of 169 mg/dL, treating a mildly raised TSH lowers it by about 8.5 mg/dL in randomised trials (from a review's abstract) and by nothing measurable in the two blinded trials in older adults, and inside the normal TSH range the gap between low and high is about 2 mg/dL [01.1][01.7][01.9][01.19]. A TSH test before a cholesterol-lowering drug is therefore worth asking for, and treating a clearly underactive thyroid first fits the older and the modern standard alike: the test is listed at $16.80, the only practice study found it done in 49.5% of patients, and the Endocrine Society recommends "ruling out hypothyroidism as the cause of the hyperlipidemia before treatment with lipid-lowering medications" [05.45][05.29][05.5]. Treating by symptoms when TSH is normal is unproven, not disproven: the 1930s standard itself required a supporting test, three small blinded trials found thyroid hormone no better than placebo, and none counted heart attacks or deaths [02.2][04.19][04.20][04.21]. On question 3, no study in people shows that TSH itself causes inflammation or artery disease: the laboratory case comes mainly from one group, its liver-cell experiments used 1 and 4 µM of TSH in a tissue with 0.4 units of TSH-receptor RNA against 257 in the thyroid, and TSH injected into people on fixed thyroid hormone raised total cholesterol from 192 to 207 mg/dL over 4 days, with contradictory inflammation results [03.2][03.19][03.15][03.12][03.13]. The idea that a lower TSH is better runs against the largest human datasets, in which TSH behaves as a marker of thyroid status: across 134,346 adults the lowest risk of cardiovascular events and death was at a TSH of about 1.90 to 2.90 mIU/L, the lowest fifth of TSH had a 9% higher death rate (adjusted for age and sex only), a low-normal TSH went with more hip fractures, and genetically lower TSH with more heart attacks [03.33][03.34][03.43].

## What this means for you

This section is for any adult offered a cholesterol-lowering drug, or wondering whether an underactive thyroid has been ruled out. It gives evidence and questions to take to a doctor.

It is not individual medical advice. Nothing here is a reason to start, stop or change a medicine on your own.

- **Ask whether your TSH has been measured, and what it was.** The test is listed at $16.80 (Kentucky Medicaid 2026 schedule) [05.45], and it may not have been done: in the only practice study found, 49.5% of 8,795 US adults with newly found high cholesterol had it checked within about 6 months, and of those tested 5.2% had a raised TSH and 1.7% a TSH above 10 mIU/L [05.29]. The Endocrine Society recommends ruling hypothyroidism out before any lipid-lowering drug [05.5]. The 2026 ACC/AHA guideline does not recommend a TSH before every statin, only an evaluation of other causes in three situations such as an LDL of 190 mg/dL or more [05.1]. In England, NICE has since 2023 asked for TSH only "in people with symptoms of underactive or overactive thyroid" [05.12].

- **If the TSH is high and T4 is low (overt hypothyroidism), ask whether to treat the thyroid and recheck cholesterol before deciding on the drug.** Treatment lowers LDL by a pooled 41.1 mg/dL from a mean of 169 mg/dL, in uncontrolled before-and-after studies [01.1], and within weeks [01.25]. The Endocrine Society suggests waiting until thyroid tests are normal before treating cholesterol, a conditional suggestion on very low-quality evidence [05.5], and the UK rosuvastatin label says hypothyroidism causing raised cholesterol "should be treated prior to initiating therapy" [05.27]. For people who already have cardiovascular disease, NICE says to "Treat comorbidities and secondary causes of dyslipidaemia at the same time as starting statin treatment" [05.12].

- **If the TSH is mildly raised and T4 is normal (subclinical hypothyroidism), ask for a repeat test, how high the TSH is, and what treating it would change.** Such results often do not last: of 2,647 adults aged 65 or over re-tested for the TRUST trial after an earlier raised TSH, 62% were back below 4.60 mIU/L [04.1]. The level matters. In 11 cohorts (55,287 adults, with coronary events recorded in 25,977 from 7 of them), coronary events were no more frequent at a TSH of 4.5 to 6.9 mIU/L (HR 1.00) and clearly more frequent at 10 to 19.9 (HR 1.89, 95% CI 1.28 to 2.80); coronary deaths were raised from 7.0 upward (HR 1.42 at 7.0 to 9.9, 1.58 at 10 to 19.9) (abstract) [03.29]. Treatment is unlikely to replace a cholesterol drug, since LDL fell by about 8.5 mg/dL in randomised trials (abstract) [01.7][01.9], and whether it prevents heart attacks is unknown: the only placebo-controlled trials that counted them saw 44 cardiovascular events in 842 people aged 65 or over [04.5].

- **If the TSH is normal and you have symptoms, know what has and has not been tested.** Within the normal range thyroid status explains very little of a raised LDL: 117.0 against 119.0 mg/dL between the lower half and the top tenth of TSH in 24,765 adults [01.19]. Three small blinded trials (18, 20 and 22 patients; main comparisons of 4 to 12 weeks) found thyroid hormone no better than placebo for symptoms, and none counted heart events [04.19][04.20][04.21]. No trial has tested months of dose adjustment or measured its safety, and current guidelines, which define the disease by TSH, offer no such route [05.50].

- **If you have read that TSH is inflammatory and should be kept low, know that the human evidence points the other way.** TSH has no demonstrated harm of its own in people [03.12][03.13][03.15]. In 55,412 people with a TSH inside the reference range, each 1 mIU/L higher TSH carried no change in coronary death (HR 0.97, 0.90 to 1.04) [03.28]. A low-normal TSH of 0.45 to 0.99 went with more hip fractures than 3.50 to 4.49 (HR 1.25, 1.05 to 1.49; 56,835 people) [03.34], and in 105,224 Copenhagen adults the genetic risk estimate for heart attack was 1.35 per standard deviation of lower TSH [03.43]. None tested a treatment target, and none supports taking thyroid hormone to lower a normal TSH.

- **If you take thyroid hormone, or are offered it, ask what TSH is being aimed for and how over-replacement will be caught.** Among 705,307 US veterans on thyroid hormone, cardiovascular death was 39% more frequent at a TSH below 0.1, 13% more frequent at 0.1 to 0.5, and 2.67 times as frequent above 20 [04.49]. In a Scottish cohort, read in abstract, a TSH of 0.03 or below went with about twice the fracture rate (HR 2.02), and 0.04 to 0.4 showed no significant excess [04.47]. In the general population the lowest risk was at a TSH of about 1.90 to 2.90 mIU/L [03.33], and the 2014 American Thyroid Association guideline finds "insufficient evidence of benefit" for aiming treated patients with raised cholesterol at a low-normal TSH [05.50]. These figures are observational and no trial has compared targets, but they give no reason to aim low and some reason to avoid a suppressed TSH.

- **Thyroid hormone is not a cholesterol drug for people whose thyroid tests are normal.** It did not change cholesterol in the one such trial [01.35], and a thyroid drug tried as a cholesterol treatment at a TSH-suppressing dose was stopped in late 1971 for excess deaths [04.25][04.29].

**What would change this.** A placebo-controlled trial counting heart events in adults under 65 with a mildly raised TSH, or in people treated by symptoms, would; on 21 November 2026 none was registered [04.74][04.75]. So would a trial of a lower against a higher TSH target, or of months of TSH injections against placebo; the searches found neither [04.23][03.15]. So would facts a general report cannot know: existing cardiovascular disease, an LDL of 190 mg/dL or more, or an age under 65, since the event trials enrolled only older people [04.5].

**Assumptions to discount if they are wrong.** The brief assumed a US reader; in the UK routine TSH testing before a statin is no longer standard [05.12]. It took "historical" to mean diagnosis by symptoms and signs; the documented 1938 standard also required signs and a supporting test [02.2], and if it means Broda Barnes's symptoms-plus-temperature method, the blinded trials above are the nearest controlled test of it [02.25]. If "a lower TSH" means treating a TSH of 10 or more, the coronary data support it; if it means pushing TSH into the low-normal range, they do not [03.29][03.33].

## How much thyroid function moves cholesterol

Treating overt hypothyroidism with levothyroxine lowers LDL cholesterol by a pooled 41.1 mg/dL (95% confidence interval 35.7 to 46.5) from a mean baseline of 169 mg/dL, total cholesterol by 58.4 mg/dL and apoB by 34.0 mg/dL [01.1]. The figures are uncontrolled changes from baseline in 2,305 participants, and the studies disagree widely (I² above 87%), so the direction is firmer than the size [01.1]. A fall of 41 from 169 is about a quarter (this report's arithmetic), close to what a low-intensity statin does [01.41]. Overt hypothyroidism is uncommon (0.4% of 25,862 Colorado adults), a major cause of raised cholesterol for the person who has it and a minor one across the population [01.2].

A cleaner test comes from adults who had their thyroid removed for cancer and stopped thyroid hormone before radioiodine treatment, each acting as their own control. Four weeks without thyroid hormone raised LDL cholesterol by 46 and 70 mg/dL (47% and 58%) and apoB by 46% to 48% in two cohorts of 48 and 52 adults (this report's arithmetic from the reported means and medians) [01.25][01.27]. The rise reversed within 4 weeks of restarting in one of them [01.25]. The mechanism is the liver's clearance of LDL, which depends on thyroid hormone (abstract) [01.29]; whether TSH adds an effect of its own is taken up below.

In subclinical hypothyroidism the effect is small and shrinks as study quality rises. With every study design pooled, LDL falls by 11.1 mg/dL, but 143 of that review's 166 studies were not randomised [01.1]. In randomised trials the pooled effect is −0.22 mmol/L (about −8.5 mg/dL; 12 trials, 940 participants), falling to −0.09 mmol/L in trials longer than 6 months; these figures come from the review's abstract [01.7]. A post-hoc pooled analysis of the two double-blind trials in older adults (286 adults aged 65 or over, median TSH 6.44 mIU/L, 12 months) found −0.07 mmol/L (95% CI −0.15 to 0.02), not significant [01.9]. Only 27 participants had a TSH of 10 or more, and uncontrolled studies overstate the effect because a mildly raised TSH often settles unaided [01.9]. No large blinded trial with LDL as its main outcome has tested adults under 65, or anyone with a TSH of 10 to 20.

Inside the TSH reference range the effect on LDL is causal but tiny: 0.026 to 0.032 standard deviations of LDL per standard deviation of TSH in two Mendelian randomisation studies, which use inherited gene variants to test cause and effect (about 1 mg/dL by this report's conversion; both read in abstract) [01.21][01.22], and 117.0 against 119.0 mg/dL between the lower half and the top tenth of TSH in 24,765 adults without overt thyroid disease, 325 of whom had a TSH above 5.0 mIU/L [01.19]. The gene variants shift the whole pituitary-thyroid set point, so this "TSH effect" cannot be told apart from a thyroid hormone effect.

The only randomised trial found that enrolled people by hypothyroid symptoms with normal thyroid tests (22 patients, 100 µg thyroxine for 12 weeks, double-blind crossover) showed no change in total cholesterol: difference 0.05 mmol/L (95% CI −0.27 to 0.37), with TSH 0.66 mU/L on thyroxine against 1.77 on placebo [01.35]. It measured total cholesterol only and is too small to rule out a fall of about 10 mg/dL [01.35].

## What "healthy by the older standard" meant

There were two pre-TSH standards, and they differ about a hundredfold in how many people they class as hypothyroid. The standard at a leading US clinic about 1938 was narrow (calling it the mainstream standard is this report's characterisation): at Massachusetts General Hospital, myxoedema, the full picture of thyroid failure, was diagnosed on cardinal symptoms and signs supported by a low basal metabolic rate (BMR), and a low BMR alone was not an indication for thyroid [02.2]. Diagnosed hypothyroidism was about 0.2% of adults in a north-east England survey published in 1972, whose authors inferred a large undiagnosed pool [02.6].

The expansive standard is Broda Barnes's: a waking armpit temperature below 97.8°F plus any of a long list of complaints [02.25]. His 40% prevalence is a stated conviction, and his normal range of 97.8 to 98.2°F rests on his own unreferenced readings; apart from his 1942 report comparing temperature with basal metabolism in 1,000 students, which was not opened, the search found no published validation against a thyroid measure [02.25].

Symptoms alone separate poorly, and the older clinicians said so. In Wayne's Glasgow series of 1959, tiredness was reported by 98% of proved hypothyroid patients, 88% of euthyroid suspects (people referred who turned out not to have it) and 42% of healthy controls [02.4]. In 25,862 Colorado adults, single-symptom sensitivity was 2.9% to 28.3%, measured against TSH [02.16]. Wayne warned against diagnosing mild hypothyroidism from clinical features alone and used objective support, including serum cholesterol, which was above 300 mg/100 ml in 81% of his hypothyroid cases [02.4].

One pre-TSH series tests question 2 directly. In the 1939 Yale series, all 16 patients who recovered on desiccated thyroid (dried animal thyroid gland) had an untreated serum cholesterol of 315 to 741 mg%, and one of the 16 was very slow to recover. The 13 patients with a low metabolic rate or hypothyroid-type symptoms and lower cholesterol (149 to 300 mg%) showed little or no improvement; in 8 of the 12 with data the cholesterol fall was only 11 to 65 mg%, and in 3 of those followed closely it was transient [01.37]. The result is partly circular, since the series defined hypothyroidism in part by cholesterol and by response to thyroid [01.37].

The usual dose at Massachusetts General about 1938, 1 to 3 grains of desiccated thyroid (most 1½), is within today's labelled range; the clearly higher doses belong to the 1950s and 1960s [02.2][02.40].

The move to TSH rested on small biochemical studies (32 patients in 1965, 22 in 1973, 19 in 1987) and no outcome trial that the search could find [02.10][02.8][02.11]. The 2012 US guideline's Grade A rule against clinical scoring systems rests on three studies it grades as level 3, at least two of which classified patients by TSH [02.23]. Modern studies judge symptoms against TSH, so none can show whether TSH misses disease. The TSH reference range is itself a statistical convention, the 2.5th to 97.5th percentiles, set without regard to outcomes; the largest outcome study of thyroid function set out to define a range by risk instead [03.33].

Today's practice treats more people than the narrow older standard did. US retail thyroid prescriptions peaked at about 23 million in 1966 (read off a reproduced chart) [02.13][02.14]; 8.0% of US adults reported thyroid hormone use in 2016 (abstract) [02.33]; and of 58,706 insured US adults who started levothyroxine in 2008 to 2018 with both TSH and T4 on record, 30.5% had normal results [02.34].

## What thyroid treatment does to heart events and deaths, and what too much does

For overt hypothyroidism no trial has compared treatment with none, since withholding it would be unethical. In a Danish register, read in abstract, untreated hypothyroid people had higher mortality than people with normal tests (HR 1.46; 95% CI 1.26 to 1.69) and treated people did not [04.50].

For subclinical hypothyroidism, the only placebo-controlled trials of levothyroxine that counted events pooled 842 people aged 65 or over (mean TSH 6.4) for a median 17 months: 44 cardiovascular events (HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.41 to 1.35) and 21 deaths (HR 1.28, 0.54 to 3.03) [04.5]. The larger trial, TRUST, was cut from a planned 3,000 participants and its report says it cannot exclude protection or harm [04.3][04.1]. Its participants had mild disease, and 62% of the 2,647 people re-tested for entry no longer had a raised TSH [04.1]. The results do not cover younger adults, marked symptoms or a TSH above 10, the band where untreated coronary risk is clearly raised [03.29].

The search found no placebo-controlled trial that counted heart events or deaths for thyroid hormone in overt hypothyroidism, in adults under 65 with subclinical hypothyroidism, or in people with symptoms and a normal TSH [04.7][04.76]. On 21 November 2026 the only registered trials with events as their primary outcome were open-label: NCT01848171 (700 planned, ages 40 to 75; status unknown, no results) and NCT07380854 (338 planned, in bypass surgery) [04.74][04.75].

Observational studies, read in abstract, associate levothyroxine for subclinical hypothyroidism with fewer events in younger adults (HR 0.61 at ages 40 to 70; pooled mortality relative risk 0.50 under 65 to 70) but not in adults over 65 to 70 [04.9][04.12]. Two analyses of one UK database gave mortality HRs of 1.17 and 0.71, both with fewer cardiovascular events [04.14][04.15].

In symptom-defined patients, three blinded placebo-controlled studies (1960, n = 18; 1960, n = 20; 2001, n = 22 completing) found thyroid hormone no better than placebo [04.20][04.21][04.19]. The 1960 studies chose patients with a low basal metabolic rate and normal thyroid tests of the time (one allocated by rotation, one only partly blinded), the 2001 trial patients with tests in the reference range; the main comparisons ran 4 to 12 weeks, and none counted events. Every blinded test, including the two that predate TSH, shows a large placebo response and no hormone effect; but about 60 patients is very few, and no trial tested dose adjustment to relief over months. Barnes's uncontrolled 1959 series of 80 patients reported a cholesterol fall and no data on arterial disease [04.35].

The Coronary Drug Project gave dextrothyroxine 6 mg a day to 1,110 men after a heart attack (placebo 2,789) and stopped the arm in late 1971 for excess deaths [04.25]. The dose was about 1.7 times the 3.5 mg that suppresses TSH (a mean from 6 patients), and the marketed tablets later assayed at 0.50% to 2.30% levothyroxine, so in this report's reading the arm tested thyroid excess, not replacement (both papers read in abstract) [04.29][04.30].

The firmest finding concerns over-replacement. On treatment, cardiovascular death was HR 1.39 at a TSH below 0.1, 1.13 at 0.1 to 0.5 and 2.67 at a TSH above 20 (705,307 US veterans) [04.49]; in Tayside, Scotland, a TSH of 0.03 or below carried HR 1.37 for cardiovascular disease and 2.02 for fracture, while 0.04 to 0.4 showed no significant excess (abstract) [04.47]. A UK cohort of 162,369 found deaths raised below 0.1 (HR 1.18) and no link with atrial fibrillation or fracture, so the sources disagree on some outcomes [04.48]. In 524 thyroid cancer survivors kept on suppressive doses, cardiovascular death rose 3.08-fold (1.32 to 7.21) for each tenfold fall in TSH (abstract) [03.45]. All are observational: a low TSH on treatment also marks weight loss, frailty and deliberate suppression after thyroid cancer, and in each design it comes with excess thyroid hormone, so they cannot say which of the two does the harm.

## Whether TSH itself does harm, and which TSH level goes with the best outcomes

Testing whether TSH itself does harm needs evidence that separates it from thyroid hormone, since a high TSH almost always means low thyroid hormone. There are three kinds, and they point different ways [03.1][03.15][03.33].

### Cells and animals: a real signal, mostly from one laboratory

A group at Shandong Provincial Hospital in Jinan, China, has reported that TSH acting on liver TSH receptors raises HMG-CoA reductase, the cholesterol-making enzyme that statins block, in rats held on fixed thyroid hormone (abstract) [03.1] and in liver cells treated with bovine TSH at 1 and 4 µM for 48 hours; mice lacking the receptor (5 against 4) had lower total cholesterol, 1.90 against 2.93 mmol/L (full text) [03.2]. The group also reported that TSH suppresses bile-acid synthesis, the body's main route for disposing of cholesterol (abstract) [03.3]. Its 2019 paper found that atherosclerosis-prone mice lacking the TSH receptor developed less atherosclerosis and vessel inflammation while dietary thyroxine kept their T3, T4 and TSH matched to controls, and that TSH at 0.2 to 5 ng/mL switched on inflammatory pathways in macrophages (full text; a 2022 correction changed a methods detail only) [03.6][03.7]. Other groups reported related effects: TSH raised PCSK9 in a liver-cancer cell line in Nanjing [03.5], and release of the inflammatory signal IL-6 from mouse and human fat cells in Ottawa (abstracts) [03.9][03.10][03.11].

Three things limit what this shows about people. The liver doses were far above blood levels, where even the standard therapeutic TSH injection peaks at an average 116 mU/L (label, snippet only) [03.46]; the thread's rough conversion, from no source, puts 1 µM tens of thousands of times above a normal TSH, while the macrophage doses were nearer the physiological range [03.6]. The receptor is scarce where the effects are claimed: 0.4 nTPM of TSH-receptor RNA in human liver against 257 in thyroid, 0.5 in fat and 0.3 in heart muscle [03.19], and an independent review calls the physiological importance of receptors outside the thyroid "in many cases" a subject of controversy (abstract) [03.20]. And the liver, bile-acid and macrophage-atherosclerosis findings come from the Jinan group alone, which also produced the supporting human cross-sectional studies [03.4][03.8]; the thread found no independent laboratory that had reproduced them. This is the concentration in a few groups, with circular validation, that the bias note predicted.

A parallel claim that TSH protects bone, from mice [03.21], did not survive independent and genetic tests (abstracts) [03.22][03.24].

### People with thyroid hormone held fixed: small, brief and inconsistent

The cleanest human test gives injected TSH to thyroid cancer survivors on a steady levothyroxine dose, so that TSH rises for a few days while T4 does not. In 27 women, TSH went from 0.43 to 12.3 mU/L and total cholesterol from 192 to 207 mg/dL over 4 days, with LDL 115 to 126 mg/dL (p = 0.066) (full text) [03.15]. In an independent study of 82 patients, apoB went from 0.90 to 0.92 g/L and triglycerides rose, changes that tracked a fall in T3, which the authors read as TSH acting on thyroid hormone metabolism rather than on the liver (abstract) [03.16]. On inflammation and blood vessels the studies contradict each other: in 24 patients artery dilation fell and inflammatory markers rose [03.12], and in 20 adhesion molecules rose at TSH levels the authors called "supraphysiological" [03.14], while in 34 dilation improved and IL-6 and hs-CRP did not change [03.13]. All were read as abstracts, none had a placebo injection, and none lasted beyond days.

Two natural experiments point to thyroid hormone, not TSH, as the driver. When thyroid hormone was withdrawn for four weeks and TSH rose from 0.14 to 66.9 mU/L, hs-CRP fell from 0.17 to 0.08 mg/L [01.25], and it was unchanged at a TSH near 99 in a second cohort [01.27]. When thyroid hormone is low because the pituitary has failed, so that TSH is not raised, raised lipids were still common at the Mayo Clinic and improved with thyroid hormone (abstract) [03.17]; in 90 patients with pituitary hypothyroidism, those with a low TSH had low HDL more often than those with a normal TSH (abstract) [03.18]. Other pituitary deficiencies confound these. Genetically higher TSH did not raise CRP, while higher CRP raised TSH (abstract) [03.25], so a raised CRP in hypothyroidism [03.26] looks like a marker of the hypothyroid state, not of TSH driving inflammation.

### Populations: risk is lowest in the middle of the range

The largest analysis pooled 134,346 adults from 26 cohorts. The lowest risk of cardiovascular events and death was at the 60th to 80th percentiles of TSH (median 1.90 to 2.90 mIU/L), and against that band the lowest fifth of TSH had HR 1.09 (1.05 to 1.14) for death from any cause, adjusted for age and sex only [03.33]. Free T4 gave the clearer signal, with the most risk in the top fifth (HR 1.34 for death) [03.33]. The checker confirmed these figures in the full manuscript.

Within the reference range TSH showed no link with coronary disease: in 55,412 people from 14 cohorts, each 1 mIU/L higher TSH carried HR 0.97 (0.90 to 1.04) for coronary death and 1.00 (0.97 to 1.03) for coronary events (abstract; the checker confirmed the figures in the full text) [03.28]. One cohort in that pool, HUNT in Norway, had found more coronary death in women at 2.5 to 3.5 mIU/L than at 0.50 to 1.4 (HR 1.69, 1.14 to 2.52), the strongest single result for a lower target, and the pooled analysis did not confirm it [03.27][03.28].

The low end of the range carried costs. Hip fracture was more common at a TSH of 0.45 to 0.99 than at 3.50 to 4.49 (HR 1.25, 1.05 to 1.49; 56,835 people) [03.34], and atrial fibrillation tracked free T4 rather than TSH [03.35]. In genetic studies, lower TSH went with more atrial fibrillation [03.41][03.42]; in 105,224 Copenhagen adults the genetic risk estimate per standard deviation of lower TSH was 1.35 (1.06 to 1.71) for heart attack and 1.28 (1.08 to 1.52) for atrial fibrillation, though the abstract does not say whether these are odds ratios or how many people the genetic analysis used [03.43]; and genetically higher TSH went with slightly lower all-cause mortality in UK Biobank [03.44]. All were read as abstracts.

Single cohorts on mortality conflict, and age explains much of it: US NHANES III found death raised at both ends of the range [03.30], while in older US adults, in Rotterdam (from age 50) and among people aged 85, higher TSH went with longer life, and centenarians and their children had higher TSH (abstracts) [03.31][03.32][03.36][03.37][03.38][03.39].

All of these population findings treat TSH as a marker of thyroid status, and none shows it to be a cause of harm. They still answer the "lower is better" claim, which is about where a TSH should sit: at the bottom of the range it goes with slightly more death, more hip fracture and more atrial fibrillation than in the middle [03.33][03.34][03.43].

## What guidelines and labels ask for before a cholesterol drug

The Endocrine Society guideline (2020) recommends "ruling out hypothyroidism as the cause of the hyperlipidemia before treatment with lipid-lowering medications" (recommendation 6.1, strong, high-quality evidence) and suggests not treating hyperlipidaemia in overt hypothyroidism until euthyroid, meaning thyroid tests are normal (recommendation 6.3, conditional, very low quality) [05.5]. Its own text says the effect of thyroxine on long-term cardiovascular outcomes "is unknown" [05.5].

The main US cholesterol guideline is narrower. The 2026 ACC/AHA guideline, which replaced the 2018 one on 13 March 2026, tells clinicians to exclude or evaluate secondary causes, hypothyroidism among them, in three situations: LDL cholesterol of 190 mg/dL or more (class 1), persistently raised triglycerides and statin muscle symptoms [05.1][05.2][05.3]. It has no recommendation to test TSH before a statin: a search of the full text in an Internet Archive copy on 21 November 2026 found no mention of TSH testing (the online data supplement was not searched) [05.1].

In England, NICE moved from TSH for everyone before a statin (CG181, 2014, recommendation 1.3.13) to TSH only "in people with symptoms of underactive or overactive thyroid" (NG238 recommendation 1.5.5, published 14 December 2023, wording first introduced in May 2023) [05.11][05.12]. NG238 still says to treat secondary causes before starting a statin for primary prevention (1.6.6) and, for people who already have cardiovascular disease, "at the same time as starting statin treatment" (1.7.6) [05.12]. The European cardiology guideline has no recommendation on excluding hypothyroidism [05.17].

Current FDA statin labels list "uncontrolled hypothyroidism" as a myopathy (muscle damage) risk factor without asking for a test [05.20][05.21][05.22]; the UK Crestor label (30 March 2026) says hypothyroidism "should be treated prior to initiating therapy" and contraindicates the 40 mg dose [05.27].

A TSH test is listed at $16.80 and a lipid panel at $13.39 (Kentucky Medicaid 2026 schedule) [05.45]. With TSH rechecked 4 to 8 weeks after each dose change, the cholesterol recheck comes about 2 to 4 months after diagnosis (by inference) [05.6]. Subtracting the mean LDL falls after treatment (41.1 mg/dL from 168.8 in overt, 11.1 in subclinical hypothyroidism) leaves an average LDL near 128 mg/dL in both groups (this report's arithmetic), so treating the thyroid often does not end the cholesterol question [05.5].

For people with symptoms and a normal TSH, no current guideline offers a route to thyroid treatment ahead of a cholesterol drug: the 2014 American Thyroid Association guideline recommends strongly against levothyroxine for "nonspecific symptoms and normal biochemical indices of thyroid function", and for treated patients finds "insufficient evidence of benefit" in aiming for a low-normal TSH in those with raised cholesterol, excess weight or depression [05.50]. Every guideline defines the disease by TSH, so none of them tests the older standard.

## What the evidence did to the starting position

The brief's starting position was to be tested, not assumed. It had two stated parts and one implication.

**"Historical" means diagnosis by symptoms and signs before the TSH test.** This held in part. The case for retiring clinical diagnosis is weaker than its grading, resting on biochemical studies of 19 to 32 patients and no outcome trial [02.10][02.8][02.11][02.23]. But the documented standard was never symptoms alone: it required signs and a supporting test and classed about 0.2% of adults as hypothyroid, and the symptoms-and-temperature version is Barnes's, never validated [02.2][02.6][02.25]. Nor is the older standard the more generous: 8.0% of US adults reported thyroid hormone use in 2016 (abstract) [02.33].

**Bringing thyroid function to that standard before a cholesterol drug would lower cholesterol.** This held for overt hypothyroidism, where the older and the modern standard largely agree and LDL falls by about 41 mg/dL [01.1]. It failed where it was tested in people defined by symptoms: the 1939 Yale patients with ordinary cholesterol did not respond, and the 2001 trial found no cholesterol change [01.37][01.35]. It could not be tested for dose adjustment to relief over months, or for heart events and deaths, because no controlled study was found [04.19].

**TSH is pro-inflammatory, so a lower TSH than current practice has support from several angles.** The first half has laboratory support and little human support. Cell and mouse studies show TSH raising cholesterol synthesis and inflammation, but mostly from one group, with the liver work at doses far above blood levels in a tissue with little receptor [03.2][03.6][03.19]. In people, the designs that separate TSH from thyroid hormone are short, uncontrolled and contradictory [03.13][03.15][03.16], and pituitary hypothyroidism, withdrawal and genetic studies point to thyroid hormone [03.17][01.25][03.25]. The second half failed. What supports a lower TSH is one Norwegian cohort in women, not confirmed when pooled, and the observational link between treating subclinical hypothyroidism and fewer events in adults under about 70 [03.27][03.28][04.9]. Against it stand the pooled cohorts, with the lowest risk at about 1.90 to 2.90 mIU/L and more death and hip fracture at a low-normal TSH [03.33][03.34], the genetic and longevity studies [03.43][03.36], and the treated-patient data on suppressed TSH [04.49][03.45]. The version of the claim that survives is narrow: a TSH of 10 or more marks a deficiency that carries coronary risk [03.29]. That is current practice, not a lower target.

## What the evidence doesn't say

Thread 03 settled more about TSH as a marker than about TSH as a cause. No independent laboratory has reproduced the liver or macrophage findings, and no study has used primary human liver cells or a liver-only receptor knockout [03.1][03.6]. No TSH injection study had a placebo injection or lasted longer than days [03.15]. Two clean natural experiments appear unstudied for lipids: TSH-receptor mutations that block the response to a high TSH, and TSH-secreting pituitary tumours. The genetic studies cannot isolate TSH because their variants also move thyroid hormone. The Jinan group's funding was not checked [03.8]. Most of thread 03's sources were read as abstracts; only the liver-cell, macrophage and injection-lipid papers, a correction and the receptor database were read in full [03.2][03.6][03.15][03.19].

The "best TSH" data come from people sorted by a single TSH reading, not from trials of targets, and cannot say whether moving a treated person's TSH to 1.90 to 2.90 would help [03.33]. Several pooled analyses probably draw on overlapping cohorts, HUNT among them, so they are not independent confirmations of one another (this report's reading; the cohort lists were not compared) [03.28][03.29][03.33][03.34]. None of the outcome cohorts enrolled people by symptoms, so thread 03 says nothing about the symptom-defined patients of question 2.

Five trials would settle most of what is open, and none has been run: levothyroxine against placebo for heart events in subclinical hypothyroidism, including adults under 65; symptom-adjusted thyroid hormone against placebo over a year or more in adults with a normal TSH; thyroid first against statin first; a lower against a higher TSH target; and 3 to 6 months of TSH against placebo injections at fixed thyroid hormone levels. The likely reason is money: levothyroxine is off patent, and TRUST shrank from 3,000 to 737 participants chiefly through delays in obtaining tablets and matching placebo [04.3][04.1]. The gap reflects who pays for research, not what the answer would be.

The only study of how often TSH is checked covers one US centre in 2003 to 2011 [05.29], and the pre-TSH criteria come from specialist clinics whose everyday counterparts were probably looser [02.13].

The brief's bias note predicted the distortions that appeared. Modern studies sort people by the test that replaced symptoms, so the pre-1975 papers were read in the original [04.20]. Sellers sit on both sides [02.5][02.29]. The pull of current guidelines showed when the lipid thread expected a modest LDL benefit in subclinical hypothyroidism and revised it down on the 2026 blinded analysis [01.9]. On TSH, the predicted concentration of laboratory work in a few groups was found, and thread 03 carried out the planned compensation: it favoured human designs that separate TSH from thyroid hormone, sought findings against the starting position and found them in the genetic and longevity studies, and recorded authors' industry interests, none of which obviously favours one answer [03.33][03.32][03.43]. The model tendency to confirm a hypothesis it is handed did not show; the opposite risk, of dismissing the laboratory work, is why that work is set out in full above.

## Method note

Written on 21 November 2026 from five research threads, with the brief, sources.md and verification.md. Thread 03 (direct effects of TSH) was written later the same day on a different model, after its first research agent was stopped by a model's safety filter before it wrote anything (README run notes); this version of the report integrates it.

Verification covered 32 claims, each checked against its source: 19 confirmed and 13 corrected for wording (the figures matched; the corrected wording is used here), none unsupported and none inaccessible. Thirty are the threads' load-bearing claims, checked by agents that had not written them; C04.7 and C05.7 were checked by the report writer. Two further statements in "What this means for you", the coronary risk by TSH band [03.29] and the American Thyroid Association's wording on low-normal targets [05.50], were checked against their sources by the writer of this version on 21 November 2026 as C03.7 and C05.8. verification.md also lists the absence claims and the access limits.

Of the 268 sources in that file, 112 were read in full, 148 as abstracts and 8 as search snippets; the text says so where a key statement rests on an abstract.

# Thyroid function and cholesterol: what the evidence supports before a cholesterol-lowering drug

## Research Question

The brief asked three questions about adults:

1. How does thyroid function affect cholesterol, and how large is the effect?
2. Should doctors bring thyroid function to a healthy level before prescribing cholesterol-lowering drugs, where "healthy" is judged by the standard used before the modern TSH test (diagnosis by symptoms and signs) and not only by today's TSH reference ranges?
3. Is TSH itself harmful, for example by driving inflammation, so that keeping it lower than current practice does would be better?

A reader's comment on an earlier version of this report added a further starting position to test: that a TSH below about 0.01 mIU/L most likely reflects hyperthyroidism, that the harms attributed to a low TSH may be harms of excess thyroid hormone, and that no research seems to isolate people with a low TSH who are not hyperthyroid. A sixth research thread tested it.

US guidelines come first, with UK and European ones for comparison. Whether lowering cholesterol reduces deaths belongs to a companion review; children and pregnancy are out of scope.

Terms: TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) is the pituitary signal that rises when the thyroid is underactive and falls when there is too much thyroid hormone; values are in mIU/L, the same as mU/L. T4 (thyroxine) is the main thyroid hormone and T3 the more active one; blood tests measure their free forms. Levothyroxine is the T4 tablet and liothyronine the T3 tablet; desiccated thyroid, dried animal gland, contains both. Overt hypothyroidism is a high TSH with a low T4, subclinical hypothyroidism a raised TSH with a normal T4, and subclinical hyperthyroidism a low TSH with free T4 and T3 in range. LDL cholesterol is what statins lower; apoB is a related measure. A hazard ratio (HR) of 1.25 means a 25% higher rate of the outcome.

## Summary

Thyroid function moves cholesterol a lot only when the thyroid has clearly failed: treating overt hypothyroidism lowers LDL cholesterol by a pooled 41.1 mg/dL from a mean of 169 mg/dL, treating a mildly raised TSH lowers it by about 8.5 mg/dL in randomised trials (from a review's abstract) and by nothing measurable in the two blinded trials in older adults, and inside the normal TSH range the gap between low and high is about 2 mg/dL [01.1][01.7][01.9][01.19]. A TSH test before a cholesterol-lowering drug is therefore worth asking for, and treating a clearly underactive thyroid first fits the older and the modern standard alike: the test is listed at $16.80, the only practice study found it done in 49.5% of patients, and the Endocrine Society recommends "ruling out hypothyroidism as the cause of the hyperlipidemia before treatment with lipid-lowering medications" [05.45][05.29][05.5]. Treating by symptoms when TSH is normal is unproven, not disproven: the 1930s standard itself required a supporting test, three small blinded trials found thyroid hormone no better than placebo, and none counted heart attacks or deaths [02.2][04.19][04.20][04.21]. On question 3, no study in people shows that TSH itself causes inflammation or artery disease: the laboratory case comes mainly from one group, with liver-cell doses of 1 and 4 µM, and TSH injected into people on fixed thyroid hormone raised total cholesterol from 192 to 207 mg/dL over 4 days, with contradictory inflammation results [03.2][03.15][03.12][03.13]. Across 134,346 adults the lowest risk of cardiovascular events and death was at a TSH of about 1.90 to 2.90 mIU/L, and the low-end figures once quoted against a low TSH cannot be pinned on TSH itself: the lowest fifth's 9% higher death rate was adjusted for age and sex only and included people below the range and people taking thyroid hormone, the hip-fracture excess at a low-normal TSH was not adjusted for free T4, and the gene variants that lower TSH raise thyroid function set by free T3 [03.33][06.1][06.2][06.28]. A TSH below about 0.1, and especially an undetectable one, usually means more thyroid hormone than the body is set for, from an overactive gland or too high a dose, and that is where the clearest harms sit: on thyroid hormone, cardiovascular death was 39% more frequent below 0.1 and 13% more frequent at 0.1 to 0.5 [06.10][04.49]. A low TSH with normal hormone levels has been studied, with two-sided results: untreated people at 0.10 to 0.44 with a normal free T4 had more deaths (HR 1.23) and atrial fibrillation (HR 1.63), though in the largest such cohort their free T4 ran higher within the range; on levothyroxine that band shows little or no excess; and no outcome study has followed treated people whose free T4 and free T3 were both known to be in range [06.4][06.23][04.47].

## What this means for you

This section is for any adult offered a cholesterol-lowering drug, wondering whether an underactive thyroid has been ruled out, or told that their TSH is low. It gives evidence and questions to take to a doctor. It is not individual medical advice, and nothing here is a reason to start, stop or change a medicine on your own.

- **Ask whether your TSH has been measured, and what it was.** The test is listed at $16.80 [05.45], and it may not have been done: in the only practice study found, 49.5% of 8,795 US adults with newly found high cholesterol had it checked within about 6 months, and of those tested 5.2% had a raised TSH and 1.7% a TSH above 10 mIU/L [05.29]. The Endocrine Society recommends ruling hypothyroidism out before any lipid-lowering drug [05.5]; the 2026 ACC/AHA guideline asks for other causes to be evaluated only in three situations, such as an LDL of 190 mg/dL or more [05.1]; and NICE in England has since 2023 asked for TSH only "in people with symptoms of underactive or overactive thyroid" [05.12].

- **If the TSH is high and T4 is low (overt hypothyroidism), ask whether to treat the thyroid and recheck cholesterol before deciding on the drug.** Treatment lowers LDL by a pooled 41.1 mg/dL from a mean of 169 mg/dL in uncontrolled studies [01.1], within weeks [01.25]. The Endocrine Society suggests waiting until thyroid tests are normal, a conditional suggestion on very low-quality evidence [05.5], and the UK rosuvastatin label says such hypothyroidism "should be treated prior to initiating therapy" [05.27]. For people who already have cardiovascular disease, NICE says to "Treat comorbidities and secondary causes of dyslipidaemia at the same time as starting statin treatment" [05.12].

- **If the TSH is mildly raised and T4 is normal (subclinical hypothyroidism), ask for a repeat test, how high the TSH is, and what treating it would change.** Of 2,647 adults aged 65 or over re-tested for the TRUST trial after an earlier raised TSH, 62% were back below 4.60 mIU/L [04.1]. Coronary events were no more frequent at a TSH of 4.5 to 6.9 (HR 1.00) and clearly more frequent at 10 to 19.9 (HR 1.89, 95% CI 1.28 to 2.80), with coronary deaths raised from 7.0 upward (abstract) [03.29]. Treatment is unlikely to replace a cholesterol drug, since LDL fell by about 8.5 mg/dL in randomised trials (abstract) [01.7][01.9], and the only placebo-controlled trials that counted heart events saw 44 in 842 people aged 65 or over [04.5].

- **If the TSH is normal and you have symptoms, know what has and has not been tested.** Within the normal range LDL was 117.0 against 119.0 mg/dL between the lower half and the top tenth of TSH in 24,765 adults [01.19]. Three small blinded trials (18, 20 and 22 patients; main comparisons of 4 to 12 weeks) found thyroid hormone no better than placebo for symptoms, and none counted heart events [04.19][04.20][04.21]. No trial has tested months of dose adjustment, and current guidelines, which define the disease by TSH, offer no such route [05.50].

- **If you have read that TSH is inflammatory and should be kept low, know that the human evidence points the other way.** TSH has no demonstrated harm of its own in people [03.12][03.13][03.15], and the low-end findings belong at least as much to thyroid hormone. A TSH of 0.45 to 0.99 went with more hip fractures than 3.50 to 4.49 (HR 1.25, 1.05 to 1.49) [03.34], but free T4 also predicted hip fracture (HR 1.22 per standard deviation) and the paper reports no model with both [06.2]. Genetically lower TSH went with more heart attacks (risk estimate 1.35 per standard deviation) [03.43], but the variants that lower TSH raise thyroid function set by free T3 [06.28]. No study supports taking thyroid hormone to lower a normal TSH.

- **If your TSH is low and you do not take thyroid hormone, ask for a repeat test with free T4 and T3, and whether anything else could explain it.** The European guideline asks for a retest within 2 to 3 months, with free T4 and T3 [06.9]. A low but detectable TSH often settles (38 of 50 people over 60 were normal at 12 months), while an undetectable one tends to persist (14 of 16 still low) [06.11]. An undetectable TSH usually points to an overactive gland: 96% of hyperthyroid patients had a TSH below 0.005, while in hospital patients whose low TSH came from illness or glucocorticoid drugs it was usually above 0.01 [06.10]. A normal free T4 is not full reassurance: at a TSH of 0.10 to 0.44 with a normal free T4, deaths were 23% and atrial fibrillation 63% more frequent [06.4], and in Copenhagen people with a low TSH and a normal free T4 had a higher average free T4 than people with a normal TSH (16.89 against 15.05 pmol/L) [06.23]. The guideline sees treatment as indicated over 65 with a TSH below 0.1, as worth considering over 65 at 0.1 to 0.39, and as unsupported in younger people at 0.1 to 0.39 without symptoms [06.9].

- **If you take thyroid hormone, or are offered it, ask what TSH is being aimed for and how over-replacement will be caught.** Among 705,307 US veterans on thyroid hormone, cardiovascular death was 39% more frequent at a TSH below 0.1, 13% more frequent at 0.1 to 0.5, and 2.67 times as frequent above 20 [04.49]; in a Scottish cohort, read in abstract, a TSH of 0.03 or below went with about twice the fracture rate (HR 2.02), and 0.04 to 0.4 showed no significant excess [04.47]. A TSH just below the range is not the same as a suppressed one: after removal of the thyroid, 91.3% of patients on levothyroxine with a TSH below 0.5 had a free T3 within range, though 70.5% had a free T4 above it [06.16], and in 46 with a TSH above 0.03 and up to 0.3 and free T3 at their pre-surgery level, lipids, SHBG and bone markers (tested in 24 of them) did not differ significantly from before surgery [06.17]. That is a marker study; no outcome study has followed treated people whose free T4 and free T3 were both known to be in range. A high free T4 can reflect when the tablet was taken: 72% to 81% of results with a high free T4 and an unsuppressed TSH came from levothyroxine users [06.19]. The 2014 American Thyroid Association guideline finds "insufficient evidence of benefit" for aiming treated patients with raised cholesterol at a low-normal TSH [05.50]. No trial has compared targets for heart or bone outcomes, but the observational data give no reason to aim low and consistent reason to avoid a suppressed TSH.

- **If you take liothyronine, a combination or desiccated thyroid, ask whether T3 has been measured.** Free T4 cannot show over-replacement on these regimens: in thyroidectomised patients given liothyronine to a TSH of 0.5 to 1.5, free T4 fell below detection while free T3 averaged 5.9 pmol/L (range 2.8 to 6.5), against 4.0 on levothyroxine [06.18]. The only outcome data by TSH band, from US health records, found more atrial fibrillation and deaths on levothyroxine alone than on T3-containing treatment among people with a TSH below 0.45, but measured neither free T4 nor T3, and one author consults for companies including AbbVie, which distributes a desiccated thyroid product [06.40][02.40].

- **Thyroid hormone is not a cholesterol drug for people whose thyroid tests are normal.** It did not change cholesterol in the one such trial [01.35], and a thyroid drug tried as a cholesterol treatment at a TSH-suppressing dose was stopped in late 1971 for excess deaths [04.25][04.29].

**What would change this.** A placebo-controlled trial counting heart events in adults under 65 with a mildly raised TSH, or in people treated by symptoms; on 21 November 2026 none was registered [04.74][04.75]. A trial of a lower against a higher TSH target, or of months of TSH injections against placebo; the searches found neither [04.23][03.15]. For a low TSH, a re-analysis of the pooled cohorts that measured T3, cross-classifying TSH by free T4 and T3 [06.4]. And facts a general report cannot know: existing cardiovascular disease, an LDL of 190 mg/dL or more, or an age under 65, since the event trials enrolled only older people [04.5].

**Assumptions to discount if they are wrong.** The brief assumed a US reader; in the UK routine TSH testing before a statin is no longer standard [05.12], and the low-TSH grades and retesting advice come from the European guideline, because the 2016 American Thyroid Association guideline could not be read in full [06.9]. It took "historical" to mean diagnosis by symptoms and signs; the documented 1938 standard also required signs and a supporting test [02.2], and for Broda Barnes's symptoms-plus-temperature method the blinded trials above are the nearest controlled test [02.25]. If "a lower TSH" means treating a TSH of 10 or more, the coronary data support it; if it means pushing TSH into the low-normal range, they do not [03.29][03.33].

## How much thyroid function moves cholesterol

Treating overt hypothyroidism with levothyroxine lowers LDL cholesterol by a pooled 41.1 mg/dL (95% confidence interval 35.7 to 46.5) from a mean baseline of 169 mg/dL, total cholesterol by 58.4 mg/dL and apoB by 34.0 mg/dL [01.1]. These are uncontrolled changes from baseline in 2,305 participants, and the studies disagree widely (I² above 87%), so the direction is firmer than the size [01.1]. A fall of 41 from 169 is about a quarter (this report's arithmetic), close to what a low-intensity statin does [01.41]. Overt hypothyroidism is uncommon (0.4% of 25,862 Colorado adults): a major cause of raised cholesterol for the person who has it and a minor one across the population [01.2].

In adults whose thyroid had been removed for cancer, four weeks off thyroid hormone raised LDL cholesterol by 46 and 70 mg/dL (47% and 58%) and apoB by 46% to 48% in cohorts of 48 and 52 (this report's arithmetic from the reported means and medians) [01.25][01.27], and the rise reversed within 4 weeks of restarting in one of them [01.25]. The mechanism is the liver's clearance of LDL, which depends on thyroid hormone (abstract) [01.29].

In subclinical hypothyroidism the effect is small and shrinks as study quality rises. With every design pooled, LDL falls by 11.1 mg/dL, but 143 of that review's 166 studies were not randomised [01.1]. In randomised trials the pooled effect is −0.22 mmol/L (about −8.5 mg/dL; 12 trials, 940 participants), falling to −0.09 mmol/L in trials longer than 6 months (abstract) [01.7]. A post-hoc pooled analysis of the two double-blind trials in older adults (286 aged 65 or over, median TSH 6.44 mIU/L, 12 months) found −0.07 mmol/L (95% CI −0.15 to 0.02), not significant; only 27 had a TSH of 10 or more [01.9]. No large blinded trial with LDL as its main outcome has tested adults under 65, or anyone with a TSH of 10 to 20.

Inside the TSH reference range the effect on LDL is causal but tiny: 0.026 to 0.032 standard deviations of LDL per standard deviation of TSH in two Mendelian randomisation studies, which use inherited gene variants to test cause and effect (about 1 mg/dL by this report's conversion; both read in abstract) [01.21][01.22], and 117.0 against 119.0 mg/dL between the lower half and the top tenth of TSH in 24,765 adults without overt thyroid disease [01.19]. The variants shift the whole pituitary-thyroid set point, so this "TSH effect" cannot be told apart from a thyroid hormone effect.

The only randomised trial found that enrolled people by hypothyroid symptoms with normal thyroid tests (22 patients, 100 µg thyroxine for 12 weeks, double-blind crossover) showed no change in total cholesterol: difference 0.05 mmol/L (95% CI −0.27 to 0.37), with TSH 0.66 mU/L on thyroxine against 1.77 on placebo. It is too small to rule out a fall of about 10 mg/dL [01.35].

## What "healthy by the older standard" meant

There were two pre-TSH standards, and they differ about a hundredfold in how many people they class as hypothyroid. The standard at a leading US clinic about 1938 was narrow (calling it the mainstream standard is this report's characterisation): at Massachusetts General Hospital, myxoedema, the full picture of thyroid failure, was diagnosed on cardinal symptoms and signs supported by a low basal metabolic rate (BMR), and a low BMR alone was not an indication for thyroid [02.2]. Diagnosed hypothyroidism was about 0.2% of adults in a north-east England survey published in 1972, whose authors inferred a large undiagnosed pool [02.6]. The expansive standard is Broda Barnes's: a waking armpit temperature below 97.8°F plus any of a long list of complaints. His 40% prevalence is a stated conviction, and his normal range rests on his own unreferenced readings; apart from his 1942 report, which was not opened, the search found no published validation against a thyroid measure [02.25].

Symptoms alone separate poorly, and the older clinicians said so. In Wayne's Glasgow series of 1959, tiredness was reported by 98% of proved hypothyroid patients, 88% of euthyroid suspects (people referred who turned out not to have it) and 42% of healthy controls [02.4]. In 25,862 Colorado adults, single-symptom sensitivity was 2.9% to 28.3%, measured against TSH [02.16]. Wayne used objective support, including serum cholesterol, above 300 mg/100 ml in 81% of his hypothyroid cases [02.4].

One pre-TSH series tests question 2 directly. In the 1939 Yale series, all 16 patients who recovered on desiccated thyroid had an untreated serum cholesterol of 315 to 741 mg%, and one of the 16 was very slow to recover. The 13 with a low metabolic rate or hypothyroid-type symptoms and lower cholesterol (149 to 300 mg%) showed little or no improvement; in 8 of the 12 with data the cholesterol fall was only 11 to 65 mg%, and in 3 of those followed closely it was transient. The result is partly circular, since the series defined hypothyroidism in part by cholesterol and by response to thyroid [01.37]. The usual dose at Massachusetts General about 1938, 1 to 3 grains of desiccated thyroid (most 1½), is within today's labelled range [02.2][02.40].

The move to TSH rested on small biochemical studies (32 patients in 1965, 22 in 1973, 19 in 1987) and no outcome trial that the search could find [02.10][02.8][02.11]. The 2012 US guideline's Grade A rule against clinical scoring systems rests on three studies it grades as level 3, at least two of which classified patients by TSH [02.23]. Modern studies judge symptoms against TSH, so none can show whether TSH misses disease, and the TSH reference range is itself a statistical convention, the 2.5th to 97.5th percentiles, set without regard to outcomes [03.33]. Today's practice treats more people than the narrow older standard did: 8.0% of US adults reported thyroid hormone use in 2016 (abstract) [02.33], and of 58,706 insured US adults who started levothyroxine in 2008 to 2018 with TSH and T4 on record, 30.5% had normal results [02.34].

## What thyroid treatment does to heart events and deaths, and what too much does

For overt hypothyroidism no trial has compared treatment with none, since withholding it would be unethical. In a Danish register, read in abstract, untreated hypothyroid people had higher mortality than people with normal tests (HR 1.46; 95% CI 1.26 to 1.69) and treated people did not [04.50].

For subclinical hypothyroidism, the only placebo-controlled trials of levothyroxine that counted events pooled 842 people aged 65 or over (mean TSH 6.4) for a median 17 months: 44 cardiovascular events (HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.41 to 1.35) and 21 deaths (HR 1.28, 0.54 to 3.03) [04.5]. The larger trial, TRUST, was cut from a planned 3,000 participants and its report says it cannot exclude protection or harm [04.3][04.1]; 62% of the 2,647 people re-tested for entry no longer had a raised TSH [04.1], so the results do not cover younger adults, marked symptoms or a TSH above 10, where untreated coronary risk is clearly raised [03.29]. No placebo-controlled trial was found that counted heart events or deaths in overt hypothyroidism, in adults under 65 with subclinical hypothyroidism, or in people with symptoms and a normal TSH [04.7][04.76], and on 21 November 2026 the only registered trials with events as their primary outcome were open-label [04.74][04.75]. Observational studies, read in abstract, associate levothyroxine for subclinical hypothyroidism with fewer events in younger adults (HR 0.61 at ages 40 to 70; pooled mortality relative risk 0.50 under 65 to 70) but not in older ones [04.9][04.12].

In symptom-defined patients, three blinded placebo-controlled studies (1960, n = 18; 1960, n = 20; 2001, n = 22 completing) found thyroid hormone no better than placebo [04.20][04.21][04.19]. The 1960 studies chose patients with a low basal metabolic rate and normal thyroid tests of the time (one allocated by rotation, one only partly blinded), the 2001 trial patients with tests in the reference range; the main comparisons ran 4 to 12 weeks, none counted events, and no trial tested dose adjustment to relief over months. Barnes's uncontrolled 1959 series reported a cholesterol fall and no data on arterial disease [04.35].

The Coronary Drug Project gave dextrothyroxine 6 mg a day to 1,110 men after a heart attack (placebo 2,789) and stopped the arm in late 1971 for excess deaths [04.25]. The dose was about 1.7 times the 3.5 mg that suppresses TSH (a mean from 6 patients), and the marketed tablets later assayed at 0.50% to 2.30% levothyroxine, so in this report's reading the arm tested thyroid excess, not replacement (both papers read in abstract) [04.29][04.30].

The firmest finding concerns over-replacement, and it sits at a suppressed TSH. On treatment, cardiovascular death was HR 1.39 at a TSH below 0.1, 1.13 at 0.1 to 0.5 and 2.67 above 20 (705,307 US veterans) [04.49]; in Tayside, Scotland, a TSH of 0.03 or below carried HR 1.37 for cardiovascular disease and 2.02 for fracture, while 0.04 to 0.4 showed no significant excess (abstract) [04.47]. A UK cohort of 162,369 found deaths raised below 0.1 (HR 1.18) but not at 0.1 to 0.4, and no link with atrial fibrillation or fracture [04.48]. In thyroid cancer survivors kept on suppressive doses, cardiovascular death rose 3.08-fold (1.32 to 7.21) for each tenfold fall in TSH (abstract) [03.45].

None of these cohorts separated TSH from the hormone level: the veterans' analyses modelled TSH and free T4 separately (free T4 above 1.9 ng/dL: HR 1.29 for cardiovascular death), and no treated cohort cross-classified the two [06.6]. On levothyroxine a low TSH usually comes with a normal free T3: of 207 patients without a thyroid whose TSH was below 0.5, 70.5% had a free T4 above range but 91.3% a free T3 within it [06.16]. In 46 such patients with a TSH above 0.03 and up to 0.3, and free T3 at their pre-surgery level, lipids, SHBG (a liver marker of thyroid hormone action) and bone markers (tested in 24) did not differ significantly from before surgery, while at 0.03 or below SHBG and a bone marker rose [06.17]. That is the closest human evidence that a mildly low TSH on levothyroxine carries no tissue sign of excess, and it has no outcomes. A low TSH on treatment also marks weight loss, frailty and deliberate suppression after cancer.

Regimens that contain T3 need T3 to judge them: on liothyronine dosed to a TSH of 0.5 to 1.5, free T4 fell below detection [06.18]. The only outcome data by TSH band, from US health records, found more atrial fibrillation and death on levothyroxine alone than on T3-containing regimens among people with at least two TSH results below 0.45, with the figures for that group in a supplementary table not read [06.40]. The study measured neither free T4 nor T3, may reflect who is prescribed T3 rather than what T3 does, and has an author who consults for companies including AbbVie, which distributes Armour Thyroid [06.40][02.40].

## Whether TSH itself does harm, and which TSH level goes with the best outcomes

Testing this needs evidence that separates TSH from thyroid hormone, because TSH moves against the hormone: a high TSH almost always means too little, and a suppressed TSH usually too much. Four kinds of evidence bear on it, and they point different ways [03.1][03.15][03.33][06.4].

### Cells and animals: a real signal, mostly from one laboratory

A group at Shandong Provincial Hospital in Jinan, China, has reported that TSH acting on liver TSH receptors raises HMG-CoA reductase, the cholesterol-making enzyme that statins block, in rats held on fixed thyroid hormone (abstract) [03.1] and in liver cells treated with bovine TSH at 1 and 4 µM for 48 hours; mice lacking the receptor (5 against 4) had lower total cholesterol, 1.90 against 2.93 mmol/L (full text) [03.2]. The group also reported that TSH suppresses bile-acid synthesis (abstract) [03.3], and that atherosclerosis-prone mice lacking the TSH receptor developed less atherosclerosis while dietary thyroxine kept their T3, T4 and TSH matched to controls, with TSH at 0.2 to 5 ng/mL switching on inflammatory pathways in macrophages (full text) [03.6]. Other groups reported that TSH raised PCSK9 in a liver-cancer cell line [03.5] and IL-6 release from human fat cells (abstracts) [03.10].

Three things limit what this shows about people. The liver doses were far above blood levels, where even the standard therapeutic TSH injection peaks at an average 116 mU/L (label, snippet only) [03.46]; the thread's rough conversion, from no source, puts 1 µM tens of thousands of times above a normal TSH, while the macrophage doses were nearer the physiological range [03.6]. The receptor is scarce where the effects are claimed: 0.4 nTPM of TSH-receptor RNA in human liver against 257 in thyroid, 0.5 in fat and 0.3 in heart muscle [03.19]. And the liver, bile-acid and macrophage findings come from the Jinan group alone, which also produced the supporting human cross-sectional studies [03.4][03.8]; no independent laboratory was found to have reproduced them. This is the concentration in a few groups, with circular validation, that the bias note predicted.

### People with thyroid hormone held fixed: small, brief and inconsistent

The cleanest human test gives injected TSH to thyroid cancer survivors on a steady levothyroxine dose, so that TSH rises for a few days while T4 does not. In 27 women, TSH went from 0.43 to 12.3 mU/L and total cholesterol from 192 to 207 mg/dL over 4 days, with LDL 115 to 126 mg/dL (p = 0.066) (full text) [03.15]. In an independent study of 82 patients, apoB went from 0.90 to 0.92 g/L, a change that tracked a fall in T3, which the authors read as TSH acting on thyroid hormone metabolism rather than on the liver (abstract) [03.16]. On inflammation and blood vessels the studies contradict each other: artery dilation fell and inflammatory markers rose in 24 patients [03.12], adhesion molecules rose at TSH levels the authors called "supraphysiological" in 20 [03.14], and dilation improved with no change in IL-6 or hs-CRP in 34 [03.13]. All were read as abstracts, none had a placebo injection, and none lasted beyond days.

Natural experiments point to thyroid hormone, not TSH, as the driver. When thyroid hormone was withdrawn for four weeks and TSH rose from 0.14 to 66.9 mU/L, hs-CRP fell from 0.17 to 0.08 mg/L [01.25], and it was unchanged at a TSH near 99 in a second cohort [01.27]. When thyroid hormone is low because the pituitary has failed, so that TSH is not raised, raised lipids were still common at the Mayo Clinic and improved with thyroid hormone (abstract) [03.17]; in 90 patients with pituitary hypothyroidism, those with a low TSH had low HDL more often than those with a normal TSH (abstract) [03.18]. Other pituitary deficiencies confound these. Genetically higher TSH did not raise CRP, while higher CRP raised TSH (abstract) [03.25], so a raised CRP in hypothyroidism [03.26] looks like a marker of the hypothyroid state.

### Populations: risk is lowest in the middle of the range

The largest analysis pooled 134,346 adults from 26 cohorts. The lowest risk of cardiovascular events and death was at the 60th to 80th percentiles of TSH (median 1.90 to 2.90 mIU/L), and against that band the lowest fifth had HR 1.09 (1.05 to 1.14) for death from any cause, adjusted for age and sex only [03.33]. That fifth was not a clean low-normal group. Only whole cohorts of pregnant women, of people with overt thyroid disease or of people with cardiovascular disease were excluded, with participants under 18, so the main analysis included people with a TSH below the range, by its design any overt hyperthyroidism in the cohorts, and 5,722 people (4.6%) taking thyroid medication; TSH and free T4 were modelled separately, and T3 was left out because most cohorts lacked it [06.1]. The authors report that restricting to euthyroid participants "showed similar results to our main analyses", with the figures in an appendix this review did not read [06.1]. Thread 06's researcher estimated, from the 3.2% to 4.2% of people below 0.45 in overlapping cohorts, that roughly one sixth to one fifth of the lowest fifth was below the range [06.4][06.5]. That estimate is the researcher's own, not a sourced figure; on it, the 9% excess is mostly a low-normal result. Free T4 gave the clearer signal, with the most risk in the top fifth (HR 1.34 for death) [03.33].

Within the reference range TSH showed no link with coronary disease: in 55,412 people from 14 cohorts, each 1 mIU/L higher TSH carried HR 0.97 (0.90 to 1.04) for coronary death and 1.00 (0.97 to 1.03) for coronary events [03.28]. HUNT in Norway had found more coronary death in women at 2.5 to 3.5 mIU/L than at 0.50 to 1.4 (HR 1.69); the pooled analysis that included it did not confirm it [03.27][03.28].

Hip fracture was more common at a TSH of 0.45 to 0.99 than at 3.50 to 4.49 (HR 1.25, 1.05 to 1.49; 56,835 people) [03.34], but that analysis kept people on thyroid medication (3.1%), did not exclude an out-of-range free T4, and modelled TSH and free T4 separately; free T4 also predicted hip fracture (HR 1.22 per standard deviation) [06.2]. Atrial fibrillation tracked free T4 rather than TSH [03.35].

In genetic studies lower TSH went with more atrial fibrillation [03.41]; in 105,224 Copenhagen adults the genetic risk estimate per standard deviation of lower TSH was 1.35 (1.06 to 1.71) for heart attack and 1.28 (1.08 to 1.52) for atrial fibrillation, though the abstract does not say whether these are odds ratios [03.43]; and genetically higher TSH went with slightly lower mortality in UK Biobank [03.44]. These results cannot separate TSH from thyroid hormone. In up to 271,040 people with normal thyroid function, TSH-associated variants "reflect the thyroid function determined by free T3", and genetically higher free T3 raised atrial fibrillation risk while higher free T4 lowered it [06.28]. The same variants predict an overactive gland: the top quarter of a TSH-raising gene score had 0.19 times the odds of hyperthyroidism of the bottom quarter [06.29]. A genetically low TSH is a lifelong tilt toward more thyroid hormone action, and the heart findings fit a T3 path at least as well as a TSH one.

Single cohorts on mortality conflict, and age explains much of it: NHANES III found death raised at both ends of the range [03.30], while in older adults and at age 85 higher TSH went with longer life (abstracts) [03.31][03.32][03.36]. None of these findings shows TSH to be a cause of harm. They still answer the "lower is better" claim, which is about where a TSH should sit: no analysis found a benefit at the bottom of the range, and the slight excesses there were never separated from the free T4 and T3 that come with a low TSH [03.33][03.34][06.2][06.28].

On its second point, untreated people with a low TSH and a normal free T4 have been studied, and they still do worse. In pooled cohorts of 52,674 people not taking thyroxine or antithyroid drugs, a TSH of 0.10 to 0.44 with a normal free T4 carried total mortality HR 1.23 (1.04 to 1.45) and atrial fibrillation HR 1.63 (1.10 to 2.41), adjusted for age and sex, though 350 people with a low TSH and no free T4 value stayed in the analysis; excluding the 143 with an abnormal T3, measured in 5 of 10 cohorts, left the whole low-TSH group with atrial fibrillation HR 1.64 and total mortality HR 1.20 [06.4]. In Tayside, a TSH low on two tests at least 4 months apart, with normal free or total T4 and normal total T3, carried cardiovascular morbidity HR 1.36 (1.19 to 1.57) and dysrhythmia HR 1.39 (1.02 to 1.90) after excluding people who became overtly hyperthyroid (abstract) [06.13]. In 586,460 Copenhagen people with a normal free T4, atrial fibrillation rose as TSH fell: incidence rate ratio 1.12 (1.03 to 1.21) at 0.2 to 0.4 and 1.41 (1.25 to 1.59) below 0.1 [06.23].

But "normal free T4" did not mean the same free T4. In Copenhagen it averaged 16.89 pmol/L in people with a TSH below 0.2 against 15.05 at 0.2 to 5.0 [06.23], and inside the normal range free T4 tracks atrial fibrillation better than TSH does [03.35]. Because subclinical hyperthyroidism is defined by the low TSH, a study that requires normal free hormones still selects people whose free T4 sits high in the range, so these studies cannot say whether the excess comes from TSH or from hormone that is in range but above the person's own set point. T3, the likelier driver, was measured in only half the pooled cohorts and as total T3 in Tayside [06.4][06.13]. Why the same band looks better on levothyroxine is untested; one possibility is that levothyroxine gives a lower free T3 at a given TSH [06.16].

For fracture the human evidence points mostly to thyroid hormone. In Tayside no link between an untreated low TSH and fracture was found once people who became overtly hyperthyroid were excluded (abstract) [06.13]. In 74 people with pituitary hypothyroidism, whose TSH is low or normal whatever the dose, vertebral fractures went with a higher free T4, a higher levothyroxine dose and untreated growth hormone deficiency (cross-sectional) [06.36]. Genetically lower TSH did not change bone density [03.24], and a 2024 genetic analysis found no causal effect of any thyroid measure on bone density or fracture [06.28]. Against this stands one cross-sectional study of 130 postmenopausal women with low bone density, in whom the lowest third of TSH had more vertebral fractures after adjustment for free T4 (OR 2.8, 1.20 to 6.79; abstract) [06.34].

## What guidelines and labels ask for before a cholesterol drug

The Endocrine Society guideline (2020) recommends "ruling out hypothyroidism as the cause of the hyperlipidemia before treatment with lipid-lowering medications" (recommendation 6.1, strong, high-quality evidence) and suggests not treating hyperlipidaemia in overt hypothyroidism until thyroid tests are normal (recommendation 6.3, conditional, very low quality) [05.5]. Its own text says the effect of thyroxine on long-term cardiovascular outcomes "is unknown" [05.5].

The main US cholesterol guideline is narrower. The 2026 ACC/AHA guideline, which replaced the 2018 one on 13 March 2026, tells clinicians to exclude or evaluate secondary causes, hypothyroidism among them, in three situations: LDL cholesterol of 190 mg/dL or more (class 1), persistently raised triglycerides and statin muscle symptoms [05.1]. A search of its full text in an Internet Archive copy on 21 November 2026 found no mention of TSH testing (the online data supplement was not searched) [05.1]. In England, NICE moved from TSH for everyone before a statin (CG181, 2014) to TSH only "in people with symptoms of underactive or overactive thyroid" (NG238 recommendation 1.5.5, published 14 December 2023, wording first introduced in May 2023) [05.11][05.12]. NG238 still says to treat secondary causes before starting a statin for primary prevention (1.6.6) and, for people who already have cardiovascular disease, "at the same time as starting statin treatment" (1.7.6) [05.12]. The European cardiology guideline has no recommendation on excluding hypothyroidism [05.17].

Current FDA statin labels list "uncontrolled hypothyroidism" as a myopathy (muscle damage) risk factor without asking for a test [05.20][05.21][05.22]; the UK Crestor label (30 March 2026) says hypothyroidism "should be treated prior to initiating therapy" and contraindicates the 40 mg dose [05.27].

A TSH test is listed at $16.80 and a lipid panel at $13.39 (Kentucky Medicaid 2026 schedule) [05.45]. With TSH rechecked 4 to 8 weeks after each dose change, the cholesterol recheck comes about 2 to 4 months after diagnosis (by inference) [05.6]. Subtracting the mean falls after treatment (41.1 mg/dL from 168.8 in overt, 11.1 in subclinical hypothyroidism) leaves an average LDL near 128 mg/dL in both (this report's arithmetic), so treating the thyroid often does not end the cholesterol question [05.5].

For people with symptoms and a normal TSH, no current guideline offers a route to thyroid treatment ahead of a cholesterol drug: the 2014 American Thyroid Association guideline recommends strongly against levothyroxine for "nonspecific symptoms and normal biochemical indices of thyroid function", and finds "insufficient evidence of benefit" in aiming treated patients with raised cholesterol, excess weight or depression at a low-normal TSH [05.50]. Every guideline defines the disease by TSH, so none tests the older standard.

## What the evidence did to the starting position

The brief's starting position had two stated parts and one implication; a reader's comment added a further position in three parts.

**"Historical" means diagnosis by symptoms and signs before the TSH test.** This held in part. The case for retiring clinical diagnosis is weaker than its grading, resting on biochemical studies of 19 to 32 patients and no outcome trial [02.10][02.8][02.11][02.23]. But the documented standard was never symptoms alone: it required signs and a supporting test and classed about 0.2% of adults as hypothyroid, and the symptoms-and-temperature version is Barnes's, never validated [02.2][02.6][02.25].

**Bringing thyroid function to that standard before a cholesterol drug would lower cholesterol.** This held for overt hypothyroidism, where the two standards agree [01.1], and failed where it was tested in people defined by symptoms [01.37][01.35]. Dose adjustment over months, and heart events and deaths, have no controlled study [04.19].

**TSH is pro-inflammatory, so a lower TSH has support from several angles.** The first half has laboratory support, mostly from one group at doses far above blood levels, and little human support; the human designs that separate TSH from thyroid hormone point to the hormone [03.2][03.19][03.15][03.17][01.25][03.25]. The second half failed. A lower TSH has one unconfirmed Norwegian cohort and an observational benefit of treating subclinical hypothyroidism under about 70 behind it [03.27][03.28][04.9]; against it stand the lowest pooled risk at about 1.90 to 2.90 mIU/L, the longevity studies and the treated-patient data on suppressed TSH [03.33][03.36][04.49]. The low-end excesses and genetic findings also run against a lower target, though they cannot be laid on TSH rather than thyroid hormone [06.1][06.2][06.28]. What survives is current practice: a TSH of 10 or more marks a deficiency that carries coronary risk [03.29].

**A TSH below about 0.01 most likely reflects hyperthyroidism.** This held for untreated outpatients, if hyperthyroidism includes the subclinical kind, which is what it usually is over 60 [06.10][06.11]. On levothyroxine it reflects a dose above the pituitary's set point; in one study of patients on levothyroxine with any TSH below 0.5, free T3 was usually still within range, though the below-0.01 group was not reported separately [06.16]. On liothyronine, free T4 cannot show excess at all [06.18].

**The harms attributed to a low TSH may be harms of excess thyroid hormone.** For fracture the evidence leans this way, with one small study against [06.13][06.28][06.34]. For atrial fibrillation and death it fits only if "excess" includes hormone inside the reference range but above the person's own set point, since untreated people with a low TSH and a normal free T4 still had more of both, with free T4 higher in the range [06.4][06.23]. On levothyroxine the harm sat at a suppressed TSH [04.47][04.49].

**No research isolates people with a low TSH who are not hyperthyroid.** This failed in part: studies that required a normal free T4, and two that required or tested a normal T3, still found more atrial fibrillation, cardiovascular disease and deaths, including at 0.10 to 0.44 [06.4][06.13][06.23]. What holds is narrower. Because subclinical hyperthyroidism is defined by the low TSH, those studies cannot separate TSH from high-normal hormone, and no outcome study has followed treated people with free T4 and free T3 both known to be in range, or T3 and desiccated-thyroid users analysed by T3 [06.4][06.40].

## What the evidence doesn't say

On TSH as a cause, no independent laboratory has reproduced the liver or macrophage findings [03.1][03.6], no TSH injection study had a placebo injection or lasted beyond days [03.15], and two clean natural experiments, TSH-receptor mutations that block the response to a high TSH and TSH-secreting pituitary tumours, appear unstudied for lipids. Genetic studies cannot isolate TSH, because the variants that set TSH also set free T3 [06.28].

The "best TSH" data come from people sorted by a single TSH reading, not from trials of targets [03.33], and several pooled analyses probably share cohorts, so they do not confirm one another independently (this report's reading; the cohort lists were not compared) [03.28][03.29][03.33][03.34][06.4]. None of the outcome cohorts enrolled people by symptoms.

On a low TSH, thread 06 found no outcome study of treated people whose low TSH came with free T4 and free T3 both known to be in range (a PubMed search combining the terms returned nothing), none of T3 or desiccated-thyroid users analysed by T3, and no large cohort that put TSH, free T4 and free T3 in one model; the only free-T4-adjusted TSH result was the 130-woman fracture study [06.34]. The Xu 2023 appendix, the full Tayside papers and the 2016 American Thyroid Association guideline were not obtained, and the European guideline was read only to its first 100,000 characters, through a summarising tool [06.1][06.9]. The cheapest way to close the main gap is a re-analysis of individual data in the pooled cohorts that measured T3, cross-classifying TSH by free T4 and T3 for atrial fibrillation and fracture [06.4]; the definitive test is a randomised trial in adults without a thyroid comparing TSH targets of 0.1 to 0.4 and 1 to 2.5, with monitored atrial fibrillation and bone density.

Five other trials would settle most of the rest, and none has been run: levothyroxine against placebo for heart events in subclinical hypothyroidism, including under 65; symptom-adjusted thyroid hormone against placebo with a normal TSH; thyroid first against statin first; a lower against a higher TSH target; and months of TSH against placebo injections. The likely reason is money: levothyroxine is off patent, and TRUST shrank from 3,000 to 737 participants chiefly through delays in obtaining tablets and matching placebo [04.3][04.1]. The only study of how often TSH is checked covers one US centre in 2003 to 2011 [05.29].

The bias note's predicted distortions appeared. Modern studies sort people by the test that replaced symptoms, so pre-1975 papers were read in the original [04.20]. Sellers sit on both sides [02.5][02.29], and the one outcome comparison favouring T3-containing treatment has an author who consults for the distributor of a desiccated thyroid product [06.40][02.40]. Guidelines monitor treatment by TSH alone, so few datasets hold T3. The pull of current guidelines showed when the lipid thread expected a modest LDL benefit in subclinical hypothyroidism and revised it down on the 2026 blinded analysis [01.9]. The tendency of a model to confirm the hypothesis it is handed was tested twice and did not show: thread 03 did not confirm the brief's starting position, and thread 06 found the reader's further position right in part and wrong in part.

## Method note

Written on 21 November 2026 from six research threads, with the brief, sources.md and verification.md. Thread 03 (direct effects of TSH) was written later that day on a different model, after its first research agent was stopped by a model's safety filter; thread 06 (a low TSH without excess hormone) was added after a reader's comment on the earlier version of this report. This version integrates both.

Verification covered 42 claims, each checked against its source: 25 confirmed and 17 reworded (the figures matched; the corrected wording is used here), none unsupported and none inaccessible. Thirty-eight are the threads' load-bearing claims, checked by agents that had not written them; C04.7, C05.7, C03.7 and C05.8 were checked by earlier report writers. For this version the writer checked six further statements in the Summary and "What this means for you" against their sources (C06.9 to C06.14 in verification.md): five were confirmed, and one, the US records study of T3-containing treatment, was confirmed in direction only, because its low-TSH figures are in a supplementary table that was not read; the text says so and quotes no figure from it. verification.md also lists the absence claims and the access limits.

Each line in Sources ends with the source's tier and how much of it was read, copied from sources.md. Of the 313 sources in that file, 131 were read in full, 173 as abstracts and 9 as search snippets; the text says so where a key statement rests on an abstract. This report is general evidence for a public reader. It contains no information about any individual and is not medical advice.

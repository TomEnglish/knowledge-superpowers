# Thyroid function and cholesterol: what the evidence supports before a cholesterol-lowering drug

## Research Question

The brief asked three questions about adults:

1. How does thyroid function affect cholesterol, and how large is the effect?
2. Should doctors bring thyroid function to a healthy level before prescribing cholesterol-lowering drugs, where "healthy" is judged by the standard used before the modern TSH test (diagnosis by symptoms and signs) and not only by today's TSH reference ranges?
3. Is TSH itself harmful, for example by driving inflammation, so that keeping it lower than current practice does would be better?

US guidelines come first, with UK and European ones for comparison. Whether lowering cholesterol reduces deaths belongs to a companion review; children and pregnancy are out of scope.

Terms: TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) is the pituitary signal that rises when the thyroid is underactive, and the blood test now used to diagnose it. T4 (thyroxine) is the main thyroid hormone; levothyroxine is the T4 tablet. Overt hypothyroidism is a high TSH with a low T4; subclinical hypothyroidism is a raised TSH with a normal T4. LDL cholesterol is what statins are prescribed to lower; apoB is a related measure.

## Summary

Thyroid function moves cholesterol a lot only when the thyroid has clearly failed: treating overt hypothyroidism lowers LDL cholesterol by a pooled 41.1 mg/dL from a mean of 169 mg/dL, treating a mildly raised TSH lowers it by about 8.5 mg/dL in randomised trials (from a review's abstract) and by nothing measurable in the two blinded trials in older adults, and inside the normal TSH range the gap between low and high is about 2 mg/dL [01.1][01.7][01.9][01.19]. A TSH test before a cholesterol-lowering drug is therefore worth asking for, and treating a clearly underactive thyroid first fits the older and the modern standard alike: the test is listed at $16.80, the only practice study found it done in 49.5% of patients, and the Endocrine Society recommends "ruling out hypothyroidism as the cause of the hyperlipidemia before treatment with lipid-lowering medications" [05.45][05.29][05.5][02.4]. The evidence does not support going further and treating by symptoms when TSH is normal: a leading US clinic of the 1930s required the full clinical picture backed by a metabolic test, three small blinded trials found thyroid hormone no better than placebo, and no trial has counted heart attacks or deaths, so that approach is unproven, not disproven [02.2][04.19][04.20][04.21].

Question 3, whether TSH itself is harmful, is unanswered: the part of the research that was to examine it was never written, and this report draws no conclusion on it. What the completed research does show is that too much and too little thyroid hormone both go with worse outcomes: among 705,307 US veterans taking thyroid hormone, a TSH below 0.1 went with a 39% higher rate of cardiovascular death and a TSH above 20 with a rate 2.67 times higher [04.49].

## What this means for you

This section is for any adult offered a cholesterol-lowering drug, or wondering whether an underactive thyroid has been ruled out. It gives evidence and questions to take to a doctor.

It is not individual medical advice. Nothing here is a reason to start, stop or change a medicine on your own.

- **Ask whether your TSH has been measured, and what it was.** The test is listed at $16.80 (Kentucky Medicaid 2026 schedule) [05.45], and it may not have been done: in the only practice study found, 49.5% of 8,795 US adults with newly found high cholesterol had it checked within about 6 months, and of those tested 5.2% had a raised TSH and 1.7% a TSH above 10 mIU/L [05.29]. The Endocrine Society recommends ruling hypothyroidism out before any lipid-lowering drug [05.5]. The 2026 ACC/AHA guideline asks for other causes to be excluded or evaluated in three situations (LDL cholesterol of 190 mg/dL or more, persistently raised triglycerides, muscle symptoms on a statin) and does not recommend a TSH before every statin [05.1]. In England, NICE has since 2023 asked for TSH only "in people with symptoms of underactive or overactive thyroid" [05.12].

- **If the TSH is high and T4 is low (overt hypothyroidism), ask whether to treat the thyroid and recheck cholesterol before deciding on the drug.** Treatment lowers LDL by a pooled 41.1 mg/dL from a mean of 169 mg/dL, in before-and-after studies without control groups [01.1]. The change is quick: in one study of 48 people, the LDL rise from four weeks off thyroid hormone had reversed within four weeks of restarting [01.25]. The Endocrine Society suggests waiting until thyroid tests are normal before treating cholesterol, a conditional suggestion on very low-quality evidence [05.5], and the UK rosuvastatin label says hypothyroidism causing raised cholesterol "should be treated prior to initiating therapy" [05.27]. One exception matters: for people who already have cardiovascular disease, NICE says to "Treat comorbidities and secondary causes of dyslipidaemia at the same time as starting statin treatment" [05.12].

- **If the TSH is mildly raised and T4 is normal (subclinical hypothyroidism), ask for a repeat test and what treating it would change.** Such results often do not last: of 2,647 adults aged 65 or over re-tested for the TRUST trial after an earlier raised TSH, 62% were back below 4.60 mIU/L [04.1]. Treatment is unlikely to replace a cholesterol drug: LDL fell by about 8.5 mg/dL across 12 randomised trials (from the review's abstract) and not significantly in the two blinded trials in adults aged 65 or over [01.7][01.9]. Whether it prevents heart attacks is unknown: the only placebo-controlled trials that counted them enrolled 842 people aged 65 or over and saw 44 cardiovascular events, too few to show protection or harm [04.5][04.1]. Observational studies, read in abstract, link treatment with fewer events below age 65 to 70, but two analyses of one UK database disagree on deaths [04.9][04.12][04.14][04.15].

- **If the TSH is normal and you have symptoms, know what has and has not been tested.** Within the normal range thyroid status explains very little of a raised LDL: 117.0 against 119.0 mg/dL between the lower half and the top tenth of TSH in 24,765 adults [01.19]. Three small blinded trials (18, 20 and 22 patients; main comparisons of 4 to 12 weeks) found thyroid hormone no better than placebo for symptoms, and none counted heart events [04.19][04.20][04.21]. Treating on symptoms alone is therefore unproven. It is not disproven either: no trial has tested months of dose adjustment or measured its safety. Current guidelines, which define the disease by TSH, offer no such route [05.50].

- **If you take thyroid hormone, or are offered it, ask what TSH is being aimed for and how over-replacement will be caught.** Among 705,307 US veterans on thyroid hormone, cardiovascular death was 39% more frequent at a TSH below 0.1, 13% more frequent at 0.1 to 0.5, and 2.67 times as frequent above 20 [04.49]. In a Scottish cohort, read in abstract, a TSH of 0.03 or below went with about twice the fracture rate (hazard ratio 2.02), and 0.04 to 0.4 showed no significant excess [04.47]. These figures are observational, and an extreme TSH also marks other illness, but both ends carry risk.

- **Thyroid hormone is not a cholesterol drug for people whose thyroid tests are normal.** Thyroxine did not change total cholesterol in the only randomised trial found in people with symptoms and normal tests [01.35]. A large trial of a thyroid drug as a cholesterol treatment stopped that treatment in late 1971 for excess deaths; its dose was above the level that suppresses TSH, according to a paper read in abstract [04.25][04.29].

**What would change this.** A placebo-controlled trial counting heart events in adults under 65 with a mildly raised TSH, or in people treated by symptoms, would; on 21 November 2026 none was registered [04.74][04.75]. So would an answer to question 3, which this report lacks. So would facts a general report cannot know: existing cardiovascular disease, an LDL of 190 mg/dL or more, or an age under 65, since the event trials enrolled only older people [04.5].

**Assumptions to discount if they are wrong.** The brief assumed a US reader; in the UK routine TSH testing before a statin is no longer standard [05.12]. It also took "historical" to mean diagnosis by symptoms and signs. The standard documented at a leading US clinic about 1938 required signs and a supporting test and would not have treated on symptoms alone [02.2]. If "historical" means Broda Barnes's method of symptoms plus a low waking temperature, far more people qualify (his 40% is a stated conviction, not a measurement), and the blinded trials above are the nearest thing to a controlled test of it [02.25].

## How much thyroid function moves cholesterol

Treating overt hypothyroidism with levothyroxine lowers LDL cholesterol by a pooled 41.1 mg/dL (95% confidence interval 35.7 to 46.5) from a mean baseline of 169 mg/dL, total cholesterol by 58.4 mg/dL and apoB by 34.0 mg/dL [01.1]. The 2,305 participants are the total across the overt-hypothyroidism analyses, the figures are uncontrolled changes from baseline, and the studies disagree widely with one another (I² above 87%), so the direction is firmer than the size [01.1]. A fall of 41 from 169 is about a quarter (this report's arithmetic), close to what a low-intensity statin does [01.41]. Overt hypothyroidism is uncommon, 0.4% of 25,862 adults in a Colorado survey, so it is a major cause of raised cholesterol for the person who has it and a minor one across the population [01.2].

A cleaner test comes from adults who had their thyroid removed for cancer and stopped thyroid hormone before radioiodine treatment, each acting as their own control. Four weeks without thyroid hormone raised LDL cholesterol by 46 and 70 mg/dL (47% and 58%) and apoB by 46% to 48% in two cohorts of 48 and 52 adults; the differences are this report's arithmetic from the reported means and medians [01.25][01.27]. The rise reversed within 4 weeks of restarting in one of them (n = 48) [01.25]. A third cohort (n = 61) had no LDL measurement before withdrawal; its total cholesterol rose by 54 mg/dL and was back to baseline about 6 months after restarting [01.26]. The mechanism is the liver's clearance of LDL, which depends on thyroid hormone (a 1981 tracer study, read in abstract) [01.29]; an overactive thyroid does the reverse and lowers LDL [01.28].

In subclinical hypothyroidism the effect is small and shrinks as study quality rises. With every study design pooled, LDL falls by 11.1 mg/dL, but 143 of that review's 166 studies were not randomised [01.1]. In randomised trials the pooled effect of levothyroxine on LDL cholesterol is −0.22 mmol/L (about −8.5 mg/dL; 12 trials, 940 participants), falling to −0.09 mmol/L in trials longer than 6 months; these figures come from the review's abstract [01.7]. A post-hoc (after-the-fact) pooled analysis of the two double-blind trials in older adults (286 adults aged 65 or over, median TSH 6.44 mIU/L, 12 months) found −0.07 mmol/L (95% CI −0.15 to 0.02), not significant [01.9]. Its authors call the sample small, and only 27 participants had a TSH of 10 or more [01.9]. Uncontrolled studies overstate the effect because a mildly raised TSH often settles unaided: 39.9% of one trial's placebo group had a normal TSH within a year [01.9]. No large blinded trial with LDL as its main outcome has tested adults under 65, or anyone with a TSH of 10 to 20.

Inside the TSH reference range the effect on LDL is causal but tiny: 0.026 to 0.032 standard deviations of LDL per standard deviation of TSH in two Mendelian randomisation studies, which use inherited gene variants to test cause and effect (about 1 mg/dL by this report's conversion; both read in abstract) [01.21][01.22], and 117.0 against 119.0 mg/dL between the lower half and the top tenth of TSH in 24,765 adults without overt thyroid disease, 325 of whom had a TSH above 5.0 mIU/L [01.19].

Nearly all of these studies sort people by TSH. The only randomised trial found that enrolled people by hypothyroid symptoms with normal thyroid tests (22 patients, 100 µg thyroxine for 12 weeks, double-blind crossover) showed no change in total cholesterol: difference 0.05 mmol/L (95% CI −0.27 to 0.37), with TSH 0.66 mU/L on thyroxine against 1.77 on placebo [01.35]. It measured total cholesterol only and is too small to rule out a fall of about 10 mg/dL [01.35].

## What "healthy by the older standard" meant

There were two pre-TSH standards, and they differ about a hundredfold in how many people they class as hypothyroid. The standard at a leading US clinic about 1938 was narrow (calling it the mainstream standard is this report's characterisation): at Massachusetts General Hospital, myxoedema, the full picture of thyroid failure, was diagnosed on cardinal symptoms and signs supported by a low basal metabolic rate (BMR), and a low BMR alone was not an indication for thyroid [02.2]. Diagnosed hypothyroidism was about 0.2% of adults in a north-east England survey published in 1972, whose authors inferred a large undiagnosed pool [02.6].

The expansive standard is Broda Barnes's: a waking armpit temperature below 97.8°F plus any of a long list of complaints [02.25]. His 40% prevalence is a stated conviction, and his normal armpit range of 97.8 to 98.2°F rests on his own unreferenced readings; apart from his 1942 report comparing temperature with basal metabolism in 1,000 students, which was not opened in full for this report, the search found no published validation against a thyroid measure [02.25]. The American Thyroid Association gives 97.6°F as the 8 a.m. oral average in 148 healthy people, citing a 1992 study whose abstract reports only an overall mean of 98.2°F [02.30][02.31].

Symptoms alone separate poorly, and the older clinicians said so. In Wayne's Glasgow series of 1959, tiredness was reported by 98% of proved hypothyroid patients, 88% of euthyroid suspects (people referred who turned out not to have it) and 42% of healthy controls [02.4]. In 25,862 Colorado adults, single-symptom sensitivity was 2.9% to 28.3%, measured against TSH [02.16]. Wayne warned against diagnosing mild hypothyroidism from clinical features alone and used objective support, including serum cholesterol, which was above 300 mg/100 ml in 81% of his hypothyroid cases [02.4].

One pre-TSH series tests question 2 directly. In the 1939 Yale series, all 16 patients who recovered on desiccated thyroid (dried animal thyroid gland) had an untreated serum cholesterol of 315 to 741 mg%, and one of the 16 was very slow to recover. The 13 patients with a low metabolic rate or hypothyroid-type symptoms and lower cholesterol (149 to 300 mg%) showed little or no improvement; in 8 of the 12 with data the cholesterol fall was only 11 to 65 mg%, and in 3 of those followed closely it was transient [01.37]. The result is partly circular, since the series defined hypothyroidism in part by cholesterol and by response to thyroid [01.37].

Maintenance doses were 1 to 3 grains of desiccated thyroid (most 1½) at Massachusetts General about 1938, 0.2 to 0.4 mg of thyroxine in textbooks about 1970 (as reported by a 1973 paper), and 0.1 to 0.2 mg once titrated (adjusted) to TSH in 22 patients in 1973 [02.2][02.8]. Mean replacement doses in two single-centre titration studies were 169 µg (1974) and 112 µg (1987, 19 patients), a fall the later study, read in abstract, attributes to the 1982 reformulation of the tablet [02.11]. The usual 1938 dose is within today's labelled range for desiccated thyroid; the clearly higher doses belong to the 1950s and 1960s [02.40].

The move to TSH rested on small biochemical studies (32 patients in 1965, 22 in 1973, 19 in 1987) and no outcome trial that the search could find; none of the three papers says practice changed because of it [02.10][02.8][02.11]. The 2012 US guideline's Grade A rule against clinical scoring systems cites three rating-scale studies that the guideline itself grades as level 3 evidence; at least two of them classified patients by thyroid tests including TSH, and the third, from 1969, was not checked [02.23]. Modern studies judge symptoms against TSH, so none can show whether TSH misses disease.

Today's practice treats more people than the narrow older standard did. US retail thyroid prescriptions peaked at about 23 million in 1966 (read off a reproduced chart covering 1964 to 1988) [02.13][02.14]; 8.0% of US adults reported thyroid hormone use in 2016 (from the paper's abstract) [02.33]; and of 58,706 insured US adults who started levothyroxine in 2008 to 2018 with both TSH and T4 on record, 30.5% had normal results [02.34].

## What thyroid treatment does to heart events and deaths, and what too much does

For overt hypothyroidism no trial has compared treatment with none, since withholding it would be unethical. In a Danish register, read in abstract, untreated hypothyroid people had higher mortality than people with normal tests (hazard ratio, HR, 1.46, meaning a 46% higher rate; 95% CI 1.26 to 1.69) and treated people did not [04.50].

For subclinical hypothyroidism, the only placebo-controlled trials of levothyroxine that counted events pooled 842 people aged 65 or over (mean TSH 6.4) for a median 17 months: 44 cardiovascular events (HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.41 to 1.35) and 21 deaths (HR 1.28, 0.54 to 3.03) [04.5]. The larger trial, TRUST, was cut from a planned 3,000 participants and its report says it cannot exclude protection or harm [04.3][04.1]. Its participants had mild disease, and 62% of the 2,647 people re-tested for entry no longer had a raised TSH [04.1]. The results do not cover younger adults, a TSH above 10 or marked symptoms.

The search found no placebo-controlled trial that counted heart events or deaths for thyroid hormone in overt hypothyroidism, in adults under 65 with subclinical hypothyroidism, or in people with symptoms and a normal TSH [04.7][04.76]. On 21 November 2026 the only registered trials with events as their primary outcome were open-label, with no placebo: NCT01848171 (700 planned, ages 40 to 75, overt or subclinical hypothyroidism; status unknown, no results posted) and NCT07380854 (338 planned, people having bypass surgery, events within 4 months) [04.74][04.75].

Observational studies, whose figures here rest on abstracts, associate levothyroxine for subclinical hypothyroidism with fewer events in younger adults (HR 0.61 at ages 40 to 70; pooled mortality relative risk 0.50 under 65 to 70) but not in adults over 65 to 70 in those two sources [04.9][04.12]. Two analyses of one UK database of adults over 50 gave mortality HRs of 1.17 and 0.71, and both reported fewer cardiovascular events with levothyroxine (HR 0.91 and 0.82) [04.14][04.15]. On deaths, the answer depended on the method.

In symptom-defined patients, three blinded placebo-controlled studies (1960, n = 18; 1960, n = 20; 2001, n = 22 completing) found thyroid hormone no better than placebo [04.20][04.21][04.19]. The two 1960 studies chose patients with a low basal metabolic rate whose protein-bound iodine and radioiodine uptake, the thyroid tests of the time, were normal (one allocated by rotation, one only partly blinded); the 2001 trial required thyroid tests in the reference range. The main comparisons ran 4 to 12 weeks, and none counted events. Against treating by symptoms: every blinded test shows a large placebo response and no hormone effect, including the two that predate TSH. For it: about 60 patients is very few, and no trial tested the older method of adjusting the dose to relief over months. What supports that method is uncontrolled: Barnes's 1959 series of 80 patients reported a cholesterol fall and stated that "Sufficient time has not elapsed for significant observations on the incidence of arteriosclerosis" [04.35].

The Coronary Drug Project, a trial of cholesterol drugs, gave dextrothyroxine 6 mg a day to 1,110 men after a heart attack (placebo 2,789) and stopped the arm in late 1971 for excess deaths [04.25]. The dose was about 1.7 times the 3.5 mg that suppresses TSH, and tablets of the marketed brand later assayed at 0.50% to 2.30% levothyroxine [04.29][04.30]. On these figures the arm tested thyroid excess, not replacement. That reading is this report's: the 3.5 mg figure is a mean from 6 patients, the trial's own tablet supply was not assayed, both supporting papers were read in abstract, and the trial's 1972 report was not obtained.

The firmest finding concerns over-replacement. On treatment, cardiovascular death was HR 1.39 at a TSH below 0.1, 1.13 at 0.1 to 0.5 and 2.67 at a TSH above 20 (705,307 US veterans) [04.49]; in Tayside, Scotland, a TSH of 0.03 or below carried HR 1.37 for cardiovascular disease and 2.02 for fracture, while 0.04 to 0.4 showed no significant excess (read in abstract) [04.47]. A UK cohort of 162,369 found deaths raised below 0.1 (HR 1.18) and no link with atrial fibrillation or fracture, so the sources disagree on some outcomes [04.48]. All are observational: a low TSH on treatment also marks weight loss, frailty and deliberate suppression after thyroid cancer.

## What guidelines and labels ask for before a cholesterol drug

The Endocrine Society guideline (2020) recommends "ruling out hypothyroidism as the cause of the hyperlipidemia before treatment with lipid-lowering medications" (recommendation 6.1, strong, high-quality evidence) and suggests not treating hyperlipidaemia in overt hypothyroidism until euthyroid, meaning thyroid tests are normal (recommendation 6.3, conditional, very low quality) [05.5]. Its own text says the effect of thyroxine on long-term cardiovascular outcomes "is unknown" [05.5].

The main US cholesterol guideline is narrower. The 2018 AHA/ACC guideline was replaced on 13 March 2026. The 2026 ACC/AHA guideline tells clinicians to exclude or evaluate secondary causes in three situations: LDL cholesterol of 190 mg/dL or more (section 4.2.4.3, class 1; Table 15 lists hypothyroidism), persistently raised triglycerides (section 4.2.9; Table 23 lists uncontrolled hypothyroidism) and statin muscle symptoms (section 4.2.11; Table 24 lists hypothyroidism as a cause of the symptoms) [05.1][05.2][05.3]. It has no recommendation to test TSH before a statin: a search of the full text in an Internet Archive copy on 21 November 2026 found no mention of TSH testing (the online data supplement was not searched) [05.1].

In England, NICE moved from TSH for everyone before a statin (CG181, 2014, recommendation 1.3.13) to TSH only "in people with symptoms of underactive or overactive thyroid" (NG238 recommendation 1.5.5, published 14 December 2023; the wording was introduced in the May 2023 update of CG181) [05.11][05.12]. Its 23 July 2025 update of quality standard QS100 replaced the 2015 statements, which included "Excluding secondary causes", and none of the current statements covers secondary causes [05.13][05.14]. NG238 still says to treat secondary causes before starting a statin for primary prevention (1.6.6) and, for people who already have cardiovascular disease, "at the same time as starting statin treatment" (1.7.6) [05.12]. The European cardiology guideline has no recommendation on excluding hypothyroidism [05.17].

Current FDA statin labels list "uncontrolled hypothyroidism" as a myopathy (muscle damage) risk factor without asking for a test (Lipitor 4/2024, Crestor 09/2026, Zocor 8/2023) [05.20][05.21][05.22]; the UK Crestor label (30 March 2026) says hypothyroidism "should be treated prior to initiating therapy" and contraindicates the 40 mg dose [05.27]. In one French survey, untreated hypothyroidism carried an odds ratio of 1.71 for muscle symptoms on a statin, as reported by a later review; severe muscle breakdown is known only from case reports [05.40][05.44].

In the only practice study found, from a single centre, 49.5% of 8,795 US adults with newly found hyperlipidaemia (2003 to 2011) had TSH checked within about 6 months; 5.2% of those tested had a raised TSH and 1.7% had a TSH above 10 mIU/L [05.29]. Two population studies, read in abstract, argue against testing everyone [05.35][05.36].

A TSH test is listed at $16.80 and a lipid panel at $13.39 (Kentucky Medicaid 2026 schedule) [05.45]; after treatment of overt hypothyroidism LDL cholesterol falls by a mean 41.1 mg/dL from 168.8 mg/dL, and by 11.1 mg/dL in subclinical hypothyroidism [05.5]. Guidelines recheck TSH 4 to 8 weeks after starting or changing levothyroxine, so by inference the cholesterol recheck comes about 2 to 4 months after diagnosis [05.6]. Subtracting the mean falls leaves an average LDL near 128 mg/dL in both groups (this report's arithmetic), so treating the thyroid often does not end the cholesterol question [05.5].

For people with symptoms and a normal TSH, no current guideline offers a route to thyroid treatment ahead of a cholesterol drug; the 2014 American Thyroid Association guideline recommends strongly against levothyroxine for "nonspecific symptoms and normal biochemical indices of thyroid function" [05.50]. Every guideline defines the disease by TSH, so none of them tests the older standard.

## What the evidence did to the starting position

The brief's starting position was to be tested, not assumed. It had two stated parts and one implication.

**"Historical" means diagnosis by symptoms and signs before the TSH test.** This held in part. The older diagnosis was clinical, and the case for retiring it is weaker than its grading: the switch rested on biochemical studies of 19 to 32 patients and no outcome trial was found [02.10][02.8][02.11][02.23]. It failed in that the documented standard was never symptoms alone. The narrow version required signs and a supporting test and classed about 0.2% of adults as hypothyroid; the version that treats on symptoms and temperature is Barnes's, with no validation found [02.2][02.6][02.25]. Nor is the older standard the more generous: 8.0% of US adults reported thyroid hormone use in 2016 (abstract) [02.33].

**Bringing thyroid function to that standard before a cholesterol drug would lower cholesterol.** This held for overt hypothyroidism, where the older and the modern standard largely agree and LDL falls by about 41 mg/dL [01.1]. It failed where it was tested in people defined by symptoms: the 1939 Yale patients with ordinary cholesterol did not respond, and the 2001 trial found no cholesterol change [01.37][01.35]. It could not be tested for dose adjustment to relief over months, or for heart events and deaths, because no controlled study was found [04.19].

**TSH is pro-inflammatory, so a lower TSH than current practice has support from several angles.** The first half could not be tested, because the thread on TSH's direct effects was never written. The other threads touched the second half only from the angles they covered. For a lower TSH, both from abstracts: within the normal range a higher TSH raises LDL causally, though by about 1 mg/dL [01.21][01.22], and observational studies link treating a mildly raised TSH with fewer events below age 65 to 70 [04.9][04.12]. Against: blinded dose changes that moved TSH between 0.3 and 2.8 mU/L in 56 treated patients did not change symptoms or wellbeing (abstract) [04.23]; total cholesterol differed by 8 mg/dL between a TSH of 1.6 and a suppressed 0.3 in one withdrawal study [01.26]; and cohort data link a TSH below 0.1 with more cardiovascular death and fracture [04.49][04.47]. None of this tests whether TSH itself causes inflammation or artery disease, and no trial has compared TSH targets for heart outcomes.

## What the evidence doesn't say

Question 3 is unanswered. The thread that was to examine whether TSH itself, apart from thyroid hormone levels, drives inflammation, cholesterol production or artery disease, and what TSH level goes with the best outcomes, was not completed. This report therefore says nothing, for or against, on whether TSH is harmful in its own right. The other threads noted these leads in passing. None was examined, they point in different directions, and none is a finding.

- Injected TSH, with thyroid hormone held constant, was followed by a rise in total cholesterol from 192 to 207 mg/dL over 4 days in 27 women, with no control group (abstract) [01.45].
- When thyroid hormone is low because the pituitary has failed, so that TSH is not raised, a 1950 Yale paper found cholesterol usually normal [01.38] and a Mayo Clinic series found high lipids common (abstract) [01.44].
- The inflammation marker hs-CRP fell from 0.17 to 0.08 mg/L while TSH rose from 0.14 to 66.9 mU/L over four weeks off thyroid hormone [01.25], and was unchanged at a TSH near 99 in another [01.27].
- The 1973 TSH normal range was a 95% interval from 236 people [02.8]; in overt hypothyroidism a clinical score tracked cholesterol and reflex time but not TSH (abstract) [02.18]; and the 2014 American Thyroid Association guideline accepts that a normal TSH may not mean normal thyroid status in every tissue [05.50].

Four trials would settle most of what is open, and none has been run: levothyroxine against placebo for heart events in subclinical hypothyroidism, including adults under 65; symptom-adjusted thyroid hormone against placebo in adults with a normal TSH, for a year or more; thyroid first against statin first; and a lower against a higher TSH target within the reference range. The likely reason is money: levothyroxine is off patent, and TRUST shrank from 3,000 to 737 participants chiefly through delays in obtaining tablets and matching placebo [04.3][04.1]. The gap reflects who pays for research, not what the answer would be.

Some findings rest on thin or old evidence. The only study of how often TSH is checked covers one US centre in 2003 to 2011 [05.29]. The pre-TSH criteria come from specialist clinics, and everyday practice was probably looser [02.13].

The brief's bias note predicted the distortions that appeared. Every modern study sorts people by the test that replaced symptoms, so the pre-1975 papers were read in the original and the 1960 trials are reported beside the modern one [04.20]. Sellers sit on both sides: a thyroid-product maker funded the survey that found the most hypothyroidism, and Barnes's foundation sells his books [02.5][02.29]. The pull of current guidelines showed once, when the lipid thread expected a modest LDL benefit in subclinical hypothyroidism and revised it down on the 2026 blinded analysis [01.9]. One planned compensation was not carried out: looking for human studies that separate TSH from thyroid hormone, and for findings against the starting position such as cohorts where a higher TSH goes with longer life, was the job of the unwritten thread.

## Method note

Written on 21 November 2026 from four completed research threads of five planned, with the brief, sources.md and verification.md. Thread 03 (direct effects of TSH) was never written, so question 3 is unanswered and has no verified claims.

Verification covered 24 load-bearing claims, each checked against its source by a checker that had not written it: 13 confirmed, 11 partly (the figures matched and the wording was corrected; the corrected wording is used here), none unsupported and none inaccessible. Two further statements used in "What this means for you" were checked against their sources while this report was written and confirmed (C04.7 and C05.7), which makes 26 rows in verification.md. That file also lists the absence claims and the access limits.

Each line in Sources ends with the source's tier and how much of it was read, copied from sources.md. Of the 221 sources in that file, 107 were read in full, 108 as abstracts and 6 as search snippets; the text says so where a key statement rests on an abstract.

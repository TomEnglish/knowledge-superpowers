# Thread 01: How thyroid status changes blood lipids, how large the effect is, and how much treatment reverses it

## Question
How does thyroid status change blood lipids (LDL cholesterol, total cholesterol, apoB, Lp(a), triglycerides, HDL), by what mechanism, how large is the effect across overt hypothyroidism, subclinical hypothyroidism and low-normal function inside the reference range, and how much does thyroid hormone treatment reverse it?

Units: cholesterol in mg/dL, with mmol/L where the source uses it (1 mmol/L = 38.67 mg/dL). Percentages and conversions marked "my calculation" are mine and not the paper's. Searching and reading were done on 21 November 2026.

## Key Findings

### 1. The size of the effect depends almost entirely on the degree of thyroid failure

| Thyroid state | LDL-C difference or change | Strength of evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Overt hypothyroidism (high TSH, low T4) | +30 to +45 mg/dL against euthyroid people; about −41 mg/dL (about −24%) on treatment | Firm on direction and rough size; mostly uncontrolled before-and-after studies |
| Severe short-term hypothyroidism (hormone stopped for 4 weeks, TSH 67–99 mU/L) | +46 to +70 mg/dL (+47% to +58%), reversed on restarting | Firm; three consistent within-person cohorts |
| Subclinical hypothyroidism (high TSH, normal T4; mostly TSH 5–10) | 0 to +11 mg/dL against euthyroid people; 0 to −11 mg/dL on treatment | Contested; blinded trials show less than open ones |
| TSH inside the reference range | About 2 mg/dL between the lower half and the top tenth of TSH | Association firm, effect very small |

### 2. Overt hypothyroidism: a large, reversible rise in LDL, apoB and total cholesterol

**Cross-sectional size.** In the Colorado health-fair survey (25,862 adults, 1995), the 114 people with TSH above 5.1 mIU/L and low T4 had mean total cholesterol 251 mg/dL and LDL 170 mg/dL, against 216 and 140 mg/dL in 22,842 euthyroid people; triglycerides were 180 against 147 mg/dL and HDL 53 against 51 mg/dL [01.2]. That is +30 mg/dL of LDL (+21%, my calculation). These are crude means; I found no age- and sex-adjusted table in the text read [01.2]. In a Vienna clinic series LDL was 178 ± 70 mg/dL in 92 people with overt hypothyroidism against 134 ± 39 mg/dL in 4,886 euthyroid people [01.3].

**Treatment effect.** The largest pooled estimate is the Mayo Clinic systematic review (literature 1970 to April 2018). In overt hypothyroidism (2,305 participants; 15 to 72 studies per lipid) levothyroxine changed [01.1]:

- LDL-C by −41.1 mg/dL (95% CI −46.5 to −35.7) from a mean baseline of 169 mg/dL (about −24%);
- total cholesterol by −58.4 mg/dL (−64.7 to −52.1) from 260 mg/dL (about −22%);
- apoB by −34.0 mg/dL (−41.1 to −26.8) from 132 mg/dL (about −26%);
- triglycerides by −27.3 mg/dL (−36.6 to −17.9) from 147 mg/dL (about −18%);
- Lp(a) by −5.6 mg/dL (−9.1 to −2.1) from 27 mg/dL (about −21%);
- HDL-C by −4.1 mg/dL (−5.7 to −2.6) from 54 mg/dL.

Percentages are my calculation from the review's baselines. The abstract prints the triglyceride change as −7.25 mg/dL; the results text and confidence interval give −27.25, so the abstract figure is a misprint [01.1]. Heterogeneity was extreme (I² 87% to 96%), the figures are change from baseline with no control group, and the review did not assess publication bias [01.1]. Studies longer than 6 months showed the same change as those under 3 months [01.1].

**The effect scales with the starting level.** A 1996 pooled analysis found treatment lowered total cholesterol by 1.2 mmol/L (46 mg/dL) when the starting level was up to 8 mmol/L and by 3.4 mmol/L (131 mg/dL) when it was above 8 mmol/L [01.14]. Individual variation is wide: pre-TSH case series recorded cholesterol from 335 to 911 mg per 100 mL in untreated myxoedema [01.37][01.38].

**Natural experiment: hormone withdrawal.** Adults whose thyroid was removed for cancer stopped levothyroxine for 4 weeks before radioiodine. They are otherwise well, and each is their own control.

- Seoul, 48 patients: TSH went from 0.14 to 66.9 mU/L. LDL rose from 96.5 to 142.1 mg/dL (+47%), apoB from 71.2 to 105.7 mg/dL (+48%), total cholesterol from 178.7 to 238.7 mg/dL, triglycerides from 141 to 159 mg/dL, HDL from 54.0 to 64.8 mg/dL. Four weeks after restarting, LDL was 107.6 and apoB 74.5 mg/dL [01.25].
- Korea, 61 patients, with a pre-operative euthyroid baseline (TSH 1.6 mU/L): total cholesterol rose from 194.8 to 249.1 mg/dL (+54.2 ± 42.6) at a TSH of 82 mU/L, and was 186.8 mg/dL about 6 months after restarting. LDL was 167.7 mg/dL off treatment and 113.9 mg/dL back on it. The rise was larger with BMI of 23 or more (+61.5 against +40.2 mg/dL) [01.26].
- Debrecen, 52 patients (2026): median LDL 3.1 to 4.9 mmol/L (about +70 mg/dL, +58%), apoB 0.97 to 1.42 g/L (+46%), total cholesterol 5.0 to 7.3 mmol/L, triglycerides 1.5 to 2.0 mmol/L, HDL 1.4 to 1.8 mmol/L, at a TSH near 99 mU/L. Lp(a) did not change (32 against 33 mg/L, P = 0.63) [01.27].

Two cautions. In two of the three studies the "on treatment" value was taken at a deliberately suppressed TSH, so the swing runs from mild excess to severe deficiency [01.25][01.27]. And in the study with a true euthyroid baseline, total cholesterol at TSH 1.6 (194.8 mg/dL) and later at TSH 0.3 (186.8 mg/dL) differed by only 8 mg/dL, which suggests that pushing TSH from mid-normal to suppressed adds little lipid lowering [01.26].

**Does treatment restore lipids fully?** Nearly. A meta-analysis of 65 studies of overt hypothyroidism treated to a normal TSH found LDL 3.3 ± 1.6 mg/dL higher (P = 0.044) and total cholesterol 9.6 ± 3.6 mg/dL higher (P = 0.007) than in healthy controls [01.16]. The gap is small next to the treatment effect and comes from observational comparisons.

**Mirror image.** In 20 hyperthyroid patients studied before and after treatment, the hyperthyroid state had total and LDL cholesterol 28% lower, apoB 27% lower and Lp(a) 26% lower [01.28]. Pooled, treating overt hyperthyroidism raised LDL by 31 mg/dL [01.1].

### 3. Mechanism: thyroid hormone sets how fast the liver clears LDL

- **LDL receptor clearance is the main lever.** A 1981 tracer study found receptor-mediated LDL catabolism "markedly reduced" in a hypothyroid patient and restored by thyroxine, a reversible version of the defect in familial hypercholesterolaemia; it was one patient [01.29]. A metabolic-ward study the same year (27 hypothyroid, 13 hyperthyroid patients) concluded that thyroid hormones act on LDL catabolism independently of cholesterol synthesis, absorption or excretion [01.30]. In rodents, thyroid hormone raises LDL receptor expression through SREBP-2 [01.31] and through thyroid response elements in the receptor's own promoter [01.32].
- **PCSK9.** Hyperthyroidism lowered circulating PCSK9 by 22%. A liver-selective thyroid hormone analogue (eprotirome) given to 14 healthy volunteers for 14 days also lowered PCSK9, lipoprotein cholesterol, apoB and Lp(a) [01.28], which shows that thyroid hormone action on the liver is sufficient to produce the effect.
- **Bile acids and absorption.** The same study found a marker of bile acid synthesis 43% higher in hyperthyroidism, markers of cholesterol absorption 18% to 25% lower, and cholesterol synthesis unchanged; the authors judged the bile acid response "not critical" for LDL lowering [01.28]. The 1981 ward study found no evidence that thyroid hormones alter bile acid synthesis in man [01.30]. The CYP7A1 route is unsettled in people.
- **Triglycerides and HDL.** Reviews attribute the triglyceride rise to lower lipoprotein lipase activity and the HDL rise to lower hepatic lipase and CETP activity [01.33]. HDL goes up in overt hypothyroidism [01.25][01.27] and falls on treatment [01.1], so total cholesterol overstates the atherogenic change; LDL or apoB is the better measure.
- **Lp(a).** One study reported Lp(a) of 255 mg/L in 19 hypothyroid, 150 mg/L in 54 euthyroid and 75 mg/L in 27 hyperthyroid subjects, with a 55% fall on starting levothyroxine [01.34]. Against that, 4 weeks of profound hypothyroidism did not move Lp(a) [01.27]. Either Lp(a) responds slowly or the small older studies overstate it.

Whether TSH itself adds to these effects is thread 03's question; evidence met here is listed under Gaps.

### 4. Subclinical hypothyroidism: a small effect that shrinks as study quality rises

**Association: results conflict.**

- Colorado, TSH 5.1–10 mIU/L (n = 1,799): total cholesterol 223 against 216 mg/dL and LDL 144 against 140 mg/dL (both P < .003), unadjusted [01.2].
- Vienna: LDL 137 ± 40 mg/dL in 1,055 subclinical cases against 134 ± 39 mg/dL, not different, and no relation between TSH (4.0 to 49.0) and LDL within the subclinical group [01.3].
- US NHANES III (8,228 adults aged 40+, subclinical = TSH 6.7–14.99, n = 215): total cholesterol 226.0 against 217.1 mg/dL (P = .003) and LDL 139.7 against 136.2 mg/dL (P = .19) unadjusted. After adjustment for age, sex, race and lipid-drug use nothing remained: odds ratio for LDL above 130 mg/dL 0.89 (0.59–1.35) [01.4].
- Busselton, Australia: LDL 4.1 against 3.5 mmol/L in a subgroup with 30 subclinical cases, P = 0.024 after adjustment; total cholesterol P = 0.061 [01.5].
- A 2021 meta-analysis of 35 observational studies with TSH under 10: LDL +11.0 mg/dL (6.4–15.5), total cholesterol +12.8 mg/dL, no difference in apoB [01.6].

The honest range is 0 to about 11 mg/dL of LDL. Age and sex explain part of the crude difference, because people with a raised TSH are older and more often women [01.4].

**Treatment.**

- All designs pooled: LDL −11.1 mg/dL (−13.1 to −9.0) from 139.5 mg/dL (about −8%), total cholesterol −12.0 mg/dL, apoB −6.6 mg/dL, triglycerides −4.5 mg/dL, Lp(a) −2.0 mg/dL, HDL unchanged; 4,588 participants, I² 74% for LDL and 78% for total cholesterol. Of the review's 166 studies, 143 were non-randomised, and it gives no randomised-only or TSH-stratified estimate for this group [01.1].
- Randomised trials only: 12 trials, 940 participants, LDL −0.22 mmol/L (−0.36 to −0.09; about −8.5 mg/dL). In trials longer than 6 months the effect was −0.09 mmol/L (about −3.5 mg/dL) against −0.46 mmol/L in shorter trials (P = .006) [01.7]. An earlier review (13 studies, 247 patients) found LDL −10 mg/dL, with a smaller fall in previously untreated people than in under-treated hypothyroid patients [01.8].
- Double-blind trials: Basel, 66 women with mean TSH 11.7, 48 weeks, LDL −0.33 mmol/L (−8.2%), more when TSH exceeded 12, Lp(a) unchanged [01.10]. Newcastle, 100 patients with mean TSH 6.6, 12 weeks of 100 µg, LDL 142.9 to 131.3 mg/dL [01.11].
- **The pooled blinded data in older adults are null.** A 2026 post-hoc analysis of the TRUST and IEMO80+ placebo-controlled trials (286 adults aged 65+, median TSH 6.44 mIU/L, 44% on statins, 12 months) found LDL −0.07 mmol/L (95% CI −0.15 to 0.02), total cholesterol −0.17 mmol/L (−0.34 to 0.00) and apoB −0.03 g/L (−0.07 to 0.00), none significant after correction for multiple testing. In the 27 people with TSH of 10 or more, lipids improved at nominal significance only. Lipids were measured by NMR (baseline LDL 1.91 mmol/L), so absolute values are not interchangeable with routine laboratory LDL [01.9].
- **A warning about pooled results.** A 2022 meta-analysis in older adults reported LDL −0.54 mmol/L and total cholesterol −0.92 mmol/L. The cholesterol estimate rests on three Chinese-language trials and the LDL estimate on two of them plus one TRUST sub-study, with I² of 89%; the authors state that the lipid finding appears only once the Chinese-language trials are included [01.13]. That is five to eight times the effect in the blinded European trials of the same age group [01.9]. A 2017 meta-analysis reporting LDL −14% pooled five small trials (253 patients) [01.12]. A 2007 Cochrane review (12 trials, 350 people) found only "a trend" [01.47].

Reductions concentrate where TSH is 10 or more: in a lipid clinic, LDL fell significantly only in those with pre-treatment TSH at or above 10 mU/L [01.15], and the Basel and TRUST/IEMO80+ subgroups agree [01.10][01.9]. A 1992 grading study found total cholesterol and apoB clearly raised only in overt disease [01.43].

### 5. Low-normal function inside the reference range: real but very small

- HUNT (Norway, 30,656 adults without known thyroid disease): LDL, total and non-HDL cholesterol and triglycerides rose and HDL fell linearly across the TSH reference range (P for trend < 0.001) [01.17]. The abstract gives no magnitudes and the full text could not be obtained. The 11-year follow-up called the prospective associations "very modest and not consistent between the sexes" [01.18].
- Taiwan (24,765 adults without overt thyroid disease, 325 of them with TSH above 5.0 mIU/L): LDL 117.0 mg/dL in the lower half of TSH (0.47–1.48 mIU/L) against 119.0 mg/dL in the top tenth (above 2.93); total cholesterol 197.4 against 202.1 mg/dL; triglycerides 113.7 against 126.6 mg/dL. Crude means [01.19].
- Tehran (3,020 people euthyroid over 6 years): "statistically significant … but the effect size is not clinically significant" [01.20]. In a Dutch cohort free T4 was inversely related to LDL, standardised beta −0.068 [01.46].
- Mendelian randomisation, which tests causation through inherited variants: one standard deviation higher TSH within the normal range raised LDL by 0.026 SD in one study [01.21] and 0.032 SD in a second, with no evidence of reverse causation [01.22]; a third reported a coefficient of 0.041 on a scale it does not state clearly [01.24]. One SD higher free T4 lowered LDL by 0.072 SD in one study [01.22] and had no significant effect in another [01.24]. A fourth found the clearest effects on triglyceride-rich lipoproteins and an odds ratio for coronary disease of 1.03 (0.99–1.07) per SD of TSH [01.23].

With a population SD of LDL of 33 to 39 mg/dL [01.3][01.4][01.19], 0.026 to 0.032 SD is about 1 mg/dL of LDL per SD of TSH (my calculation). The causal direction is supported; the size is about one per cent of a typical LDL.

**Experiments inside the range.** In 110 people referred for cholesterol above 7.5 mmol/L with a normal TSH, randomised to 25 or 50 µg thyroxine for 2 months with no placebo arm, total and LDL cholesterol fell significantly only in those with TSH 2.0–4.0 given 50 µg, and not in the antibody-negative members of that group [01.36]. In 42 newly treated hypothyroid patients allocated to a TSH target of 0.4–2.0 or 2.0–4.0 mIU/L for 12 months, lipids did not differ between targets [01.39]. A double-blind crossover of three levothyroxine doses in 56 patients (mean TSH 2.8, 1.0 and 0.3 mU/L) reported "expected changes" in markers of thyroid hormone action; I could not obtain the full text with the cholesterol figures [01.40].

### 6. People defined by symptoms with a normal TSH: almost no data

Almost every study above sorts people by TSH. I found one randomised trial that enrolled by symptoms. In Glasgow, 22 patients with at least three hypothyroid symptoms for 6 months and thyroid tests within the reference range, and 19 healthy controls, took 100 µg thyroxine and placebo for 12 weeks each, double-blind. In the patients TSH was 0.66 mU/L on thyroxine against 1.77 on placebo, and total cholesterol was unchanged: 6.33 against 6.27 mmol/L, difference 0.05 (95% CI −0.27 to 0.37). In controls the difference was −0.21 mmol/L (−0.46 to 0.04) [01.35]. Only total cholesterol was measured, and 22 people cannot exclude a fall of up to 0.27 mmol/L (10 mg/dL).

Two pre-TSH primary sources address the same group. They cut against the idea that symptom-based diagnosis found a pool of cholesterol-responsive patients whom TSH testing later missed.

- **Yale, 1939** (29 patients; diagnosis by clinical signs, basal metabolic rate and response to desiccated thyroid). The 16 who recovered, on 2½ grains a day or less, had untreated cholesterol of 335–603 mg% (classical myxoedema) or 315–741 mg% (atypical cases); treatment brought it to 275 mg% or below in the classical group. The 13 with a low metabolic rate or hypothyroid-type symptoms who did not improve had "much lower" cholesterol, all but one under 259 mg% in the low-metabolic-rate group. In them thyroid lowered cholesterol by only 11 to 65 mg% in 8 of 12, many needed 2 grains or more, and in three followed closely the fall "was only transient". The authors concluded that in people without thyroid deficiency "some regulatory reaction may soon permit the cholesterol to return to its normal level", and that a cholesterol below 275 mg% "practically excludes hypothyroidism" [01.37].
- **Yale, 1950** (125 patients with thyroid disorders, judged against serum precipitable iodine). The same group withdrew the stronger claim: high cholesterol "favors the diagnosis of hypothyroidism, but a low cholesterol does not exclude this condition", and its level "is not correlated with the degree of thyroid deficiency". Cholesterol was below the normal mean of 194 mg% on 20 occasions with a hypothyroid-range iodine, nine of them in patients with malnutrition or serious illness. In one of two patients with schizophrenia who were made myxoedematous with thiouracil, cholesterol rose from about 200 to 306 mg%, then fell to 151 mg% on 0.06 g of thyroid daily, a dose too small to correct metabolic rate; a second subject behaved the same way. Cholesterol began to rise before the iodine level reached the hypothyroid range, which the authors read as "minor degrees of thyroid deficiency" [01.38].

So pre-TSH clinicians at this centre used a high cholesterol to tell true hypothyroidism from the larger group with tiredness, cold intolerance and a low metabolic rate, and in their hands the symptomatic patients with ordinary cholesterol did not respond.

### 7. Major or minor cause of raised cholesterol?

Both, depending on the question. For a person with overt hypothyroidism it is a major cause: correcting it lowers LDL by about a quarter on average [01.1], and by 24% and 32% from the peak in the two withdrawal studies that re-measured LDL after restarting (my calculation from [01.25][01.26]). For comparison, a low-intensity statin lowers LDL by under 30% and a moderate-intensity statin by 30% to 49% [01.41]. Across everyone with raised cholesterol it is a minor cause: overt hypothyroidism was 0.4% of the Colorado sample [01.2], hypothyroidism of any degree was 4.2% of 1,509 lipid-clinic referrals with 11 new overt cases [01.15], and the common milder states move LDL by a few mg/dL at most.

## What this means for the person's situation

There is no individual. This is for any adult offered a cholesterol-lowering drug or wondering whether thyroid has been ruled out. It is evidence, not medical advice.

- **A TSH result is worth having before the decision**, because the one state in which thyroid moves LDL a lot is cheap to detect. If TSH is clearly raised with a low T4, expect LDL to fall by roughly 40 mg/dL, more if it starts very high, once thyroid hormone is replaced, and expect a repeat lipid panel to look different. The change is visible within 4 weeks [01.25] and no larger after 6 months than at 3 [01.1].
- **If TSH is mildly raised (about 5 to 10) with a normal T4, thyroid treatment will not substitute for a lipid drug.** The best blinded evidence, in people over 65, shows no significant LDL change [01.9]; pooled trials suggest at most about 9 mg/dL, less in longer trials [01.7]. Whether younger adults respond more has not been tested in a large blinded trial.
- **If TSH is normal, thyroid status explains very little of a raised LDL**: about 2 mg/dL between low-normal and high-normal TSH in the largest cohort with figures [01.19]. The one blinded trial in people with symptoms and normal tests found no cholesterol change on thyroxine [01.35].
- **Lp(a) should not be expected to fall with thyroid treatment** unless hypothyroidism is overt, and even then the evidence conflicts [01.1][01.27].

**Dependence on assumptions.** The brief assumes "historical" means diagnosis by symptoms and signs. The Yale papers show that pre-TSH practice at one leading US centre also used basal metabolic rate, serum cholesterol itself, and the response to a trial of thyroid [01.37][01.38]. If the historical standard includes a raised cholesterol, then "thyroid treatment lowers cholesterol in historically diagnosed patients" is partly true by definition. If it means symptoms alone, the 1939 non-responders and the 2001 trial are the only direct evidence found, and both are negative and small. The US-first assumption does not change any finding here: the studies come from the US, Europe and East Asia and agree in direction.

## Load-bearing claims
- C01.1: Treating overt hypothyroidism with levothyroxine lowers LDL-C by a pooled 41.1 mg/dL (95% CI 35.7–46.5) from a mean baseline of 169 mg/dL, total cholesterol by 58.4 mg/dL and apoB by 34.0 mg/dL (2,305 participants across the overt-hypothyroidism analyses; uncontrolled change from baseline; I² above 87%). [01.1]
- C01.2: Four weeks without thyroid hormone raised LDL-C by 46 and 70 mg/dL (+47% and +58%) and apoB by 46% to 48% in two cohorts of thyroidectomised adults (n = 48 and 52; the differences are my arithmetic from the reported means and medians). The rise reversed within 4 weeks of restarting in one of them (n = 48). A third cohort (n = 61) had no pre-withdrawal LDL-C; its total cholesterol rose by 54 mg/dL and was back to baseline about 6 months after restarting. [01.25][01.26][01.27]
- C01.3: In subclinical hypothyroidism the pooled randomised-trial effect of levothyroxine on LDL-C is −0.22 mmol/L (about −8.5 mg/dL; 12 trials, 940 participants), falling to −0.09 mmol/L in trials longer than 6 months; a post-hoc pooled analysis of the two double-blind trials in older adults (286 adults aged 65+, median TSH 6.44 mIU/L, 12 months) found −0.07 mmol/L (95% CI −0.15 to 0.02), not significant. [01.7][01.9]
- C01.4: Inside the TSH reference range the effect on LDL is causal but tiny: 0.026 to 0.032 SD of LDL per SD of TSH in Mendelian randomisation (about 1 mg/dL, my conversion), and 117.0 against 119.0 mg/dL between the lower half and the top tenth of TSH in 24,765 adults without overt thyroid disease (325 with TSH above 5.0 mIU/L). [01.21][01.22][01.19]
- C01.5: The only randomised trial found that enrolled people by hypothyroid symptoms with normal thyroid tests (22 patients, 100 µg thyroxine for 12 weeks, double-blind crossover) showed no change in total cholesterol: difference 0.05 mmol/L (95% CI −0.27 to 0.37), with TSH 0.66 mU/L on thyroxine against 1.77 on placebo. [01.35]
- C01.6: In the 1939 Yale series, all 16 patients who recovered on desiccated thyroid had untreated serum cholesterol of 315 to 741 mg%, while 13 patients with a low metabolic rate or hypothyroid-type symptoms and lower cholesterol (149 to 300 mg%) showed little or no improvement; in 8 of the 12 with data the cholesterol fall was only 11 to 65 mg%, and in 3 of those followed closely it was transient. One of the 16 counted as recovered was very slow to recover. [01.37]

## Confidence Notes

**Strong.** Overt and severe hypothyroidism raise LDL, apoB and total cholesterol substantially, and treatment reverses it. Four independent kinds of evidence agree: cross-sectional surveys, pooled before-and-after studies, within-person withdrawal studies from two countries, and pre-TSH case series using different assays. The mirror effect in hyperthyroidism and its reproduction by a liver-selective hormone analogue support a causal reading. The direction is firmer than the exact size: the pooled figures have I² above 87% and no control arms [01.1].

**Thin or contested.** (a) Subclinical hypothyroidism: open and small trials show more than blinded ones. The pooled blinded analysis is post hoc, its authors call the sample small, in people aged 65 and over, 44% on statins, with only 27 people at TSH 10 or more, so it does not settle younger adults or higher TSH [01.9]. In the Tromsø placebo-controlled trial (64 people) only apoB fell in the whole group [01.42]. (b) Lp(a): small, old, assay-dependent studies against one negative 2026 withdrawal study. (c) Normal-range magnitudes: the largest cohort was read in abstract only, and my mg/dL conversion of the Mendelian randomisation results is approximate. (d) Symptom-defined patients: one 22-person trial and one 1939 series.

**Distortions named in the brief, as they appeared.**
- Small-study and publication bias in subclinical trials is visible: the review pooling the most studies did not test for it [01.1], and one positive meta-analysis depends on trials found only in Chinese databases [01.13].
- Every modern study here except [01.35] defines its groups by TSH. The pre-TSH sources define hypothyroidism partly by cholesterol and by response to thyroid, so their finding that cholesterol falls on treatment is partly circular [01.37].
- Regression to the mean: 39.9% of the TRUST placebo group had a normal TSH within a year [01.9], so uncontrolled before-and-after estimates in subclinical hypothyroidism are biased upward.
- Funding was checked only for [01.9] (public grants; Merck KGaA supplied drug and placebo). I met no sponsor-run lipid trial of levothyroxine.
- Model default: I began expecting a modest, real LDL benefit in subclinical hypothyroidism, the usual guideline-era summary. The 2026 blinded analysis moved the estimate down.

**Method notes.** I checked the figures for [01.4], [01.9], [01.19], [01.25], [01.26], [01.27], [01.28], [01.35], [01.37] and [01.38] against the raw text or tables. Figures for [01.1] and [01.2] came through a page-extraction tool that summarises with a small model: Table 3 of [01.2] was extracted twice with identical results, and [01.1] matched its abstract apart from the misprint noted. Bibliographic details were checked against Europe PMC. No page contained instructions addressed to an automated reader. One open-access index requires an email address; a lookup with a placeholder address was blocked and not repeated, and no identifying detail was sent to any service.

- Mason, Hunt and Hurxthal 1930 (N Engl J Med) and Hurxthal 1934 (Arch Intern Med), the first large cholesterol series in myxoedema; not found online in readable form.

**Not studied, as far as I could find.**
- No blinded trial has enrolled adults by hypothyroid symptoms with a normal TSH and measured LDL, apoB or Lp(a). Likely reasons: no agreed definition, no sponsor, and professional risk in treating. Searched Europe PMC and the web for thyroxine or levothyroxine with "symptoms of hypothyroidism", "reference range", "euthyroid", "normal TSH", cholesterol and LDL.
- No large blinded trial of levothyroxine with LDL or apoB as the primary outcome in adults under 65 with TSH 4.5–10, or in anyone with TSH 10–20.
- No trial comparing "treat thyroid first, then re-measure lipids" with "start the lipid drug now".

**The trial that would settle it.** Double-blind and placebo-controlled, in adults whose LDL is above a treatment threshold, in three strata (TSH 10–20; TSH 4.5–10; TSH 2.5–4.5 with a symptom score above a set level), levothyroxine titrated to a TSH near 1, at least 12 months, primary outcome apoB, Lp(a) by an isoform-insensitive assay.

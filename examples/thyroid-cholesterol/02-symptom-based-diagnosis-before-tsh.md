# Thread 02: How hypothyroidism was diagnosed and treated before the TSH test

## Question
How was hypothyroidism diagnosed and treated before the TSH test? Which symptoms and signs were used, which supporting tests (basal metabolic rate, serum cholesterol, reflex time, basal temperature, protein-bound iodine, a trial of treatment), what doses of desiccated thyroid or thyroxine were given, and how many people did that standard treat compared with today's? What changed when TSH testing arrived (roughly 1965 to 1990), and on what evidence? How well do symptoms and signs identify hypothyroidism? What do Broda Barnes and similar writers claim, and what do the original papers show?

## Key Findings

### 1. There were two pre-TSH standards, and they differ about a hundredfold
**The mainstream clinical standard (about 1920 to 1970) was narrow.** James Means of the Massachusetts General Hospital thyroid clinic based the diagnosis of myxoedema on the cardinal symptoms and signs (puffy face, thick dry skin, brittle hair, chilliness, mental sluggishness) plus a demonstrated low basal metabolic rate (BMR) [02.2] (p. 287). He described a separate group with a low BMR and no myxoedema, often nervous and fidgety, who frequently got worse on thyroid, and concluded: "low metabolism is not per se an indication for thyroid administration" [02.2] (pp. 229-230). Walter Palmer of Columbia agreed in 1934: patients with fatigue, nervousness, cold sensitivity and menstrual disorders, whose BMR was seldom below -20%, were the group in which thyroid treatment was most disappointing; a small share, usually with a BMR of -25% or lower, improved [02.3] (p. 55). In 1972 the Newcastle thyroid unit put diagnosed hypothyroidism at about 0.2% of adults in north-east England, with a similar figure from northern Finland [02.6] (p. 291).

**The expansive standard treated many more.** Broda Barnes diagnosed on a waking armpit temperature below 97.8°F plus any of a long list of complaints and claimed 40% of Americans were affected [02.25] (pp. 15, 31, 46). Paul Starr's group in Los Angeles used a chemical test, not symptoms: a serum protein-bound iodine (PBI) of 4.0 µg/100 ml or less, found in 340 of 2,807 men (12%) and 52 of 610 executives (8.5%), from which they estimated that 6% to 9% of middle-aged American men were hypothyroid [02.5] (pp. 285-286). Their follow-up reported 7.1% of more than 5,000 subjects, which the Newcastle group called surprisingly high [02.6] (p. 291) [02.42]. A third strand, called metabolic insufficiency, treated tired patients who had a low BMR with triiodothyronine [02.4] (p. 84).

Any test of "healthy by the older standard" must say which of these it means. The mainstream version classed well under 1% of adults as hypothyroid; Barnes's version, about 40%.

### 2. Criteria of the mainstream standard, with figures
The best-documented study is Wayne's Glasgow series (Lumleian Lectures, 1959). One observer examined 150 people referred for suspected hypothyroidism; the final diagnosis came from special tests and from progress with or without treatment. One hundred proved hypothyroid and 50 euthyroid; 55 healthy controls were added (mean age 55, 82% women) [02.4] (p. 79).

| Feature (Wayne, Table V) | Proved hypothyroid (n=100) | Suspected but euthyroid (n=50) | Healthy controls (n=55) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Physical tiredness | 98% | 88% | 42% |
| Mental lethargy | 85% | 26% | 9% |
| Cold intolerance | 95% | 64% | 16% |
| Decreased sweating | 68% | 24% | 11% |
| Weight gain | 76% | 34% | 38% |
| Slow movements (sign) | 73% | 22% | 7% |
| Coarse skin (sign) | 70% | 14% | 5% |
| Periorbital puffiness (sign) | 86% | 46% | 11% |
| Hoarse voice (sign) | 87% | 42% | 15% |
| Pulse below 60 | 8% | 2% | 0% |

Wayne's own conclusion was that tiredness has diagnostic value only because nearly every hypothyroid patient has it, and that the figures show the danger of diagnosing subclinical or masked hypothyroidism from clinical features alone [02.4] (p. 79).

Supporting tests, judged against the final diagnosis:
- **BMR** agreed with the final diagnosis in 77% of cases under strict technique [02.4] (p. 82). Palmer put technical error at 10 to 20 percentage points, with myxoedema usually at -30% or below and -40% taken as total loss of thyroid function [02.3] (pp. 53-54).
- **Serum cholesterol** was above 300 mg/100 ml in 81% of hypothyroid cases and below 250 in only 6%. Wayne noted that other conditions raise it and that its fall on thyroxine could serve as a further diagnostic test [02.4] (p. 83). In Starr's working men, cholesterol exceeded 275 mg/100 ml in 39 of 140 with a low PBI and 48 of 138 with a high-normal PBI, so a low PBI on its own did not go with higher cholesterol [02.5] (pp. 288-289).
- **PBI**: Wayne reports a series in which only 9 of 82 hypothyroid patients had a value above 3 µg/100 ml [02.4] (p. 82). The accepted normal range was 4.0 to 8.0, and iodine from X-ray contrast, medicines and antiseptics invalidated the test for weeks to years [02.5] (pp. 286-287).
- **Electrocardiogram**: read blind, 79% of 60 hypothyroid tracings were characteristic or strongly suggestive [02.4] (p. 83).
- **Reaction and reflex time**: reaction time was prolonged in 46 of 50 hypothyroid patients and shortened with treatment [02.4] (p. 80). I could not open Lawson's 1958 ankle-reflex paper or Nuttall and Doe's 1964 evaluation; a 1997 study gives the ankle reflex a sensitivity of 77% [02.19].
- **Trial of treatment**: Palmer called it probably the best way to separate true hypothyroidism from a low BMR of other cause, judged by clinical change and BMR together [02.3] (p. 55).

Wayne's department built a weighted index of 15 symptoms and 11 signs. All but one of 55 definite cases scored above +15 and every healthy control below +5, but 19% of doubtful cases fell in between [02.4] (p. 81). Its published successor, the Billewicz index (1969), used 14 items weighted most heavily on a slow ankle jerk and slow movements; +25 or more indicated hypothyroidism and -30 or less excluded it [02.43][02.19]. The index usually called Wayne's is his thyrotoxicosis index [02.19].

### 3. Doses
- **Massachusetts General Hospital, revision pages I read as 1938**: start fully developed myxoedema on 3 grains a day, halve when symptoms clear, then adjust. Maintenance ran from 1 to 3 grains and most patients needed 1½ grains [02.2] (p. 300). Brands differed: 1½ grains of USP thyroid equalled 1 grain of Parke-Davis and 5 grains of Burroughs Wellcome, which was labelled by fresh-gland weight [02.2] (p. 301). That explains reports such as Palmer's patient on 6 grains of the Burroughs Wellcome product [02.3] (p. 54).
- **1950s trials**: desiccated thyroid 1.5 to 3.5 grains (up to 210 mg), thyroxine 200 to 350 µg, triiodothyronine 75 to 175 µg, with angina and heart failure on the last [02.1].
- **1960**: Wayne equated 1 grain (65 mg) of thyroid with 0.1 mg of thyroxine and said he had seen lives shortened by over-enthusiastic dosing [02.4] (pp. 83-84).
- **About 1970**: major textbooks recommended 0.2 to 0.4 mg of thyroxine a day, with 0.3 mg as the accepted average [02.8] (p. 131).
- **Barnes, 1976**: start adults on 1 grain and raise by up to 1 grain every two months; the usual adult dose was 2 grains, sometimes 3, rarely 4; after a heart attack, start at ½ grain with a ceiling of 2 [02.25] (pp. 194-195, 283-285). He attributed earlier deaths to starting doses of 4 to 30 grains [02.25] (pp. 187-188).
- **Today's label**: Armour Thyroid supplies 38 µg thyroxine and 9 µg triiodothyronine per grain (60 mg); most patients are said to need 60 to 120 mg a day; the label states the drug has not been found safe and effective by the FDA (checked 21 November 2026) [02.40].

The 1938 Boston dose and Barnes's 2 grains sit inside today's labelled range. The clearly higher doses belong to the 1950s and 1960s, when treatment was titrated to BMR or PBI.

### 4. What changed with TSH, and on what evidence
- **1965**: Utiger's radioimmunoassay separated all 32 patients with primary hypothyroidism (18 to 180 mµg/ml) from 103 euthyroid subjects (undetectable to 15); TSH was undetectable in 45% of the euthyroid [02.10]. The Newcastle assay (1971) found TSH raised in every primary hypothyroid patient tested and named its main use as diagnosing minor degrees of hypothyroidism [02.9]. TSH was thus validated against cases the old standard had already diagnosed.
- **1973**: the Newcastle group redefined the disease by grade. Overt cases had obvious features. Mild cases (19 patients) had fatigue or one isolated symptom, routine tests mostly within the normal range and a raised TSH, and were reported to improve on treatment, with no control group. Subclinical cases had a raised TSH and no symptoms [02.7]. Their TSH normal range was a 95% interval from 236 people: below 0.5 to 3.5 µU/ml in men and to 3.75 in women [02.8] (p. 132).
- **Dose**: in 22 patients, the smallest thyroxine dose that normalised TSH was 0.1 mg in 13, 0.15 mg in 6 and 0.2 mg in 3; all became symptom-free, with scores matching controls. The authors concluded that conventional doses often cause subclinical hyperthyroidism. Their evidence was a flat TSH response to TRH and one report of raised red-cell sodium on 0.3 mg, not clinical harm, and a normal TSH was itself their criterion of adequate treatment [02.8]. A US study found a mean TSH-normalising dose of 169 ± 66 µg in 1974 and a repeat in 19 patients found 112 ± 19 µg in 1987. The 1987 authors attributed the fall partly to a 1982 reformulation: absorption was 81% against an earlier estimate of 48%, and the old tablet's strength had been overstated [02.11]. Microgram doses before and after are therefore not directly comparable.
- **Preparation**: of 38 patients on desiccated thyroid 90 to 240 mg, 36 had high triiodothyronine, and 6 of 40 had thyrotoxic symptoms that eased on switching to thyroxine [02.12]. Potency varied until the US Pharmacopeia changed its standard from iodine content to hormone content in 1985 [02.1].
- **Missing**: I found no trial from 1965 to 1990 comparing symptom-guided with TSH-guided diagnosis or dosing on clinical outcomes. The history by McAninch and Bianco cites none; it cites a later unblinded study favouring higher doses and a blinded trial that did not confirm it [02.1].

### 5. How many people were treated, then and now
- **Prescriptions**: US retail pharmacies dispensed about 16.5 million thyroid prescriptions in 1964, a peak of about 23 million in 1966, 18 to 20 million through the 1970s and about 22 million in 1988 (read from the published chart) [02.13][02.14]. Four of five were for natural thyroid in 1965 [02.1] and one in four in 1988; obesity was the second most common diagnosis attached [02.13].
- **People**: in a study published in 1989, 6.9% of 2,575 older adults (10.0% of women) took thyroid hormone; 12% of the women and 29% of the men had no proper indication (obesity and high cholesterol among the reasons), and 37% of the definitely hypothyroid still had a TSH above 10 mU/L [02.15]. At a Colorado health fair in 1995, 5.9% of 25,862 people took thyroid medication and 39.9% of them had an abnormal TSH [02.16].
- **Today**: adult use rose from 4.1% in 1997 to 8.0% in 2016 [02.33]. Levothyroxine accounted for about 82.4 million prescriptions and 19.0 million patients in 2024 (checked 21 November 2026) [02.35]. Of 58,706 adults starting it in 2008 to 2018, 8.4% had overt hypothyroidism, 61.0% subclinical and 30.5% normal thyroid tests [02.34].
- **Blood-test prevalence**: 4.6% of the US population in 1988 to 1994 (0.3% overt, 4.3% subclinical) [02.36]; in the Whickham survey 8% of women and 3% of men had subclinical hypothyroidism, and spontaneous hypothyroidism runs at 1% to 2% [02.37].

By my arithmetic, with approximate populations, that is about 0.12 prescriptions per person in 1966 and 0.24 in 2024; sources and prescription lengths differ, so the comparison is rough. Present practice treats more people than the mainstream pre-TSH standard did, not fewer.

### 6. How well symptoms and signs identify hypothyroidism
- Against TSH above 20 with low thyroxine, 76 new patients reported 30.2% of listed symptoms and 147 controls 16.5%; seven or more changed symptoms gave a likelihood ratio of 8.7; area under the curve 0.72 [02.17].
- In Colorado, single-symptom sensitivities were 2.9% to 28.3% and positive predictive values 8% to 12%; overt hypothyroid subjects reported 16.6% of symptoms and euthyroid subjects 12.1% [02.16].
- In Denmark (140 new overt cases, 560 controls), tiredness occurred in 81% of cases and no single symptom had a high likelihood ratio; 10 to 13 symptoms gave 6.29 [02.20]. Symptoms worked well in young men (area under curve 0.91) and poorly in older women (0.64) [02.21].
- Zulewski's 12-item score in 332 women identified 62% of overt cases (Billewicz 42%). Classical signs appeared mainly in severe disease with low triiodothyronine. Within overt cases the score tracked ankle-reflex time (r = 0.76) and cholesterol (r = 0.60) but not TSH (r = 0.01) [02.18][02.19].
- In 130 outpatients no physical sign had a positive likelihood ratio above 3.88 [02.22].

Every modern study here uses blood tests as the reference, so none can show whether TSH misses disease. Wayne's study has the mirror weakness: the examiner's findings fed the final diagnosis. The 2012 US guideline grades its rule against clinical scoring systems as Grade A, best evidence level 1; the scoring studies it cites carry its own level 3, and its level-1 citation is the Colorado survey [02.23].

### 7. Barnes, Peat and the originals
- **Temperature test**: the 1942 paper is paywalled; I read only its opening lines, which define basal temperature [02.24]. In 1976 Barnes described it as a study of 1,000 college students (his method then used the mouth), followed by a wartime comparison in 1,000 soldiers that moved the test to the armpit. He says the normal range of 97.8 to 98.2°F rests on thousands of his own readings and that there is no risk of overdose while the reading stays at or below 98.2; he concedes that starvation, pituitary and adrenal deficiency also lower it [02.25] (pp. 43-46). I found no published validation against any thyroid measure. The American Thyroid Association gives 97.6°F as the 8 a.m. oral average in 148 healthy adults, citing a 1992 study whose abstract reports only an overall mean of 98.2°F [02.30][02.31], and a systematic review puts the normal armpit range at 35.5 to 37.0°C (95.9 to 98.6°F) [02.32]. Barnes's lower limit therefore sits inside the normal range; my inference is that it would label a large share of healthy people.
- **The 40% figure** is a stated conviction built from Starr's 10% and Barnes's 20% among Denver students in 1941, with antibiotics said to have let hypothyroid children survive [02.25] (pp. 31-32). The second book says about half the population [02.26] (p. 39). Barnes cites Wayne's 77% for BMR as proof that laboratory tests miss cases, without Wayne's warning about symptoms [02.25] (p. 47).
- **Graz and Framingham**: Barnes says he reviewed 70,000 Graz autopsy records from 1930 to 1970 and that heart attacks fell 75% from 1939 to 1945 because tuberculosis killed the same people first [02.26] (pp. 2-3). He reports 4 new heart cases against 72 expected among 1,569 thyroid-treated patients, using Framingham as the comparison [02.26] (pp. 38-39). Both comparisons are uncontrolled; analysis belongs to thread 04.
- **Peat** writes that estimates of hypothyroidism fell from 30% or 40% to 5% between 1940 and 1950 because of the PBI test, and that 1 to 2 grains was the common starting dose [02.27][02.28]. He gives no source for the first claim and the originals do not support it: mainstream texts of the 1930s restricted the diagnosis to myxoedema [02.2][02.3], Barnes's 40% dates from 1976, and the PBI surveys produced higher estimates than clinical diagnosis did [02.5][02.6]. The dose statement is broadly consistent with the originals.
- **Interests**: the Barnes foundation sells memberships, conference recordings and books [02.29]. Starr's survey was supported by Warner-Chilcott Laboratories [02.5] (p. 285), which made the thyroid product Proloid [02.14] (p. 66). Peat's site sold his books and newsletter [02.27].
- **Tests of the expansive standard**: Wayne asked for a double-blind trial of metabolic insufficiency [02.4] (p. 84). PubMed lists two controlled studies from 1960, one titled as a failure to demonstrate the syndrome; neither has an abstract, so this is a lead to check [02.41]. A 2001 crossover trial in 22 symptomatic patients with normal tests found thyroxine 100 µg no better than placebo [02.39].

## What this means for the person's situation
For an adult offered a cholesterol-lowering drug, the older standard supports one clear step. Its practitioners treated a high cholesterol as a reason to suspect an underactive thyroid and used the fall in cholesterol on treatment to confirm it [02.4]. Asking whether thyroid function has been measured before a lipid drug is started is consistent with both the old and the new standard.

For someone with symptoms and a normal TSH, the findings cut two ways. The mainstream pre-TSH standard would not have treated on tiredness, weight gain or feeling cold: these were reported by 88%, 34% and 64% of people suspected of hypothyroidism who turned out not to have it [02.4]. It looked for specific signs (slowed movement, coarse skin, puffiness round the eyes, hoarse voice, slow-relaxing reflexes), wanted objective support, and used a monitored trial for doubtful cases [02.2][02.3][02.4]. Its leading clinicians warned against treating a low metabolic rate or symptoms alone [02.2][02.4]. The version that treats on symptoms plus a low morning temperature is Barnes's, and its threshold falls inside the range of healthy people [02.25][02.31][02.32]. Against that, the evidence used to retire symptoms and signs is weaker than its grading suggests, because TSH was the reference in every study [02.16][02.23].

Dose matters as much as diagnosis. The doses of the 1950s and 1960s were later judged excessive, on blood-test evidence [02.8]; Barnes's doses are close to today's label for desiccated thyroid [02.25][02.40]. Harms of over-replacement are covered in thread 04.

Dependence on assumptions: prescribing and prevalence figures are US and UK only. If "historical standard" is taken to mean Barnes's criteria and not the mainstream textbooks, the share of people classed as hypothyroid changes from under 1% to about 40%, and the conclusions change with it. This is general information, not individual advice.

## Load-bearing claims
- C02.1: The pre-TSH standard at a leading US clinic about 1938 was narrow (calling it the mainstream standard is my characterisation): myxoedema was diagnosed on cardinal symptoms and signs supported by a low BMR, a low BMR alone was not an indication for thyroid, and diagnosed hypothyroidism was about 0.2% of adults in a north-east England survey published in 1972, whose authors inferred a large undiagnosed pool. [02.2][02.6]
- C02.2: Symptoms alone separate poorly: tiredness was reported by 98% of proved hypothyroid patients, 88% of euthyroid suspects and 42% of healthy controls in 1959; in 25,862 Colorado adults single-symptom sensitivity was 2.9% to 28.3%, measured against TSH. [02.4][02.16]
- C02.3: Maintenance doses were 1 to 3 grains of desiccated thyroid (most 1½) at Massachusetts General about 1938, 0.2 to 0.4 mg thyroxine in textbooks about 1970, and 0.1 to 0.2 mg once titrated to TSH in 22 patients in 1973; mean replacement doses in two single-centre titration studies were 169 µg (1974) and 112 µg (1987, 19 patients), a fall the later study attributes to the 1982 reformulation of the tablet. The textbook range is as reported by the 1973 paper. [02.2][02.8][02.11]
- C02.4: Barnes's 40% prevalence is a stated conviction, and his normal armpit range of 97.8 to 98.2°F rests on his own unreferenced readings; apart from his 1942 report comparing temperature with basal metabolism in 1,000 students, I found no published validation against a thyroid measure. The American Thyroid Association gives 97.6°F as the 8 a.m. oral average in 148 healthy people, citing a 1992 study whose abstract reports only an overall mean of 98.2°F. [02.25][02.30][02.31]
- C02.5: US retail thyroid prescriptions peaked at about 23 million in 1966 (read off a reproduced chart covering 1964 to 1988); 8.0% of US adults reported thyroid hormone use in 2016; and of 58,706 insured US adults who started levothyroxine in 2008 to 2018 with both TSH and T4 on record, 30.5% had normal results. [02.13][02.14][02.33][02.34]
- C02.6: The move to TSH rested on small biochemical studies (32 patients in 1965, 22 in 1973, 19 in 1987) and no outcome trial that I could find; none of the three papers says practice changed because of it. The 2012 guideline's Grade A rule against clinical scoring systems cites three rating-scale studies that the guideline itself grades as level 3 evidence; at least two of them classified patients by thyroid tests including TSH (the third, from 1969, was not checked). [02.10][02.8][02.11][02.23]

## Confidence Notes
**Strong.** Criteria, test accuracies and doses in Findings 1 to 3 come from originals opened as page scans: Means, Palmer, Wayne, Lowrey and Starr, and the Newcastle papers. The scans were read by OCR, so the figures that matter (Wayne's Table V and index, Means's dose, the 0.2% and 7.1% prevalence figures, the funding note, the 1973 TSH range) were checked against the page images. The Barnes claims are from his own books.

**Thinner.**
- "Read: full" means I opened the full text and read the sections named in the source line, not always every page.
- Kaufman 1991 is an abstract plus a chart reproduced in a thesis; counts are read off the chart to about ±0.5 million.
- Dating Means's myxoedema pages to 1938 is my reading of the footer mark 938; the text cites work from 1934, so it is later than the 1929 first printing.
- Means and Palmer describe two elite US clinics and Wayne one Scottish unit. Ordinary practice was probably looser, as the obesity prescribing in [02.13] and the unindicated use in [02.15] suggest.
- Barnes 1942 was not opened; its content is known here only from his retelling 34 years later. His books were read as Internet Archive OCR text, with page numbers taken from running heads.
- Per-person prescription rates use approximate populations from background knowledge (196 million in 1966, 340 million in 2024).

**Possible distortion.**
- Reference standards are circular in both directions (Finding 6).
- The judgement that older doses were excessive rests on pituitary tests, the measure that replaced the older targets.
- The secondary history [02.1] is written by proponents of combination therapy, a live dispute.
- Sellers appear on both sides: a thyroid-product maker funded the 1957 survey that found the most hypothyroidism, and the Barnes foundation and Peat sold publications.
- That Barnes's cut-off would label many healthy people is my inference from separate temperature studies, not a direct test.

No page contained instructions addressed to the researcher.

The likely reason is that hypothyroidism has been defined by TSH since the 1970s and thyroid hormone has no sponsor. The trial that would settle it: a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in symptomatic adults with normal TSH, selected by a hypothyroid-range clinical score plus a slow ankle reflex and raised LDL, with dose titration and with LDL or apoB, symptoms and safety as outcomes.

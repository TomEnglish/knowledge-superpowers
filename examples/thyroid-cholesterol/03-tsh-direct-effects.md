# Does TSH itself cause harm? TSH actions outside the thyroid, and the TSH level linked to the best outcomes

## Question
Does TSH itself, apart from the thyroid hormone levels that come with it, drive inflammation, cholesterol production or artery disease? What is the evidence in cells, in animals and in people, for and against? And what TSH level goes with the best outcomes (heart events, fractures, deaths) in the general population?

## Key Findings

### Summary verdict on starting position (b)
The claim that "a body of research shows TSH is pro-inflammatory, so keeping TSH lower than modern practice does has support from several angles" holds only in part. The first half has real laboratory support: cell and mouse studies, mostly from one group in Jinan, China, report that TSH acting on TSH receptors raises cholesterol synthesis in liver cells and inflammation in macrophages and fat cells [03.1][03.2][03.5][03.6][03.9]. The human evidence that separates TSH from thyroid hormone is small, short, uncontrolled and inconsistent [03.12][03.13][03.14][03.15][03.16]. The second half, that a lower TSH would be better, is contradicted by the largest human datasets. In 134,346 adults the lowest risk of heart disease and death was at TSH about 1.9 to 2.9 mIU/L, and the bottom fifth of TSH carried slightly higher risk [03.33]. Genetic studies link lifelong lower TSH to more atrial fibrillation and heart attacks, not fewer [03.42][03.43], and to slightly higher overall mortality [03.44]. Every human finding here is about TSH as a marker of thyroid status. None shows TSH to be a cause of harm in people.

### Cell and animal evidence that TSH acts on liver, fat, macrophages and vessels (case for)
- **Liver cholesterol synthesis.** In 2010 the Jinan group (Shandong Provincial Hospital; senior author Zhao Jiajun) reported that TSH raised HMG-CoA reductase, the enzyme statins block, in liver cells through the TSH receptor and the cAMP/PKA/CREB pathway. In thyroidectomised rats kept on fixed thyroid hormone, injected TSH raised liver HMG-CoA reductase [03.1]. In 2015 the same group reported that bovine TSH lowered the inactivating phosphorylation of HMG-CoA reductase via AMPK in mouse liver cells and HepG2 cells. TSH-receptor knockout mice (5 wild type vs 4 knockout) had total cholesterol of 1.90 vs 2.93 mmol/L with similar free T4 and TSH [03.2]. In the same year the group reported that TSH suppresses bile-acid synthesis (the main route by which the body gets rid of cholesterol) through SREBP-2/HNF-4α/CYP7A1, in thyroidectomised rats on thyroxine and in knockout and siRNA mice [03.3].
- **PCSK9 and LDL receptors.** A separate Nanjing group (senior author Zhou Hongwen) reported that recombinant human TSH raised PCSK9 and lowered LDL receptors in HepG2 cells, an effect blocked by the TSH-receptor-blocking antibody K1-70. Serum PCSK9 was 151 vs 85 ng/mL in subclinical hypothyroidism vs matched euthyroid people [03.5]. This is the closest thing to an independent replication of a liver effect, though it measured a different mechanism, in a hepatoma cell line.
- **Macrophages and atherosclerosis in mice.** The Jinan group's 2019 paper in the Journal of Experimental Medicine found that ApoE-knockout mice lacking the TSH receptor developed less atherosclerosis, less vascular inflammation and fewer plaque macrophages. They were kept euthyroid with thyroxine in the diet (about 15 µg T4 per kg of diet), and serum T3, T4 and TSH matched controls. The same result was seen when the receptor was deleted only in myeloid cells. In cultured macrophages TSH at 0.2 to 5 ng/mL switched on the MAPK and NF-κB inflammatory pathways and raised cytokines [03.6]. A 2022 correction fixed an siRNA sequence in the methods and did not change the conclusions [03.7]. The group has since extended the macrophage work to insulin resistance (Zhang H 2025, Exp Mol Med; title seen only, a lead) [03.47].
- **Fat cells.** An Ottawa group (Sorisky) reported that TSH raised IL-6 release 2.6-fold from mouse 3T3-L1 adipocytes at 0.01 µM and above [03.9], and 1.9-fold from human subcutaneous (not omental) adipocytes [03.10], through NF-κB [03.11].

**How these doses compare with human blood.** Three points.
- The Jinan liver experiments used bovine TSH at 1 and 4 µM [03.2], and the Ottawa adipocyte work used up to 1 µM, with effects starting at 0.01 µM [03.9]. Circulating TSH is reported in mU/L, not molar units. After a standard 0.9 mg injection of recombinant human TSH, peak serum TSH averages 116 mU/L [03.46]. My own rough conversion, which assumes a molecular mass near 28 kDa and is not taken from any source here, puts 1 µM in the order of tens of thousands of times a normal human TSH concentration, and still far above the peak after the therapeutic injection. Treat this as an estimate. It does show that the liver-cell doses were far beyond physiological levels.
- The macrophage doses in [03.6] (0.2 to 5 ng/mL of mouse TSH) are much closer to the physiological range, though potencies differ between species.
- Bovine pituitary TSH preparations are not pure recombinant protein. None of the liver papers I read addressed contamination.

**How much TSH receptor is there outside the thyroid?** The Human Protein Atlas consensus RNA data give 257 nTPM in thyroid, against 0.4 in liver, 0.5 in adipose tissue, 1.0 in bone marrow and 0.3 in heart muscle (checked 21 November 2026) [03.19]. That is roughly 500-fold less in liver than in thyroid. A review by an independent bone and thyroid researcher lists many extrathyroidal sites of the receptor but says their physiological importance "in many cases remains a subject of controversy" [03.20].

**Replication.**
- The liver HMG-CoA reductase and bile-acid findings come from one group [03.1][03.2][03.3]. I found no independent laboratory that has reproduced them in primary human liver cells or in a hepatocyte-only TSH-receptor knockout (searched PubMed for "TSH receptor hepatocyte-specific knockout cholesterol": no results).
- The macrophage atherosclerosis model has not, as far as I found, been repeated by another group.
- The adipocyte IL-6 effect comes from one group [03.9][03.10][03.11].

### Human designs that separate TSH from thyroid hormone (the most informative evidence)
**1. Recombinant human TSH in thyroidectomised patients on fixed levothyroxine.** Thyroid cancer survivors on a steady levothyroxine dose receive two 0.9 mg injections. TSH rises for a few days while free T4 stays flat. All the studies below are small and uncontrolled (before-after, no placebo injection), last days, and start from a low TSH.
- *Lipids, Sassari:* 27 premenopausal women. TSH went from 0.43 to 12.3 mU/L on day 5, with free T4 unchanged at 1.10 ng/dL. Total cholesterol rose from 192 to 207 mg/dL, triglycerides from 72 to 85 mg/dL and non-HDL cholesterol from 130 to 143 mg/dL. LDL went from 115 to 126 mg/dL (p = 0.066) [03.15].
- *Lipids, Rotterdam (independent group):* 82 patients, about 72 hours after the second injection. ApoB went from 0.90 to 0.92 g/L, Lp(a) from 0.21 to 0.24 g/L and triglycerides from 1.98 to 2.50 mmol/L; HDL fell from 0.98 to 0.81 mmol/L. T3 fell from 1.91 to 1.81 nmol/L, and the lipid changes were "most closely associated with the decrease in T3" [03.16]. The authors read this as TSH acting on thyroid hormone metabolism. Taken together, the two studies show a small, short-lived lipid shift that may run through T3 and not through a direct TSH effect on the liver.
- *Inflammation and endothelium (contradictory):*
  - Pisa, 24 patients: rhTSH lowered flow-mediated dilation from 8.9% to 7.4% and raised IL-6 and TNF-α [03.12].
  - L'Aquila, 20 patients: adhesion molecules (sICAM-1 155.9 to 183.6 ng/mL; sE-selectin 74.8 to 91.4 ng/mL) and platelet-activation markers rose. The authors called the TSH levels "supraphysiological" [03.14].
  - Naples, 34 patients: flow-mediated dilation *rose* from 10.2% to 15.6% at 6 hours, and VCAM-1, TNF-α, IL-6 and hs-CRP were *unchanged* [03.13].
  - Ottawa, 5 patients: serum IL-6 rose threefold on days 3 and 4 [03.10].
  
  The acute inflammatory response to injected TSH is therefore inconsistent between groups, and none of these studies measured an outcome.
- *Thyroid hormone withdrawal (from thread 01's sources):* hs-CRP did not rise when TSH climbed to about 67 to 99 mU/L for 4 weeks. Thread 01 reports this; I did not re-read those papers.

**2. Central (pituitary) hypothyroidism: low thyroid hormone with low or normal TSH.** If TSH drove cholesterol, these patients should have little rise in LDL. At the Mayo Clinic, hyperlipidaemia was "commonly associated with both primary and secondary hypothyroidism" (268 primary and 27 secondary cases), it improved with thyroid hormone, and the secondary group had the more atherogenic profile because of lower HDL [03.17]. In 90 Polish patients with central hypothyroidism, those with low TSH had *more* low HDL than those with normal TSH (44% vs 23%), along with more advanced pituitary disease [03.18]. These data point to thyroid hormone deficiency, not TSH, as the main driver of the lipid change. The caveat is that other pituitary hormone deficiencies confound both studies.

**3. Genetics (Mendelian randomisation).** Genetic variants that set TSH slightly higher or lower for life are not TSH-only instruments: most act through the pituitary-thyroid set point, so they cannot by themselves separate TSH from thyroid hormone. With that limit:
- Genetically higher TSH did not raise CRP. The reverse, CRP raising TSH, was supported [03.25]. This is evidence against TSH as a driver of systemic inflammation.
- Genetically lower TSH was linked to more atrial fibrillation: OR 1.15 per SD lower TSH in [03.42], and OR 0.88 per SD *higher* TSH in [03.41].
- In 105,224 Copenhagen adults, genetically lower TSH was linked to more heart attack (1.35 per SD lower TSH, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.71), atrial fibrillation (1.28) and aortic stenosis (1.53) [03.43].
- In 384,344 UK Biobank participants, 1 SD higher genetically predicted TSH went with lower all-cause mortality (OR 0.972, 0.948 to 0.996), driven mainly by respiratory deaths [03.44].

**4. Long-term TSH suppression.** In 524 thyroid cancer survivors on suppressive levothyroxine, cardiovascular death rose 3.08-fold (1.32 to 7.21) per 10-fold *fall* in TSH [03.45]. This is the opposite of what "lower TSH is protective" predicts. The harm is usually attributed to thyroid hormone excess, which this design cannot separate from low TSH. Thread 04 covers treated patients in detail.

### Bone: the reverse claim, that TSH protects bone
- Abe 2003 (Cell; Mount Sinai, Zaidi and Davies) proposed that TSH directly restrains bone turnover, and that even a 50% fall in receptor expression caused osteoporosis in mice [03.21].
- An independent London group (Williams and Bassett) showed in thyroid-hormone-receptor mutant mice that bone loss in thyrotoxicosis "is independent of circulating TSH" and is mediated by thyroid hormone acting through TRα [03.22].
- In women given rhTSH, a bone-resorption marker fell briefly, only in postmenopausal women and only for a few days [03.23].
- A Mendelian randomisation study found no effect of genetically lower TSH on bone density at the hip (0.003 SD per SD, 95% CI −0.053 to 0.048) or spine, and no association with 706 variants at the receptor gene [03.24].

The direct-bone-protection idea therefore has weak human support. The human association between low TSH and fracture is still real (next section), most likely through thyroid hormone.

### TSH level and outcomes in the general population
**Coronary heart disease.**
- In HUNT (Norway; 17,311 women and 8,002 men; median follow-up 8.3 years), women at TSH 2.5 to 3.5 mIU/L had higher coronary death than women at 0.50 to 1.4 (HR 1.69, 1.14 to 2.52). No significant trend was seen in men [03.27]. This is the strongest single cohort supporting a lower target.
- The pooled analysis of 14 cohorts, which includes HUNT, did not confirm it. In 55,412 people with TSH 0.45 to 4.49 and no known thyroid or cardiovascular disease, the HR per 1 mIU/L was 0.97 (0.90 to 1.04) for coronary death (54,649 people analysed) and 1.00 (0.97 to 1.03) for coronary events (48,875 people in 10 cohorts). The top category (3.50 to 4.49) did not differ from the bottom (0.45 to 1.49), and results were similar by sex and age [03.28].
- Above the range, coronary events were clearly more frequent only at TSH 10 to 19.9 (HR 1.89, 1.28 to 2.80; HR 1.00 at 4.5 to 6.9). Coronary deaths were already raised at 7.0 to 9.9 (HR 1.42, 1.03 to 1.95) and at 10 to 19.9 (HR 1.58, 1.10 to 2.27) [03.29].

**Overall risk across 26 cohorts (the most direct answer to the "best TSH" question).**
- Thyroid Studies Collaboration, 134,346 adults, 2023: the lowest risk of the composite of cardiovascular events and death was at the 60th to 80th percentiles of TSH (median 1.90 to 2.90 mIU/L). Compared with that band, the lowest fifth of TSH had HR 1.07 (1.02 to 1.12) for the composite and 1.09 (1.05 to 1.14) for all-cause death [03.33].
- Free T4 showed a J-shape, with the most risk at the top fifth: HR 1.34 for death and 1.57 for cardiovascular death [03.33].
- The study reports no funding [03.33].

**Mortality in single cohorts (the results conflict).**
- US NHANES III, 12,584 adults followed for 19.1 years: the *high*-normal TSH tertile had higher all-cause death (HR 1.27, 1.10 to 1.47) than the middle tertile. The *low*-normal tertile also had raised all-cause death, a U-shape [03.30].
- US Cardiovascular Health Study, 2,843 older adults: higher TSH went with *lower* mortality and less dementia, and higher free T4 with more atrial fibrillation, heart failure and death [03.31].
- Rotterdam, 7,785 adults: at age 50, men and women in the top TSH tertile lived 2.0 and 1.4 years longer than those in the bottom tertile. The top free T4 tertile lived 3.2 to 3.5 years less [03.32]. One author's research centre is funded by Nestlé Nutrition and Metagenics [03.32].

**Atrial fibrillation and fracture inside the normal range.**
- Atrial fibrillation tracked free T4, not TSH (30,085 people; top vs bottom free T4 quartile HR 1.45) [03.35].
- Hip fracture was more common at the low end of the normal TSH range: HR 1.25 (1.05 to 1.49) at TSH 0.45 to 0.99 and 1.19 at 1.00 to 1.49, against 3.50 to 4.49 (56,835 people) [03.34]. A "keep TSH at the bottom of the range" policy would move people toward the band with more hip fractures.

**The very old.**
- Leiden 85-plus (599 people aged 85): each SD (2.71 mIU/L) higher TSH went with lower mortality (HR 0.77, 0.63 to 0.94) [03.36].
- Ashkenazi centenarians had higher median TSH (1.97 mIU/L) than younger controls (1.55) [03.37]. Their offspring had higher TSH than controls (1.68 vs 1.50), with heritability 0.33, and carried TSH-receptor gene variants linked to higher TSH more often [03.38].
- Offspring of long-lived Dutch families had higher TSH and lower free T4 [03.39].
- A pooled analysis of four cohorts aged 80 and over (2,116 people) found no 5-year survival difference for any thyroid category, including subclinical hypothyroidism (HR 0.9, 0.7 to 1.2) [03.40].

**Population inflammation.** A meta-analysis of 93 mostly small case-control studies found higher CRP in subclinical and overt hypothyroidism, and a fall in CRP with levothyroxine in overt disease (SMD −0.30) [03.26]. These studies cannot separate TSH from thyroid hormone, and the heterogeneity was large. Set against the genetic result [03.25], CRP looks like a marker of hypothyroid state, or of illness raising TSH, and not of TSH driving inflammation.

**Human cross-sectional data from the mechanistic group.** The Jinan group also reports that TSH relates to carotid plaque in 1,103 people [03.6], to lower bile acids independent of thyroid hormone [03.4][03.3], and to cholesterol through a "direct" path-analysis effect in 3,664 people [03.8]. These are cross-sectional associations from the same group that produced the mechanism. A statistical "direct effect" in a path model cannot rule out confounding by tissue thyroid status, which serum free T4 measures imperfectly.

## What this means for the person's situation
For an adult offered a cholesterol-lowering drug, or wondering whether thyroid has been properly ruled out:

- **TSH has no proven harm of its own in people.** The laboratory case is real but rests mainly on one group, often at very high doses, and on mice. The human tests that hold thyroid hormone fixed show changes that are small, last days and do not agree with each other. No human study shows that TSH itself causes heart disease.
- **Aiming for a low TSH is not supported and may cost something.** Across 134,346 adults the best outcomes were at TSH about 1.9 to 2.9 mIU/L. Lower TSH went with slightly more death, more hip fracture and, genetically, more atrial fibrillation and heart attack [03.33][03.34][03.43], but none of these separates TSH from thyroid hormone: the lowest fifth in [03.33] included people with hyperthyroidism and on thyroid hormone, the hip fracture result was not adjusted for free T4, and TSH-associated gene variants reflect T3-determined thyroid function (thread 06). Asking for thyroid hormone in order to push a normal TSH toward 0.5 to 1 has no outcome evidence behind it, and the observational and genetic evidence points the other way.
- **A clearly raised TSH still matters,** but as a sign of low thyroid hormone. Coronary risk rose clearly at TSH 10 and above [03.29]. That is the range where checking and treating thyroid before a lipid decision makes sense (see threads 01, 04 and 05).
- **Older people may do better with a TSH at or above the middle of the range.** In people over 80, a mildly raised TSH was not linked to worse survival [03.36][03.40].

**Dependence on assumptions.**
- If "lower TSH" in starting position (b) means lower than about 4 to 10 mIU/L, the usual treatment thresholds for raised TSH, the evidence above partly supports it at TSH 10 and above [03.29]. If it means pushing TSH into the low-normal or below-normal range, the evidence is against it.
- If the reader's interest is people with hypothyroid symptoms and a normal TSH (the pre-TSH-era standard of starting position (a)), this thread cannot answer it. None of the outcome cohorts selected people by symptoms.
- The cohorts are mostly European, US and East Asian. US guidance is treated as primary (an assumption), but nothing here differs by country.

## Load-bearing claims
- C03.1: In 134,346 adults across 26 cohorts, TSH at the 60th to 80th percentiles (median 1.90 to 2.90 mIU/L) had the lowest risk of cardiovascular events and death. The lowest fifth of TSH had HR 1.09 (1.05 to 1.14) for all-cause death against that band, adjusted for age and sex only. [03.33]
- C03.2: In 55,412 people with TSH 0.45 to 4.49 mIU/L and no known thyroid or cardiovascular disease, each 1 mIU/L higher TSH carried HR 0.97 (0.90 to 1.04) for coronary death (54,649 analysed) and 1.00 (0.97 to 1.03) for coronary events (48,875 analysed, from 10 of the 14 cohorts). [03.28]
- C03.3: In thyroidectomised patients on fixed levothyroxine, injected TSH raised total cholesterol from 192 to 207 mg/dL in 27 women over 4 days, and apoB from 0.90 to 0.92 g/L in 82 patients, a change tied to a fall in T3. [03.15][03.16]
- C03.4: In the Copenhagen General Population Study (105,224 people), the genetic risk estimate per SD of lower TSH was 1.35 (1.06 to 1.71) for myocardial infarction and 1.28 (1.08 to 1.52) for atrial fibrillation; the abstract does not say whether these are odds ratios or how many people the genetic analysis used. [03.43]
- C03.5: Euthyroid adults with TSH 0.45 to 0.99 mIU/L had more hip fractures than those at 3.50 to 4.49 (HR 1.25, 1.05 to 1.49; 56,835 people). [03.34]
- C03.6: The liver-cell experiments that support TSH-driven cholesterol synthesis used bovine TSH at 1 and 4 µM for 48 hours, and TSH-receptor RNA in human liver is 0.4 nTPM against 257 in thyroid. [03.2][03.19]

## Confidence Notes
- **Firm:** In the general population, TSH inside the reference range is not associated with coronary heart disease [03.28]. Low-normal and low TSH go with more hip fracture, more atrial fibrillation and slightly higher mortality [03.33][03.34][03.43], as associations; thread 06 shows these do not isolate TSH from thyroid hormone. These findings come from large pooled and genetic datasets with independent groups (Bern, Rotterdam, Copenhagen, Cambridge, Stockholm).
- **Thin:** All human evidence that separates TSH from thyroid hormone. The rhTSH studies have 5 to 82 participants, no control injection, follow-up of days, and baseline TSH that was suppressed or low. Their inflammatory results contradict each other. A real but small acute lipid effect may run through lower T3 [03.16].
- **Concentrated and possibly distorted:** The liver mechanism comes almost entirely from one Jinan laboratory [03.1][03.2][03.3][03.6][03.8], which also produced the supporting human cross-sectional analyses. That is the circular-validation pattern the brief warned about. Some liver experiments used doses far above blood levels. My molar comparison is my own arithmetic with an assumed molecular mass and is not sourced. I did not check that group's funding.
- **What genetics can and cannot show:** Mendelian randomisation tests lifelong set-point differences. Most TSH variants also shift thyroid hormone, so a genetic "TSH effect" cannot show that TSH itself is the cause. That cuts both ways, against the "lower is better" claim and against reading the genetic results as proof that TSH protects.
- **Contradiction recorded, not resolved:** HUNT found higher coronary death in women with higher-normal TSH [03.27]; the 14-cohort pooled analysis that includes HUNT did not [03.28]. NHANES III found raised mortality at both ends [03.30], while three cohorts of older people found higher TSH protective [03.31][03.32][03.36]. Age is the likeliest reason: the "protective" signal is strongest in the old.
- **Funding and interests noted:** Xu 2023 had no funding, and some authors had fees from levothyroxine makers [03.33]. Bano 2017 has a Nestlé/Metagenics-funded centre [03.32]. Dalila 2023 authors declared lipid-drug industry ties [03.43]. None of these interests obviously favours one answer here.
- Most papers were read in abstract only. Two mechanistic papers and the rhTSH lipid paper were read in full [03.2][03.6][03.15]. No web page contained instructions addressed to an automated reader.

## Gaps
- **Looked for and not found:**
  - An independent replication of TSH-driven liver HMG-CoA reductase or bile-acid effects, in primary human liver cells or in hepatocyte-specific receptor knockouts. Searched PubMed with "TSH receptor hepatocyte-specific knockout cholesterol" and Europe PMC with related terms.
  - Lipid data in people with loss-of-function TSH-receptor mutations, who have high TSH but cannot respond to it. Searched "TSH receptor loss-of-function mutation lipid profile cholesterol" and "TSH resistance TSHR mutation cholesterol": no results. This is the cleanest natural experiment for the question and appears unstudied in lipid terms.
  - Lipid or outcome data in TSH-secreting pituitary adenomas (high TSH with high thyroid hormone). Searched PubMed: no results.
  - Any controlled (placebo-injection) rhTSH study.
- **Not studied at all:** Any outcome trial testing a TSH target within the reference range in untreated people, and any trial of a TSH-receptor blocker for heart disease. Likely reason: no sponsor for the first. The second needs drug development, and TSH-receptor antagonists are being developed for Graves' disease, not heart disease.
- **The trial that would settle the causal question:** In levothyroxine-treated, thyroidectomised adults, randomise to repeated rhTSH or placebo injections for 3 to 6 months at fixed free T4 and T3, measuring apoB, Lp(a), hs-CRP, IL-6 and carotid inflammation (FDG-PET). Separately, a large Mendelian randomisation study using variants that change TSH without changing free T4, if such variants can be found.
- **Not pursued:** thyroid eye disease as a model of inflammation driven through the TSH receptor (it is driven by stimulating antibodies and IGF-1 receptor crosstalk, not by TSH). The CRP-TSH relationship was examined only through [03.25] and [03.26].

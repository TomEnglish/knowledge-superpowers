# Disputes and conflicts: the sceptic case, the mainstream reply, and what each side has conceded

## Question
What is the strongest version of the sceptic case that cholesterol lowering is oversold for lifespan, and what is the strongest mainstream reply? The thread covers trial funding, access to patient-level data and whether it has changed, how benefits are presented, how trials are conducted, the claim that results changed after the 2004 and 2005 registration rules, conflicts of interest on both sides, the main public disputes, media coverage and stopping treatment, and where each side is weakest.

## Key Findings

### The dispute is three disputes
Both camps run together claims about the numbers, about how they are presented, and about motive. On the numbers, the sides are closer than their language suggests: a 2022 meta-analysis of 21 trials, whose authors include the author of a published reappraisal of the lipid hypothesis, found that statins lowered death from any cause by 9% in relative terms and 0.8 percentage points in absolute terms in the 19 trials that reported it [04.12][04.17]. On presentation, the critics have the better of several points. On motive, neither side's accusations survive checking: a BMJ panel found no misconduct by the critics it examined [04.6], and the High Court dismissed a newspaper's public-interest defence for articles it found had accused two critics of knowingly lying [04.43].

### 1. Who funds and runs the trials
- **What is agreed.** The Cholesterol Treatment Trialists' (CTT) Collaboration states that the statin trials it pools "were funded by the pharmaceutical industry, as well as by charities and government organisations", and that the Collaboration itself, funded by a heart charity and public research councils, "has not received grant funding from industry" [04.1][04.2]. Its coordinating unit at Oxford declares industry research grants to the university and a staff policy of taking no personal payments from industry [04.1][04.5].
- **Sceptic's strongest point.** A Cochrane methodology review of 75 papers found industry-sponsored studies more often reported favourable efficacy results (risk ratio 1.27, 95% CI 1.17 to 1.37) and favourable conclusions (1.34, 1.19 to 1.51), a bias "that cannot be explained by standard 'Risk of bias' assessments" [04.31]. In a study of 192 trials comparing a statin with another drug, among those funded by industry, funding by the test drug's maker was associated with results favouring that drug (odds ratio 20.16, 95% CI 4.37 to 92.98) [04.32].
- **Mainstream's strongest point.** A network meta-analysis of 183 statin trials found no systematic difference in LDL lowering between industry-sponsored and other trials (difference 1.77 mg/dL, 95% credible interval -11.12 to 7.66) [04.33]. That answers the charge for a laboratory measure, not for how clinical events or causes of death are classified, the issue in the FOURIER dispute (section 4).

### 2. Who can see the patient-level data
- **The accusation (2014).** The BMJ's panel noted that patient-level data "are held in confidence by the investigators" and "strongly" recommended release [04.6]. In 2015 the BMJ wrote to the principal investigators of 32 major statin trials; seven replied, all "potentially willing" to share. The CTT confirmed its analyses had covered only cause-specific mortality, major vascular events and cancers, and that trialists agreed in 2013 to pool adverse-event data [04.7]. In 2016 the BMJ's editor asked England's chief medical officer to fund an independent review [04.8].
- **The data holder's statement.** The CTT's data policy, archived on 16 February 2026, and the data-sharing statement in its Lancet paper of 14 February 2026 agree: data were provided "on the understanding that they would be used only for the purpose of the CTT meta-analyses and would not be released to others", and requests go "directly to the data custodians of each trial". Outside researchers may propose analyses for the Secretariat to run [04.1][04.2]; the 2022 and 2024 papers use the same wording [04.4][04.53].
- **What has changed.** (a) The adverse-event analyses now exist: muscle symptoms (2022), diabetes (2024), and all effects listed on product labels (2026) [04.1][04.4][04.53]. (b) A CTT Independent Oversight Panel first met on 11 October 2017; its terms of reference include scrutinising outside requests and seeking "an external statistical audit of particular analyses if required" [04.3]. (c) Regulators have become a route: critics obtained the FOURIER clinical study report from Health Canada in 2020 [04.10], and documents for 85 statin trials from the EMA and Health Canada [04.27].
- **What has not.** I found no re-analysis of the pooled patient-level statin data by a group independent of the trialists. The review the chief medical officer commissioned from the Academy of Medical Sciences (2017) made 12 general recommendations; the commentary I read describes no re-analysis of statin data [04.51]. A 2020 attempt to obtain statin approval data from European regulators found "no easily accessible archive" [04.48].
- **A limit on "independent".** The oversight panel was "chosen from among people who have not previously been involved in CTT" [04.3]. One of its six members co-authored the 2016 Lancet review led by the CTT's head, and two others are thanked in it for comments on drafts [04.3][04.5]. That is overlap, not evidence of bias; nor is it arm's-length audit.

### 3. How benefits are presented
- **Relative against absolute risk.** Critics say relative reductions amplify "the trivial beneficial effects of statins" [04.19]. The mainstream's flagship review does give absolute figures: lowering LDL by 2 mmol/L for 5 years in 10,000 people prevents major vascular events in about 1,000 with existing vascular disease and about 500 at increased risk without it [04.5]. And relative risk is the right scale for comparing trials because absolute reductions depend on baseline risk and follow-up, while "for patients and clinicians, absolute reduction in the risk of events is the more important metric" [04.22]. Both sides accept that a patient needs the absolute figure. The live complaint is which one: the mainstream headline counts vascular events, not deaths.
- **Combined endpoints and death from any cause.** Critics hold that "the ultimate measure of efficacy is total mortality" and that combined endpoints give equal weight to a hospital admission and a death [04.16]. The mainstream answer is explicit: all-cause mortality is "the composite outcome of death from all causes combined", so cause-specific analyses "are likely to be more sensitive"; statins show no effect on non-vascular death, so effects on all-cause mortality "reflect the observed reductions in vascular mortality" [04.5]. That review puts the reduction in vascular mortality at 12% per mmol/L, against about a quarter for vascular events [04.5]. The CTT's public home page (archived 6 October 2026) gives no figure for death from any cause, and attaches "about one fifth" per mmol/L to vascular events and vascular mortality together, which does not match the 12% [04.52]; I did not resolve why.
- **Scaling per unit of LDL lowered.** Critics report no clear trial-level relation between the size of LDL reduction and mortality [04.12][04.16][04.18]. The reply is that comparing whole trials with different populations cannot test this, risks the ecological fallacy, and treats non-significant results as evidence of no effect [04.22]. The critics' own meta-regression was reported as "inconclusive", not negative [04.12].
- **Life gained.** A Danish analysis of survival curves from 11 trials found median postponement of death of 3.2 days (primary prevention) and 4.1 days (secondary prevention) within trials lasting 2.0 to 6.1 years [04.13]; the same group's later analysis of 16 trials gave 12.6 days [04.14]. The authors, who are not statin sceptics (the senior author declares fees from three statin makers), state the limits: the figure covers only the trial period and assumes the gain is shared equally [04.13]. The mainstream counter is a lifetime model: for a 50-year-old man at average UK risk, a treatment cutting cardiovascular death by 30% gives a mean gain of 7 months, with 93% gaining nothing and 7% gaining a mean of 99 months [04.15]. One figure is measured over about five years; the other is modelled over a lifetime on an assumed effect.

### 4. How trials are conducted
- **Run-in periods.** The BMJ panel recorded the critics' point that people with early side effects were excluded during run-in, and quoted the Heart Protection Study's own report that this "could raise questions about the generalisability of the safety analyses" [04.6]. The reply: about half the large trials used a placebo run-in, which cannot hide drug effects, and "a few" an active one [04.5]; in pooled data the first-year excess of muscle symptoms did not differ by type of run-in [04.4].
- **How side effects are recorded.** Regulator-held documents for 23 placebo-controlled statin trials (median 175 participants) show only one used a proactive method to ask about muscle symptoms [04.27]. The CTT concedes "considerable heterogeneity" in how muscle symptoms were ascertained [04.4]. The reply: under-counting applies to both arms of a blinded trial, and two trials built to ask found no difference: in SAMSON (60 people who had abandoned statins) daily symptom scores were 16.3 on statin, 15.4 on placebo and 8.0 on no tablet [04.28]; in StatinWISE (200 people, 151 analysed) the statin-minus-placebo difference was -0.11 on a 0 to 10 scale (95% CI -0.36 to 0.14) [04.29].
- **The numbers each side now holds.** The BMJ articles' claim that side effects occur in 18 to 20% of users was withdrawn on 15 May 2014 as a misreading of an uncontrolled study [04.6]. The 2016 mainstream review said statins have "little effect" on ordinary muscle pain and put any symptomatic excess at up to 0.5 to 1.0% over five years [04.5]; the 2022 patient-level analysis found 27.1% on statin against 26.6% on placebo reporting muscle pain or weakness, an excess of 11 per 1,000 person-years in the first year and none clearly thereafter [04.4]. Pooled statin intolerance is 9.1% overall, but 4.9% in randomised trials against 17% in cohort studies [04.30].
- **Early stopping.** Trials stopped early for benefit report larger effects than similar trials run to completion (ratio of relative risks 0.71, 95% CI 0.65 to 0.77) [04.26]. JUPITER was stopped at a median 1.9 years; critics argued this inflated its result [04.24][04.25].
- **Cause-of-death classification: FOURIER.** Critics re-read the death narratives in the regulatory report and found that for 360 of 870 deaths the trial committee's cause differed from the local investigator's; after their re-adjudication the relative risk of cardiovascular death was 1.20 (95% CI 0.95 to 1.51) against the published hazard ratio of 1.05. Total deaths were 444 on evolocumab and 426 on placebo in both versions. They report unanswered emails to the lead investigator [04.10]. The investigators replied that the re-adjudicators used one summary document not meant for adjudication, that an unblinded validation step "irreparably taints the process", that the main change was a rise of more than 50% in "undetermined" deaths, and that the FDA audited the original adjudication without concern [04.11]. The reply does not contest the total death counts. The critics conclude that trials should be sized for all-cause mortality [04.10], the point most relevant to this project.

### 5. The claim that results changed after 2004 and 2005
- **The claim.** After new trial rules in 2005, "statin trials conducted after 2005 have failed to demonstrate a consistent mortality benefit" [04.16]; "since 2005, claims of benefit from statin trials have virtually disappeared" [04.18]; of 29 cholesterol-lowering trials reported after the rules, "only 2 ... reported a mortality benefit, while nearly two-thirds reported no cardiovascular benefit at all" [04.17].
- **What the rule said.** One critic paper says the rules "specified that all trial data had to be made public" [04.18]. The journal editors' statement of September 2004 required less: registration in a public registry, with stated outcomes, as a condition of publication, for trials starting enrolment after 1 July 2005 [04.20].
- **What supports it.** Among 55 large trials funded by the US National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, 57% published before 2000 showed benefit on the primary outcome against 8% after; prior registration was "strongly associated" with null results [04.21].
- **What undermines it.** The 29-trial table [04.17] is a vote count by statistical significance. It mixes diet trials (two, dated 2013 and 2016, I recall as re-analyses of trials run decades earlier; thread 02 should confirm), trials in dialysis and heart failure, high-dose against low-dose comparisons, and non-statin drugs. Ten of the 29 met their cardiovascular endpoint. It lists JUPITER as showing no mortality benefit using a figure footnoted as "reported among white subjects", while the trial's abstract gives death from any cause a hazard ratio of 0.80 (95% CI 0.67 to 0.97) [04.17][04.24]. Statisticians answering a related 2020 paper by the same author group made the general objection: non-significant is not "negative", and two trials with near-identical estimates were scored "yes" and "no" only because one was larger [04.22][04.23].
- **Verdict.** That statin benefit disappeared after 2005 is not supported as stated. That few trials since then show a statistically significant fall in deaths is true, and is what trials sized for cardiovascular events would produce.

### 6. Conflicts of interest
- **Guideline panels.** For the 2013 US guideline the BMJ reported that eight of the 16 panellists had current or recent industry ties, and that the chair said he had severed his in 2008 [04.34]; a later review of the guideline's own disclosures counted 7 of 16 members and 1 of 3 chairs, and found it met 5 of 9 Institute of Medicine standards [04.35]. The 2018 US guideline applied a policy that a member "must be free of any relevant relationships with industry" and removed one member on that ground [04.36]. The 2026 guideline states that members "are required to recuse themselves from voting on sections to which their specific relationships ... may apply" [04.37].
- **Trialists.** Many CTT collaborators outside Oxford declare consulting fees from lipid-drug makers [04.1].
- **Sceptics.** Six authors of the main sceptic review declare books critical of the cholesterol hypothesis; the paper was funded by the Western Vascular Institute, described as a charity, where two of its authors work [04.18]. One prominent UK critic declares co-producing the documentary The Big Fat Fix [04.45] and has a trade book titled A Statin-Free Life (publisher listing seen in search results only; a lead) [04.46]. Another has written four books on the subject and, by a statement read in the High Court, "derived only modest income" from them [04.44]. The FOURIER re-analysts declare no competing interests and a university grant [04.10]. The authors of the trial-documents audit declare public and foundation grants; one also declares past pharmaceutical advisory-board work, an interest in drug litigation and media fees [04.27].
- **Reading (my judgement).** Industry money is larger and nearer the primary data; book and media income is smaller and nearer public messaging.

### 7. The public disputes
- **BMJ, 2013 to 2016.** The panel (report published 15 August 2014) was unanimous that neither article met the criteria for retraction; the "only unequivocal error" was the side-effect figure, already corrected; the first article's calculations were "largely sound"; and it "noted with concern" that the complainant's submissions were all marked "not for publication" [04.6]. It did not rule on whether statins help low-risk people. The BMJ states that the Committee on Publication Ethics later concluded the journal had acted appropriately [04.8].
- **Cochrane, 2011 to 2013.** The 2011 review reported lower all-cause mortality (RR 0.83, 95% CI 0.73 to 0.95) but advised "caution" in low-risk people; the 2013 update reported OR 0.86 (0.79 to 0.94) and dropped the caution [04.49][04.50]. The panel describes the update as "largely based" on the CTT's 2012 patient-level meta-analysis, which is the critics' complaint [04.6][04.9]. The pooled mortality estimate did not reverse.
- **"Statin deniers", 2019 to 2024.** On 25 June 2024 the High Court dismissed a newspaper's public-interest defence over articles calling two critics liars, found a minister's statement had been used in a "seriously misleading" way, and rejected the critics' allegation of malice; it expressly did not decide "who is 'right' in the statin debate" [04.43]. The case settled in December 2024 with an apology and damages [04.44].
- **Labels.** Journal titles on each side assert motive: "Statin Denial: An Internet-Driven Cult With Deadly Consequences" [04.54] and "How statistical deception created the appearance that statins are safe and effective" [04.19].

### 8. Does critical coverage make people stop?
- **UK.** After the coverage of October 2013 to March 2014, people already on statins were more likely to stop (odds ratio 1.11 for primary and 1.12 for secondary prevention); starting was unaffected; the rise was "no longer observed after six months". The authors estimated 218,971 extra people stopping and, "if we assume causality", 2,173 to 6,372 extra cardiovascular events over ten years [04.39].
- **Denmark.** Among 674,900 people starting statins, negative news stories were associated with early stopping (odds ratio 1.09, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.12), and early stopping with heart attack (hazard ratio 1.26) and cardiovascular death (1.18) [04.38]. The second link is observational: people who stop a drug differ from those who continue.
- **Australia and France.** After a critical Australian television programme in October 2013 there were 504,180 fewer statin dispensings by 30 June 2014, affecting an estimated 60,897 people [04.40]. After a critical book, 11.9% of French regular users stopped against 8.5% in each of the two prior years, most in low-risk users [04.41]; a second Danish study found the same gradient [04.42].
- **Reading.** The association is consistent across four countries and strongest among lower-risk users. No study measured deaths caused by coverage; the event estimates are projections [04.39].

### Conceded, refuted, open
- **Conceded by the mainstream:** pooled patient-level data are not released to outsiders [04.2]; adverse events were not analysed centrally until after 2013 [04.7]; statins cause a small excess of muscle symptoms [04.4]; US guideline writers adopted a stricter conflict rule in 2018 [04.36].
- **Conceded or corrected by critics:** the 18 to 20% side-effect figure [04.6]; that pooled trials show lower all-cause mortality on statins, by a critic among the co-authors of [04.12] (see [04.17]), though not by all critics [04.18].
- **Refuted or unsupported:** that the 2004 rule required release of trial data [04.20]; that statin benefit vanished after 2005 [04.17][04.24]; that the BMJ articles warranted retraction [04.6]. The libel case did not decide whether the two critics were dishonest: the court dismissed the newspaper's public-interest defence and expressly did not decide who is right about statins, and the claim then settled with an apology [04.43][04.44].
- **Open:** independent audit of the pooled data; the true rate of side effects outside trials; whether sponsor-run adjudication shifts cause-of-death results; lifetime gain in years.

## What this means for the person's situation
For an adult offered a cholesterol-lowering drug:

1. Set aside the loudest claims on both sides. On blinded evidence side effects do not occur in a fifth of users [04.4][04.6], and critics of statins have not been shown to be dishonest or to have caused deaths [04.43][04.44].
2. Ask for the absolute number for your own risk, and which outcome it counts. Mainstream sources quote events prevented [04.5]; the figure for deaths is smaller. Even the critics' pooled figure shows fewer deaths on statins on average, by under one percentage point over a few years of trial [04.12]; thread 01 has the split by prior disease.
3. A trial reporting "no significant effect on deaths" is usually too small to show one. Counting such trials is the weakest part of the sceptic case [04.22].
4. The pooled statin data have not been checked by anyone outside the trialists' collaboration [04.2]. That warrants modest confidence in fine detail, such as exact side-effect rates or small subgroups. It is not evidence that the main result is wrong.

These points do not depend on the country assumption. The guideline conflict findings are for US panels only; UK and European panels were not checked.

## Load-bearing claims
- C04.1: The CTT's data-sharing statement in its Lancet paper of 14 February 2026 says individual participant data "would not be released to others" and that requests should be made directly to each trial's data custodians. [04.1][04.2]
- C04.2: The BMJ's independent panel (report of 15 August 2014) unanimously found that neither 2013 article met the criteria for retraction, and that the only unequivocal error was the 18 to 20% side-effect figure, corrected on 15 May 2014. [04.6]
- C04.3: A 2022 meta-analysis of 21 statin trials found, across the 19 that reported all-cause mortality, an absolute reduction of 0.8% (95% CI 0.4% to 1.2%) and a relative reduction of 9% (95% CI 5% to 14%); its authors include the author of a 2018 reappraisal of the lipid hypothesis. [04.12][04.17]
- C04.4: In 19 placebo-controlled trials (123,940 participants), 27.1% allocated a statin and 26.6% allocated placebo reported muscle pain or weakness, an excess of 11 (6 to 16) per 1,000 person-years in year 1. [04.4]
- C04.5: The journal editors' rule announced in September 2004 required prospective trial registration for trials starting enrolment after 1 July 2005; it did not require release of trial data. [04.20]
- C04.6: After UK media coverage in October 2013 to March 2014, the odds ratio for stopping a statin was 1.11 (primary prevention) and 1.12 (secondary prevention), an effect no longer observed after six months. [04.39]

## Confidence Notes
- **Strong.** The BMJ panel report, the CTT policy and oversight pages, the 2004 registration statement, the court documents, the FOURIER investigators' reply and the trial-documents audit were read in full, in the original or an archived copy.
- **Moderate.** Many findings rest on abstracts; where I read part of a paper, the source line says which. The Collins 2016 text is the accepted manuscript and may differ from the printed wording.
- **Thin.** Published letters replying to Diamond and Ravnskov 2015 and to Ravnskov 2018 were seen only in search results. The Danish media-study authors' interests and the 2026 guideline's disclosure table were not read.
- **My own judgements.** The remarks on the 29-trial table (its composition, the count of 10, the JUPITER discrepancy), on the overlap between the oversight panel and the 2016 review, and on "about one fifth" against 12% come from comparing two sources, not from a published critique.
- **Tier convention.** Literature-based reviews and meta-analyses are labelled "secondary" and patient-level meta-analyses by the data holders "primary"; figures quoted from the former are those reviews' own results.
- **Distortions guarded against.** I adopted neither camp's labels. Demasi's review [04.47] states the data-secrecy case strongly; I checked it against the data holder's own page.
- **Access.** Several journal and Oxford-hosted sites returned bot checks; I used Internet Archive snapshots, PubMed and Europe PMC and worked around none.

# Thread 03: Cohort and genetic evidence on cholesterol level and death from any cause

## Question
What do long-term cohort studies and genetic studies show about cholesterol level and death from any cause? This covers the U-shaped and inverse associations, especially in older people; how each study handled reverse causation (illness lowering cholesterol before death); Mendelian randomisation and other genetic evidence on LDL cholesterol and lifespan; people born with very high LDL (familial hypercholesterolaemia) or very low LDL (PCSK9 loss-of-function variants, hypobetalipoproteinaemia); and what these designs can and cannot say about lowering cholesterol with treatment.

## Key Findings

### 1. The curve is real: the lowest death rate is not at the lowest cholesterol

Sceptics are right about the shape. Cohorts in at least six countries find that people with low measured cholesterol die at higher rates than people in the middle of the range, and that in older people the association with total mortality is flat or inverse.

- Copenhagen (108,243 adults aged 20 to 100, median 9.4 years, 11,376 deaths): U-shaped for LDL. Against 3.4 to 3.9 mmol/L (132 to 154 mg/dL), the adjusted hazard ratio was 1.25 (95% CI 1.15 to 1.36) below 1.8 mmol/L (70 mg/dL) and 1.15 (1.05 to 1.27) above 4.8 mmol/L (189 mg/dL). Risk was lowest at 3.6 mmol/L (140 mg/dL) overall and in untreated people, and at 2.3 mmol/L (89 mg/dL) in people on lipid-lowering treatment. Cardiovascular death showed no association overall, but any increase in LDL went with more heart attacks [03.1].
- Korea (12.8 million adults, mean 10.5 years, 694,423 deaths): a U-curve for total cholesterol in every age and sex group, with the lowest mortality at 210 to 249 mg/dL in most. Below 200 mg/dL, each 1 mmol/L (39 mg/dL) higher went with 23% lower mortality; above it, 7% higher [03.3].
- United States: in NHANES 1999 to 2014 (19,034 adults, 1,619 deaths), LDL below 70 mg/dL had a fully adjusted hazard ratio of 1.37 (1.13 to 1.67) against 100 to 129 mg/dL [03.5]. In a Pittsburgh health system (177,860 adults aged 50 to 89 without diabetes or cardiovascular disease, not on statins), hazard ratios against 80 to 99 mg/dL were 1.23 (1.17 to 1.30) at 30 to 79 mg/dL, 0.87 to 0.91 across 100 to 189 mg/dL and 1.19 (1.06 to 1.34) at 190 mg/dL or more [03.6].
- Older people: in Leiden (724 people, median age 89, 642 deaths in 10 years), each 1 mmol/L higher total cholesterol went with 15% lower mortality (risk ratio 0.85, 0.79 to 0.91), through fewer deaths from cancer and infection [03.15]. Honolulu men aged 71 to 93 showed the same direction [03.10].
- The pattern is old. A 1990 US National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute conference pooled 19 cohorts: a U-shape in men and a flat relation in women, made up of more coronary deaths at high cholesterol and more deaths from some cancers, respiratory and digestive disease and trauma at low cholesterol [03.13]. In 350,977 men screened for MRFIT, cholesterol below 160 mg/dL went with twice the risk of intracranial haemorrhage and more deaths from liver cancer, cirrhosis and suicide [03.14].

The same literature points the other way for heart disease, which sceptics tend to leave out. Across 61 pooled studies (about 900,000 adults, 55,000 vascular deaths), 1 mmol/L lower total cholesterol went with lower ischaemic heart disease mortality at every age: hazard ratio 0.44 at 40 to 49, 0.66 at 50 to 69 and 0.83 at 70 to 89 [03.16]. In Copenhagen participants aged 70 to 100 without cardiovascular disease and not on statins, higher LDL still predicted heart attack; that study counted events, not deaths [03.17].

### 2. How each cohort handled reverse causation, and whether the association survived

| Study | Participants | Measure | People on lipid drugs | Step against reverse causation | Result after that step |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Copenhagen [03.1] | 108,243 aged 20–100 | LDL | Adjusted for; also analysed separately | Follow-up started at year 5; baseline cardiovascular disease, cancer and COPD excluded | U-shape remained, a little weaker |
| Korea, national insurance [03.3] | 12.8 million aged 18–99 | Total cholesterol only | Not accounted for | Adjusted for known heart disease, stroke, cancer; no exclusion of early deaths reported | U-curve in all groups |

| Danish primary care [03.19] | 118,160 aged 50 and over | Total and LDL | Prior-year statin users excluded; later use adjusted | Deaths in first year excluded; no adjustment for smoking, weight or illness | Inverse association |
| Honolulu, elderly [03.10] | 3,572 men aged 71–93 | Total cholesterol | Not in abstract | Measurements 20 years apart compared | Raised risk only in men low at both: 1.64 (1.13–2.36) |
| Leiden 85-plus [03.15] | 724 aged 85 and over | Total cholesterol | Not in abstract | Subgroup with stable cholesterol over 5 years | Similar estimate |

HR is hazard ratio. Three things follow.

First, the low-cholesterol group is not a random sample. In Copenhagen, 47% of people in the lowest LDL band were on lipid-lowering treatment, 28% had cardiovascular disease and 19% had diabetes, against 12%, 9% and 4% in the whole cohort; the association was strongest when adjusted only for age and sex and fell substantially once existing illness was adjusted for [03.1]. In Pittsburgh, the lowest band held 9.1% of patients but 30.4% of the deaths in the first year [03.6].

Second, approaching death lowers cholesterol. In 99,758 UK primary care patients aged 80 to 105 (29,200 deaths), total cholesterol fell faster in the last two years of life; late in follow-up the odds of a value under 3.0 mmol/L were 3.33 times higher (2.84 to 3.91) in untreated people who died than in survivors [03.8]. In Framingham, cholesterol that fell over 14 years predicted higher mortality over the next 18 [03.11]. In Honolulu men aged 45 to 68, a fall from middle to low cholesterol over six years went with 30% higher all-cause mortality (1.30, 1.06 to 1.59), while stable low cholesterol carried no significant excess [03.9]. In Whitehall, low cholesterol went with existing disease, recent weight loss, lower employment grade and widowhood, which explained most of its link with non-cardiovascular death [03.12]. In ASPREE the inverse link with non-cardiovascular death was concentrated in frail participants (hazard ratio per 1 mmol/L higher: 0.20 in the frail, 0.87 in the non-frail) [03.7].

Third, reverse causation has not been shown to explain everything, and saying so without checking is not supported either. The Copenhagen U-shape survived a five-year lag and the exclusion of people with major disease; the authors say they cannot tell whether what remains is residual confounding [03.1]. The NHLBI conference reported that excluding early deaths left its results unchanged [03.13]. In MRFIT the inverse association with most cancers weakened over follow-up without vanishing [03.14]. The two Honolulu analyses contradict each other: the 1995 paper found excess risk only in men whose cholesterol fell [03.9], the 2001 paper only in men low at both examinations, and its authors wrote "We have been unable to explain our results" [03.10]. One-year and three-year exclusions, the commonest step, may be too short: ASPREE needed five years to remove the association (my comparison across studies) [03.7].

No cohort could adjust for everything that lowers cholesterol and shortens life. Copenhagen had no data on weight loss and knew treatment status only at baseline [03.1]; the Korean national study had no data on lipid drugs, cholesterol fractions or cause of death [03.3].

### 3. The 2016 review of LDL and mortality in the elderly

Ravnskov and 16 co-authors searched PubMed and reported 19 studies (30 cohorts, 68,094 people aged 60 or over). LDL was inversely associated with all-cause mortality in 16 cohorts (14 significantly) and not associated in the rest. They concluded that this gives reason to doubt the cholesterol hypothesis and to re-evaluate guidelines recommending drug lowering of LDL in older people [03.18].

- The review counted cohorts by direction of result and did not pool estimates [03.18][03.20].
- One study supplied about two-thirds of the participants (44,666 of 68,094, by my addition of the review's table) [03.18]. It used cholesterol tests requested by Danish general practitioners, excluded only first-year deaths, and adjusted for age, sex, statin use and education but not smoking, weight or illness. Its authors named disease-lowered cholesterol, selective survival and residual confounding as possible explanations, and found that a statin prescription after the test went with better survival [03.19].
- On reverse causation the review relied on four or five studies that excluded terminal illness or first-year deaths; ten made no exclusions [03.18].
- An Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine appraisal found no protocol, one database, uneven inclusion criteria, no quality assessment, extraction errors, control for HDL in only three of 19 studies and no handling of statins started during follow-up; it scored the review at two of 11 AMSTAR criteria [03.20]. One of its points is inexact: it says the largest study listed no adjustment factors, but that study reported adjusting for statin use and education [03.19].

The review shows that an inverse association is common in published cohorts of older people. It does not show why.

### 4. Genetic evidence: fewer cardiovascular deaths with lifelong lower LDL; a smaller and less consistent effect on total lifespan

The genetic case is usually made for heart disease. The European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement on LDL causality concerns atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease; the terms all-cause, lifespan and longevity do not appear in its text [03.35]. The founding PCSK9 study counted coronary events, which were 88% lower in Black carriers of nonsense variants and 47% lower in White carriers of another LDL-lowering variant [03.34]. Studies of deaths or lifespan:

- Parental lifespan (1,012,240 lifespans): 1 standard deviation higher genetically predicted LDL went with 1.2 years shorter life (95% CI 0.87 to 1.55) and lower odds of reaching the 90th percentile age (odds ratio 0.72, 0.64 to 0.81). The PCSK9 region alone gave about one year; estimates for the statin target and the ezetimibe target were underpowered [03.22]. In the same data the LDL receptor gene was one of 12 regions associated with lifespan, at about 0.36 years per allele against 1.06 for APOE [03.30].
- Apolipoprotein B: 1 standard deviation higher went with 0.89 years of life lost in fathers and 0.48 in mothers, about 2 years when LDL and triglycerides were modelled jointly. In that joint model LDL cholesterol went with longer parental life once apoB was held constant, so particle number carried the effect [03.23].
- UK Biobank participants' own deaths: one analysis (347,797 people, 23,848 deaths) found hazard ratios per standard deviation of apoB of 1.11 (1.06 to 1.16) for all-cause and 1.36 (1.24 to 1.50) for cardiovascular mortality, results for LDL that its authors describe as similar, and no upturn at low levels [03.26]. Two other analyses of the same resource found no significant effect of LDL on all-cause mortality; one put apoB at 1.07 (1.02 to 1.12) and found higher LDL going with more cardiovascular and fewer neurodegenerative deaths [03.27][03.28]. These conflict on LDL and agree that any all-cause effect is modest.
- PCSK9 variants (109,566 Copenhagen participants, 16,373 deaths, validated in UK Biobank, given as 431,043 participants in the abstract and 429,749 in the body of the paper): per 0.5 mmol/L lower LDL, the risk ratio with the two combined was 0.79 (0.65 to 0.95) for cardiovascular death and 1.01 (0.94 to 1.08) for death from any cause; in Copenhagen alone, 0.79 (0.63 to 0.99) and 1.02 (0.94 to 1.12) [03.25].
- Drug-target analysis of parental attained age: variants mimicking PCSK9 inhibitors went with longer life in men (2.39 years per standard deviation of LDL, 0.42 to 4.36); variants mimicking statins and ezetimibe showed no association, with 80% power to detect 0.68 years [03.24].
- Oldest old: an LDL genetic score was lower in long-lived Dutch families and went with 13% higher mortality after age 90 in the top group (P for trend 0.043) [03.21].

Does genetics reproduce the U-shape? Three non-linear analyses in UK Biobank found no excess mortality at genetically low LDL [03.26][03.27][03.28]. Methodologists have shown that current non-linear methods give biased results in negative-control tests, including implausible results for lipids, and advise caution [03.29]. The linear results are firmer: none shows lifelong lower LDL going with higher all-cause mortality [03.22][03.25][03.26][03.27].

Known weaknesses:
- Other effects of the variants. APOE has the largest single effect on lifespan, acts mainly late in life and is the main Alzheimer's locus [03.30][03.31]; excluding it sharply reduced lipid associations with Alzheimer's disease in one study [03.23].
- Survival and selection. Genetic cohorts recruit people who lived to middle age, which can bias estimates; UK Biobank had a 5% response rate [03.32][03.27]. Parental-lifespan designs dilute the estimate [03.23].
- Ancestry. Almost all participants were European; the authors of the largest non-linear study say a threshold in East Asian populations cannot be excluded [03.26].
- Lifelong exposure. The estimates describe a difference from conception, not a drug started in adult life [03.27].

### 5. People born with very high LDL (familial hypercholesterolaemia)

Before treatment. A Dutch pedigree carrying one LDL-receptor mutation was traced from 1830 to 1989, when statins became available: 250 people with a 50% chance of carrying it, 70 deaths. The all-cause standardised mortality ratio (SMR) was 1.32 (1.03 to 1.67) overall and 1.59 (1.07 to 2.26) in the 118 definite carriers. By period it was 0.58 (0.12 to 1.71) in 1830 to 1869, 0.87 in 1870 to 1904, 1.49 in 1905 to 1934, 1.78 in 1935 to 1964 and 1.33 in 1965 to 1989. Mortality differed threefold between branches, and about 40% of untreated carriers reached a normal lifespan [03.38]. The figures before 1905 rest on ten deaths and cannot separate protection from no effect [03.38].

Before and after statins. In the UK Simon Broome register (3,382 patients, 370 deaths), the all-cause SMR was 1.37 (1.03 to 1.78) in 1980 to 1991 and 0.94 (0.84 to 1.05) from 1992, when statins were in wide use; the coronary SMR fell from 3.39 to 2.13. From 1992, patients without known coronary disease at registration had an all-cause SMR of 0.67 (0.57 to 0.79), mainly through a cancer SMR of 0.63 that the authors attribute to lifestyle advice, less smoking and surveillance; those with coronary disease had 1.43 (1.22 to 1.66) [03.36].

Treatment era. In 4,688 genotyped Norwegian patients (1992 to 2010, 113 deaths), the all-cause SMR was 0.87 (0.72 to 1.05), while the cardiovascular SMR was 1.56 (1.19 to 2.05) overall, 2.29 under age 70 and 8.03 at ages 20 to 39 [03.37].

Sceptic authors cite these registers to argue that people with the condition live as long as, or longer than, others [03.39]. The figures they cite come from cohorts that were treated, drawn from lipid clinics and counselled on lifestyle. The untreated-era figures show excess deaths, and cardiovascular deaths in young adults remain raised even with treatment [03.36][03.37][03.38]. No randomised outcome trial has been run in this condition, for ethical reasons [03.36].

### 6. People born with very low LDL

PCSK9 loss-of-function variants give lifelong lower LDL, fewer coronary events [03.34] and fewer cardiovascular deaths, but no detectable difference in death from any cause [03.25]. For hypobetalipoproteinaemia the evidence is thin: a 1976 study of eight kindreds reported life expectancy 9 and 12 years longer than US population figures in men and women, in a small, selected sample [03.40]. A preprint found PCSK9 or APOB loss-of-function variants in 104 of 13,131 healthy adults aged 70 or over, which shows that carriers reach old age but does not measure lifespan [03.41]. An exome-wide study of protein-truncating variants and lifespan in 352,338 UK Biobank participants identified four genes, all cancer-related; no lipid gene was among them [03.33].

### 7. What these designs can and cannot say about treatment

- A cohort curve shows who dies sooner among people who happen to have a given level. It cannot show what happens when that level is lowered by a drug. The Copenhagen authors state that their data should not be used as an argument against lipid-lowering treatment [03.1]; the Korean authors state that their study could not tell whether low cholesterol produced by a statin raises mortality [03.3]. In Copenhagen the lowest-risk level in treated people was 89 mg/dL, not 140 [03.1].
- Genetic studies remove reverse causation and most confounding, and they count against the idea that low LDL in itself shortens life. They estimate a lifelong difference and say nothing about a drug's other effects; the authors of the main lifespan study say that randomised trials are needed before practice changes [03.22].
- Registers show what very high LDL does and how outcomes changed after statins arrived, but before-and-after comparisons are not randomised [03.36].

## What this means for the person's situation

There is no individual here; this is written for any adult offered a cholesterol-lowering drug.

1. The claim that people with higher cholesterol live as long or longer is accurate as an observation; the usual inference from it is not supported. The low-cholesterol groups include many people who are ill, frail, losing weight or already treated because they are at high risk, and cholesterol falls in the years before death. When studies remove early deaths for long enough, or use genes instead of blood tests, the excess at low levels shrinks or disappears, though not in every study [03.1][03.7][03.8][03.26].
2. The curves do not tell you what a drug will do to your length of life. That question belongs to the randomised trials (threads 01 and 02).
3. Genetic evidence gives reasonable assurance on one narrow point: carrying lower LDL for life is not associated with dying sooner, and it goes with fewer cardiovascular deaths. On total lifespan the estimates are modest, about one year per standard deviation of lifelong LDL (roughly 1 mmol/L or 38 mg/dL from my background knowledge; the abstract gives no unit), and some studies find no effect on death from any cause [03.22][03.25][03.27]. A drug started later in life would be expected to deliver less than a lifelong difference; that is my inference, not a measured result.
4. A low or falling cholesterol in someone not taking a lipid drug is not good news by default; several cohort authors treat it as a possible marker of undiagnosed illness [03.3][03.7].
5. For adults over about 70, cohort evidence is where reverse causation is strongest, so it is least able to settle the question either way. Higher LDL still predicts heart attack at that age [03.17].
6. Familial hypercholesterolaemia is a separate case: untreated carriers in the twentieth century had excess deaths, and treated register patients have all-cause mortality close to the general population but more cardiovascular deaths at younger ages [03.36][03.37][03.38].

Dependence on assumptions: the brief assumes a US reader. Two of the cohorts are American, and the genetic studies are almost entirely of European ancestry. Where coronary disease is a small share of deaths (about 5% in Korea [03.3]), the low-cholesterol arm dominates total mortality; where it is larger, the high arm matters more. For a reader of East Asian ancestry the genetic findings apply with less confidence [03.26].

## Confidence Notes

Strong:
- U-shaped or inverse associations between measured cholesterol and all-cause mortality exist. They are replicated across countries, decades and measures [03.1][03.3][03.6][03.7].
- Illness and approaching death lower cholesterol. Independent designs agree: trajectories before death, change-over-time analyses and attenuation after long exclusions [03.7][03.8][03.9][03.11][03.12].
- Untreated familial hypercholesterolaemia carried excess all-cause mortality in the twentieth century; treated register cohorts do not, while their cardiovascular mortality stays raised [03.36][03.37][03.38].

Moderate:
- That reverse causation and confounding explain most of the low-cholesterol excess. Copenhagen's five-year analysis and the 20-year Honolulu result do not fit neatly. I would not state that reverse causation explains all of it.
- That lifelong lower LDL lengthens life by about a year per standard deviation. The direction is consistent for apoB; for LDL the UK Biobank analyses disagree, and the PCSK9 variant study found no all-cause effect. Several of these studies reuse the same UK Biobank and parental-lifespan data, so they are not independent replications.

Thin:
- Lifespan with lifelong very low LDL from APOB variants: one 1976 kindred study and one preprint.
- Non-linear genetic analysis: the method is disputed [03.29].
- Genetic estimates for the statin and ezetimibe targets on lifespan are null or underpowered [03.22][03.24]; genetics supports the PCSK9 pathway more directly than any one drug.

How much I read. Sources marked full were read in their main text (methods, results, discussion, limitations and declarations), not every table or supplement. Sources marked abstract with a note were retrieved in full but read only in the passages named. Paywalled papers ([03.8] to [03.17], [03.21], [03.22], [03.25], [03.28], [03.34]) were read as PubMed abstracts only, so I could not check how each handled lipid drugs or early deaths beyond what the abstract says. Two load-bearing claims (C03.4, C03.5) rest on abstracts.

Possible distortions. Industry ties run through the mainstream genetic and register literature, and the senior Copenhagen author's competing interests statement was later corrected [03.2]. Book authorship runs through the sceptic reviews [03.18][03.39]. My own default is the guideline framing; to counter it I reported the cohorts that survived reverse-causation checks and the genetic studies that found no all-cause effect. Two figures are my own: the two-thirds share of one study in the 2016 review (arithmetic from its table) and the size of a standard deviation of LDL (background knowledge).

## Gaps

Looked for and not found:
- A population-scale estimate of all-cause mortality or lifespan in carriers of APOB truncating variants or other causes of hypobetalipoproteinaemia. Not studied, or barely:

- Adequately powered genetic estimates in women and in the very old [03.24][03.26].
- Within-family genetic designs for LDL and lifespan; I found none.
- Large cohorts with repeated cholesterol measures, weight change and cause of death together; only small or old studies separate falling from stable low cholesterol [03.9][03.10][03.15].
